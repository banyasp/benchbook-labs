## 1. Classical AI & Law: case-based reasoning and formal models of precedent

This is the older (1980s–2010s), mostly symbolic-AI and jurisprudence-adjacent literature that already built, by hand, much of what "Extension Training" wants an LLM to learn: which fact-features (their term: *dimensions* or *factors*) a precedent's verdict actually turns on, and what it formally means to *distinguish* a case rather than follow it. This is the closest thing that exists to a spec for the "mapping" object Peter wants the judge to reward.

**Ashley, K. D. — *Modeling Legal Argument: Reasoning with Cases and Hypotheticals*. MIT Press / Bradford Books, 1990.** https://mitpress.mit.edu/books/modeling-legal-arguments (book; DTIC preprint copy: https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA250559.pdf)
The book-length presentation of **HYPO**, the founding system of computational case-based legal reasoning, built on trade-secret-misappropriation case law. HYPO represents each case as a point in a space of **dimensions** — factual axes (e.g., "did the plaintiff disclose the information to outsiders," "was there a confidentiality agreement," "how similar are the products") along which a case's facts can be more or less favorable to one side, each with a defined range from weakest to strongest instantiation. Given a new problem, HYPO retrieves the most on-point precedents by dimensional similarity, then constructs a **three-ply argument**: (1) cite the most favorable precedent for a side, (2) the opponent responds with a counter-example — a precedent decided the other way, or a distinction based on a dimension present in the cited case but absent (or weaker) in the current facts — and (3) the first side rebuts, e.g., by generating a hypothetical variant that shows the distinction doesn't matter. The companion journal paper below gives the same architecture more concisely.

Relevance: **(T-skill)** — HYPO's "dimensions" are precisely the pre-theoretic ancestor of "which relations carry the precedent's verdict"; its 3-ply structure (cite / distinguish-or-counter-example / rebut) is a template for what a "mapping" step should output move-by-move.

**Ashley, K. D. — "Reasoning with Cases and Hypotheticals in HYPO." *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies* 34(6), 1991, pp. 753–796.** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/002073739190011U
Journal presentation of HYPO (see above): dimensions serve as "a generalization scheme for accessing and evaluating cases," letting HYPO identify relevant features and make "contextually sensitive assessments of relevance and salience without relying on either a strong domain theory or a priori weighting schemes" — i.e., no hand-coded importance weights, salience falls out of which dimensions the current case shares with retrieved precedents. The four types of counter-example/distinction it can generate are laid out explicitly, along with rebuttal-by-hypothetical.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — "salience without a priori weighting" is exactly the property Peter wants the judge to reward emergent, not hand-specified.

**Rissland, E. L., Ashley, K. D. — "Hypotheticals as Heuristic Device." Proceedings, AAAI-86 / related NLP workshop, 1986.** https://aclanthology.org/H86-1016.pdf
Precursor to HYPO proper: presents five heuristics for generating legally meaningful hypothetical variants of a seed case — (1) push a "near-miss" dimension until it becomes applicable, (2) strengthen/weaken a case along an applicable dimension, (3) explore a related dimension, (4) push to an extreme case, (5) manipulate a "near win" into a loss or vice versa. The point is that varying one dimension at a time and watching whether the predicted outcome flips is how a reasoner discovers which dimensions are outcome-relevant — a manual, human-driven form of what a sensitivity/ablation analysis would do computationally.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — a ready-made recipe for generating *counterfactual case variants* to test whether a candidate mapping is fact-salience-correct (does flipping the mapped factor flip the model's predicted verdict?), directly usable as a training/eval augmentation technique.

**Aleven, V. — "Teaching Case-Based Argumentation Through a Model and Examples." Ph.D. dissertation, University of Pittsburgh, 1997.** https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/926270 (Semantic Scholar record: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8995bf5e9d0a686e635d0099976c18cb47f05172)
Introduces **CATO**, which extends HYPO's dimensions into **factors** — binary (present/absent) legally-significant fact patterns, each of which favors one side by legal-domain stipulation — organized into a **factor hierarchy**: an abstraction tree connecting concrete factors up through intermediate legal concepts (e.g., "aggressive-product-development") to the top-level issue (misappropriation of trade secrets). CATO is a tutoring system explicitly built to *teach law students* the moves of case-based argument: **citing** a favorable precedent, **distinguishing** an opponent's precedent (arguing it doesn't control because a factor present there is absent here, or a factor favoring the other side is present here but wasn't there), and **downplaying** vs. **emphasizing** — rhetorical moves that argue a shared factor should carry more or less weight than the opponent claims, by pointing to where else in the factor hierarchy it does or doesn't matter. In controlled studies, CATO taught these argumentation skills as effectively as a human instructor.
[VERIFIED — fetched via the CBR-and-law survey below, which describes CATO's mechanism directly; the dissertation's full PDF itself was not independently fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — CATO's factor hierarchy plus its explicit distinguish/downplay/emphasize move taxonomy is arguably the single closest existing artifact to Peter's "mapping" object (which relations carry the verdict, which don't, labeled follow/distinguish/no-precedent) — a pre-LLM, hand-engineered version of the exact skill he wants trained.

**Brüninghaus, S., Ashley, K. D. — "Combining Case-Based and Model-Based Reasoning for Predicting the Outcome of Legal Cases." Proc. ICCBR 2003, Springer LNCS 2689, pp. 65–79.** https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/3-540-45006-8_8
Introduces **IBP** (Issue-Based Prediction): a "weak model" of the legal domain first identifies which *issues* a case raises and maps each factor to the issue(s) it's relevant to; case-based reasoning is then used per-issue to predict which side wins that issue (by finding the most on-point precedent and, when contradicted by counter-examples, explaining them away); the per-issue predictions are combined into an overall outcome prediction with an explanation trace. IBP beat both the weak domain-model alone and unstructured case-based reasoning alone, and beat standard ML baselines (decision trees, nearest-neighbor) of the era on the same factor-annotated case set.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-eval)** — issue-decomposition-then-per-issue-precedent-prediction is a natural intermediate representation for a "mapping" training target, and its accuracy-vs-baseline framing is a template for how to evaluate a learned mapping against hand-coded factor ground truth.

**Rissland, E. L., Ashley, K. D., Branting, L. K. — "Case-Based Reasoning and Law." *The Knowledge Engineering Review* 20(3), 2005 (Cambridge University Press), pp. 293–298.** https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/KER/14.%20CBR%20and%20law%20(Rissland,%20Ashley,%20&%20Branting).pdf
A short survey by the field's three central figures, tracing HYPO (1984–1990, the first full CBR cycle applied to law, dimensions, 3-ply arguments) through **CABARET** (1991, a hybrid case-based/rule-based system for statutory interpretation in tax law, using a "dynamic agenda" to interleave the two reasoning modes when neither alone suffices) to **BankXX** (1996, best-first heuristic search over a legal-knowledge network to assemble a balanced set of supporting and opposing material for an argument, i.e., automated brief-research) and CATO/IBP as described above. Useful as a single fetchable map of the sub-field and its terminology before going to primary sources.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — good citation for a literature-map paragraph; CABARET's case+rule hybrid is a precedent for combining a learned "mapping" skill with an explicit rule-application skill rather than training either alone.

**Horty, J. F. — "Rules and Reasons in the Theory of Precedent." *Legal Theory* 17(1), 2011, pp. 1–33.** https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3B7697D8BD95470F3A814ACA72C5A2F9/S1352325211000036a.pdf/rules_and_reasons_in_the_theory_of_precedent.pdf (author's preprint: http://www.horty.umiacs.io/articles/2008-rrp.pdf)
The key formal-jurisprudence paper distinguishing three models of how precedent constrains later courts. In the **rule model**, a precedent is constraining insofar as a later court's facts satisfy the antecedent of a rule the precedent court is taken to have laid down. In the **result model** (associated with Levenbook/Alexander), constraint tracks only whether the new fact situation is "at least as strong for the winning side" as the precedent's facts, ignoring which specific rule was cited. Horty's own **reason model** is a synthesis: a precedent case is formalized as a triple ⟨X, r, s⟩ — a fact situation, a defeasible rule Y→s where Y ⊆ X are the factors the court treated as jointly sufficient reasons for outcome s, and the outcome itself. The load-bearing move is that a court's decision for s *despite* opposing factors being present is read as an implicit ordering statement — "the reasons favoring s here outweighed the reasons favoring the other side" — which then generalizes as a constraint on later courts with a relevantly similar balance of reasons. Formally, **distinguishing** a precedent does *not* require citing a new factor absent from the precedent; it consists of a later court asserting a different rule r′ (supporting the opposite outcome) whose premises it judges to outweigh r's premises in the new case — consistent with, rather than contradicting, the earlier court's own reasoning about weight.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — gives the precise formal semantics for "follow" (facts trigger the precedent's rule, no defeating reason present) vs. "distinguish" (a different, outweighing rule is asserted) vs. "no precedent" (neither rule's factor-set is present) — the exact three-way verdict Peter's mapping should predict, already formalized. Note this nuances the target instruction slightly: the note "distinguishing = adding a factor favoring the other side that was absent" is closer to the earlier, simpler **Horty & Bench-Capon 2012** a-fortiori/factor account below than to Horty 2011's own reason-model; both should be cited, and the memo should flag that "AI & Law" formalisms do not agree on a single definition of distinguishing.

**Horty, J. F., Bench-Capon, T. J. M. — "A Factor-Based Definition of Precedential Constraint." *Artificial Intelligence and Law* 20(2), 2012, pp. 181–214.** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10506-012-9125-8 (author's preprint: http://www.horty.umiacs.io/articles/2012-ail-tbc.pdf)
A more purely factor-based (as opposed to rule-based) formalization, closer to CATO's ontology than Horty 2011's rule-triples. Factors are legally-significant facts that uniformly favor plaintiff or defendant (no magnitude, just polarity + presence/absence). A precedent case is a fact-situation/rule/outcome triple as before. A new case falls under **a fortiori constraint** — must be decided the same way as precedent — when the new situation's factor set is a superset of the precedent's winning-side factors and a subset (i.e., no fewer favorable-to-loser factors) of what the precedent had; intuitively, the new case is "at least as strong" for the side that won. **Distinguishing**, in this framework, is exactly the move the task brief describes: a court formulates an alternative rule r′ for the opposite outcome by pointing to factor(s) present in the new case (favoring the losing side of the precedent) that were absent from the precedent's fact situation, so the new case is not in fact a fortiori — the precedent doesn't reach it. A companion, improved version, **"An Improved Factor-Based Approach to Precedential Constraint"** (*AI & Law* 23(4), 2015), is listed for completeness but not separately fetched here.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-eval)** — this is the paper that directly cashes out the task brief's stated definition of "distinguishing"; a mapping-training objective could literally use "is factor set a superset/subset" as a checkable a-fortiori test for the follow/no-constraint boundary, giving a partially-automatable correctness check on a model's mapping.

**Prakken, H., Sartor, G. — "Modelling Reasoning with Precedents in a Formal Dialogue Game." *Artificial Intelligence and Law* 6(2–4), 1998, pp. 231–287.** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008278309945 (draft copy: https://www.academia.edu/1375363/Modelling_reasoning_with_precedents_in_a_formal_dialogue_game)
[UNVERIFIED — page fetched but returned only metadata/references, not the abstract text; the paper's content is summarized here from its established place in the literature (cited by Horty 2011 and the Rissland/Ashley/Branting survey above as the canonical dialogue-game formalization) rather than from a directly retrieved abstract]
Models precedential argument as a formal, turn-taking dialogue game between a proponent and opponent, each move being a legal speech-act (cite a case, distinguish it, argue a factor should be weighted differently, cite a counter-precedent) with explicit rules for when a move is legal and who wins if the dialogue terminates; this operationalizes "reason-based" precedent (values/purposes behind a rule, not just its literal factors, can be invoked) inside a game-theoretic proof procedure, rather than Horty's purely declarative factor/rule semantics. It predates and is a direct ancestor of Horty's reason model above.
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — a dialogue-game framing (propose mapping → opponent move: distinguish/counter-example → rebuttal) is a natural structure for a self-play or adversarial judge setup, closer to how Peter's "judge rewards mapping validity" might be operationalized than a single-shot classification.

**Note on "reason-based" vs. "result-based" (vs. "rule-based") models, synthesized across the above:** the *result model* asks only "is the new case at least as strong, factor-for-factor, for the side that won?" — a purely structural comparison, blind to *why* the factors mattered. The *rule model* asks "does the new case's fact situation satisfy the antecedent of the rule the precedent court stated (or is best read as having applied)?" — constraint via rule-instantiation. The *reason model* (Horty) asks "is the new decision consistent with the earlier court's implicit ranking of which reasons outweigh which?" — the richest and most defeasible-reasoning-native of the three, and the one that makes "distinguishing" a matter of asserting a new, locally-outweighing rule rather than merely finding a factual disanalogy. All three formalisms agree that "distinguishing" is not "the case is different" in some vague holistic sense — it is a *structurally specific* move: point to a legally-significant factor (a "reason") that is present in the new case, absent from the precedent, and favors the side the precedent's rule would otherwise defeat. This is the operational definition Peter's judge should be checking a model's stated mapping against.

## 2. LLM legal-reasoning benchmarks and results

**Guha, N., Nyarko, J., Ho, D. E., Ré, C., Chilton, A., et al. (36 authors) — "LegalBench: A Collaboratively Built Benchmark for Measuring Legal Reasoning in Large Language Models." NeurIPS 2023 (Datasets & Benchmarks track) / arXiv 2308.11462, Aug 2023.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462
The full-scale LegalBench paper (a short 2022 prototype, arXiv 2209.06120, preceded it and is the version indexed on arXiv under the plain title). 162 tasks, hand-built by legal professionals, organized into **six categories of legal reasoning**, confirmed from the project's own task-organization page: (1) **issue-spotting** — does a fact pattern raise a given legal question or implicate a given party (e.g. `abercrombie`, `corporate_lobbying`); (2) **rule-recall** — state the correct legal rule for a jurisdiction/issue (e.g. `hearsay`, `nys_judicial_ethics`); (3) **rule-application** — does the model's reasoning trace exhibit the correct legal inference in applying a rule (e.g. `legal_reasoning_causality`, `oral_argument_question_purpose`); (4) **rule-conclusion** — given facts and a specified rule, determine the legal outcome (e.g. `canada_tax_court_outcomes`, `personal_jurisdiction`); (5) **interpretation** — parse/understand a legal text, e.g. classify a contract clause (e.g. `contract_nli_confidentiality_of_agreement`, `definition_extraction`); (6) **rhetorical-understanding** — reason about legal argumentation itself, e.g. identify a textualist argument or an `overruling` sentence. The paper evaluates 20 LLMs and explicitly maps these categories onto legal scholars' own IRAC-derived taxonomies so lawyers and ML researchers share vocabulary.
[VERIFIED — fetched: arXiv abstract via API, and the six-category definitions + examples via the project's Stanford task-organization page]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-eval)** — rule-application and rhetorical-understanding are the closest existing benchmark categories to "mapping" (does the model's stated reasoning trace the correct legal inference, does it correctly identify an `overruling`/distinguishing rhetorical move); a mapping-training eval could be framed as new LegalBench-style tasks in these two categories.

**Zheng, L., Guha, N., Anderson, B. R., Henderson, P., Ho, D. E. — "When Does Pretraining Help? Assessing Self-Supervised Learning for Law and the CaseHOLD Dataset." ICAIL 2021 / arXiv 2104.08671, Apr 2021.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08671
Introduces **CaseHOLD** (Case Holdings On Legal Decisions): 53,000+ multiple-choice items, each giving an excerpt from a U.S. court opinion with the holding-statement citation replaced by a blank, and five candidate holding sentences (one correct, four distractor holdings drawn from other cases) to choose among. A BiLSTM baseline gets F1 ≈ 0.4 (near chance among five options is 0.2, so the task is legally meaningful but hard), and domain-pretrained Legal-BERT (trained on ~3.5M U.S. court decisions, more raw text than BERT's own pretraining corpus) beats general-domain BERT by 7.2 F1 points (a 12% relative gain) — the paper's broader point is that domain pretraining helps in proportion to how domain-specific the *task* is, not just the *text*.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-data), (T-eval)** — CaseHOLD's core move (mask the holding, force selection among plausible alternative holdings from other cases) is directly reusable as a pretraining/distillation task for "what did this precedent actually hold," a prerequisite skill for mapping; its construction pipeline (extracting a holding statement paired with its source excerpt from real opinions) is a template for building the "verdict" half of a mapping training example.

**Chalkidis, I., Jana, A., Hartung, D., Bommarito, M., Androutsopoulos, I., Katz, D. M., Aletras, N. — "LexGLUE: A Benchmark Dataset for Legal Language Understanding in English." ACL 2022 / arXiv 2110.00976, Oct 2021.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00976 ; HF: https://huggingface.co/datasets/coastalcph/lex_glue
A GLUE-style aggregation of seven pre-existing legal NLU tasks into one standardized suite: ECtHR Task A (predict which European Convention on Human Rights articles a case's facts violate), ECtHR Task B (predict which articles the court merely *considered*, a finer-grained allegation-level task), **SCOTUS** (multi-class classification of U.S. Supreme Court opinions into their substantive issue area — the task the brief specifically flags), EUR-LEX (multi-label EU legislation topic tagging), LEDGAR (SEC-contract-provision topic classification), UNFAIR-ToS (detect unfair terms-of-service clauses), and CaseHOLD (folded in from Zheng et al. above). Legal-domain-pretrained models (Legal-BERT, CaseLaw-BERT) consistently beat general-domain BERT/RoBERTa of the same size across the suite. A follow-up working paper, Chalkidis 2023 (arXiv 2304.12202), zero-shot-evaluates `gpt-3.5-turbo` on LexGLUE and finds only 47.6% average micro-F1 — well below fine-tuned small legal-domain models — showing that generic instruction-following alone does not confer LexGLUE-level legal classification skill.
[VERIFIED — fetched: arXiv abstract via API and the HuggingFace dataset card for the seven-task breakdown]
Relevance: **(T-eval), (T-data)** — SCOTUS-task-style issue-area classification is a cheap, automatable proxy signal for whether a model has correctly identified *what a case is about* before attempting a mapping over it; the seven-task suite doubles as off-the-shelf eval infrastructure already keyed to real US and EU opinions.

**COLIEE (Competition on Legal Information Extraction/Entailment) — recurring shared task, 2014–2026; cited here via participant papers from the 2025–2026 rounds.** e.g. Ngo et al., "NOWJ@COLIEE 2026," arXiv 2607.16603 (Jul 2026); Alshehri et al., "Cross-Architecture LLM Ensembles...for Legal Information Processing," arXiv 2607.11400 (Jul 2026, Team DU); Tang et al., "ReaKase-8B: Legal Case Retrieval via Knowledge and Reasoning Representations with LLMs," arXiv 2510.26178 (Oct 2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16603 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11400 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26178
COLIEE (built on Japanese and Canadian case law, run annually alongside JURISIN/ICAIL) structures legal-AI competition around five tasks that map cleanly onto a "mapping" pipeline: **Task 1**, legal case retrieval — given a query case, retrieve the precedents it should cite; **Task 2**, legal case entailment — given a query case and a retrieved candidate paragraph, decide whether that paragraph entails/supports the query's decision (i.e., does this precedent-fragment actually carry the verdict); **Task 3–4**, statute retrieval and statute entailment (same two-step structure applied to code sections instead of cases); and a **pilot task**, legal judgment prediction with rationale extraction — predict a case's outcome (e.g., tort liability) and extract the supporting rationale spans. 2026 participant numbers: Team DU reports 96.3% accuracy on statute entailment (best of 33 submissions, 11 teams) and, in post-competition analysis, that switching an entailment prompt from single- to multi-selection lifts case-entailment F1 from 0.343 to 0.555; ReaKase-8B (2025) shows that augmenting an LLM's case embeddings with extracted facts/issues/legal-relation-triples/reasoning chains, not just raw text, substantially improves precedent-retrieval accuracy on COLIEE 2022–2023 data.

Relevance: **(T-data), (T-eval), (T-skill)** — COLIEE's Task 2 (case entailment: does this precedent fragment support this outcome) is structurally almost identical to the "follow" half of a mapping verdict, and it is a live, annually-refreshed shared task with public train/test splits, making it a ready external validation set rather than something Peter would need to build from scratch.

**Henderson, P., Krass, M. S., Zheng, L., Guha, N., Manning, C. D., Jurafsky, D., Ho, D. E. — "Pile of Law: Learning Responsible Data Filtering from the Law and a 256GB Open-Source Legal Dataset." NeurIPS 2022 (Datasets & Benchmarks) / arXiv 2207.00220, Jul 2022.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.00220
Releases a 256GB (and, per the paper, growing) open-source English-language legal corpus spanning court opinions, contracts, administrative rules, and legislative records, explicitly framed as an exercise in deriving *content-filtering norms from law itself* (e.g., courts' own sealing/redaction practices) rather than ad hoc toxicity filters, and argues this makes the corpus a natural testbed for learning filtering rules from data rather than hand-writing them.

Relevance: **(T-data)** — the single largest open aggregation of raw U.S. legal text pre-built for LLM pretraining/fine-tuning; a natural base corpus to draw the raw-opinion side of a mapping-training set from, though it is unstructured full text, not pre-segmented into facts/holding/reasoning (see §5).

**Dominguez-Olmedo, R., Nanda, V., Abebe, R., Bechtold, S., Engel, C., Frankenreiter, J., Gummadi, K., Hardt, M., Livermore, M. — "Lawma: The Power of Specialization for Legal Annotation." ICLR 2025 / arXiv 2407.16615, Jul 2024 (v2 Apr 2025).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16615
Introduces **CaselawQA**, 260 legal-annotation tasks (nearly all new to ML) built from real empirical-legal-studies coding schemes, and benchmarks commercial LLMs against small, lightly fine-tuned open models. Finding, confirmed from the abstract: commercial frontier models — explicitly including **GPT-4.5 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet** — achieve "non-trivial yet highly variable accuracy, generally falling short of the performance required for legal work," while small models fine-tuned on a few hundred to a thousand labeled examples per task *outperform* the commercial frontier models. The authors' practical conclusion: for concrete legal-annotation tasks with some labeled data available, a small fine-tuned open model beats prompting a much larger commercial model.

Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-data)** — direct empirical support for Peter's "train the skill on preprocessed opinions" strategy over relying on frontier-model zero/few-shot legal judgment: a modest amount of task-specific labeled data (few hundred–1,000 examples) already beats frontier prompting on real legal-annotation tasks, which bounds how much preprocessed, mapping-labeled data a small-model Extension-Training experiment would plausibly need.

**Colombo, P., Pires, T. P., Boudiaf, M., Culver, D., Melo, R., Corro, C., Martins, A. F. **Dahl, M., Magesh, V., Suzgun, M., Ho, D. E. — "Large Legal Fictions: Profiling Legal Hallucinations in Large Language Models." Journal of Legal Analysis 16(1), 2024, pp. 64–93 / arXiv 2401.01301, Jan 2024.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.01301
Systematically probes LLMs with specific, verifiable factual questions about random federal court cases and finds legal hallucination "alarmingly prevalent" — **58% of the time with ChatGPT-4, up to 88% with Llama 2** — with hallucination rates varying by jurisdiction, court level, and case age (older/more obscure cases hallucinated more). Models also frequently fail to correct a user's *false* legal premise embedded in a question (sycophantic confirmation), and are not reliably able to self-report when they are hallucinating (confidence is not a valid detector).
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-eval)** — this is the headline evidence base for the memo's "known failure mode" section; a mapping-training approach that grounds every claim in a *retrieved, real* precedent (rather than the model's parametric memory of case law) directly targets this failure mode, and these hallucination rates are the pre-intervention baseline against which any mapping-trained model's grounding should be measured.

**Zhang, L., Grabmair, M., Gray, M., Ashley, K. — "Thinking Longer, Not Always Smarter: Evaluating LLM Capabilities in Hierarchical Legal Reasoning." CSLAW 2026 / arXiv 2510.08710, Oct 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08710
The most directly on-point 2025–2026 paper found for this strand — co-authored by Kevin Ashley himself (HYPO/CATO, §1) — explicitly targeting "identifying significant distinctions between cases." The authors build a **formal three-stage framework**: cases are modeled via CATO-style factors organized into a legal-knowledge hierarchy, with verifiable rules for (Task 1) identifying a factual distinction between two cases, (Task 2) analyzing that distinction's argumentative support by tracing it up through the factor hierarchy, and (Task 3) an integrated judgment of the distinction's overall significance. Evaluating modern reasoning LLMs, they find a **sharp capability cliff with depth**: near-ceiling accuracy on Task 1 (surface-level distinction-spotting), degrading to 64.8%–92.1% on Task 2 (hierarchical reasoning), and **collapsing to 11.5%–34.0% on Task 3** (integrated significance judgment) — and, strikingly, models spend *more* inference-time compute on wrong answers than right ones ("thinking longer" does not track "thinking smarter").

Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-eval)** — this is the closest existing empirical measurement of exactly the skill "Extension Training" wants to train (distinguishing, via factor hierarchies, with a graded/decomposed verdict), on current frontier reasoning models, and it is bad news for a zero-shot approach: models can spot a surface distinction but reliably fail to correctly weigh whether that distinction is significant enough to change the outcome — precisely the "mapping validity" judgment Peter's judge would need to reward. Its three-task decomposition (spot distinction → trace through hierarchy → integrate into significance judgment) is a ready-made curriculum structure and eval harness.

## 3. Law-inspired alignment work: the closest priors

**Chen, Q. Z., Zhang, A. X. — "Case Law Grounding: Using Precedents to Align Decision-Making for Humans and AI." ACM Collective Intelligence 2025 / arXiv 2310.07019, Oct 2023 (v4 Jun 2025; earlier arXiv versions circulated under the title "Case Law Grounding: Aligning Judgments of Humans and AI on Socially-Constructed Concepts," confirmed via the ar5iv mirror of an earlier revision).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07019
The single closest existing prior to "Extension Training." Chen and Zhang argue that grounding subjective, socially-constructed decisions (e.g., is this online comment harassment?) in a written "constitution" of high-level rules is inherently underdetermined — different people interpret the same rule differently — and propose **case law grounding (CLG)**: instead of (or in addition to) a rule document, ground each new decision in a retrieved set of **past decided cases with their verdicts**, mirroring precedent use in common-law adjudication. They implement CLG in two parallel settings, a human-facilitated process and an LLM-prompting pipeline (retrieve N similar past cases + verdicts, then condition the judgment on them), and test both across five real online-moderation-style communities/task domains. Result: CLG-grounded decisions were significantly more accurately aligned to ground truth in 4 of 5 groups, by **16.0–23.3 percentage points for the human process and 20.8–32.9 points for the LLM setup** versus constitution/rule-only grounding; larger retrieval windows and treating retrieved precedents as *binding* (rather than merely advisory) both further improved accuracy.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API and WebFetch of the abstract page]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — this is direct, quantified evidence for the core wager of Extension Training (conditioning on paradigm cases with verdicts improves alignment accuracy over rules/constitutions alone), on a real task, at a magnitude (~20-point accuracy gains) large enough to matter; the two open gaps relative to Peter's proposal are that CLG retrieves-and-conditions rather than training a mapping-writing skill, and it does not have anything like a distinguish/follow/no-precedent verdict taxonomy or a judge that rewards mapping *validity* specifically (as opposed to final-decision accuracy).

**Feng, K. J. K., Chen, Q. Z., Cheong, I., Xia, K., Zhang, A. X. — "Case Repositories: Towards Case-Based Reasoning for AI Alignment." NeurIPS 2023 MP2 (Moral Philosophy & Machine Ethics) Workshop / arXiv 2311.10934, Nov 2023.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10934
From the same lab as Case Law Grounding, this is the explicit "build a case repository for AI alignment" proposal named in the task brief. It positions itself as **complementary to Constitutional AI**: instead of (or alongside) a document of principles, build a structured repository of cases (concrete questions one might ask an AI system) via a four-step process — (1) gather "seed" cases from a domain, (2) run workshops with domain experts to elicit **key dimensions** for cases in that domain (explicitly echoing HYPO/CATO's dimension/factor vocabulary from §1, though the paper is not in dialogue with that literature directly), (3) use LLMs to generate synthetic case *variations* not seen in the wild (echoing Rissland & Ashley's hypothetical-generation heuristics, §1), and (4) engage the public to judge and refine cases. The paper argues such a repository could serve AI alignment two ways: directly, as precedents that ground acceptable model behavior (i.e., feeding a CLG-style pipeline), and indirectly, as a medium through which individuals/communities do their own moral reasoning about what AI should do.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-data)** — the second of the two priors named in the brief; its "elicit key dimensions via expert workshop, then LLM-generate case variations, then get public judgments" pipeline is a directly reusable recipe for building Peter's moral-exemplar casebook (paradigm cases + reasons) once real judicial opinions have been used to train the underlying mapping skill, and it is a workshop paper (unreviewed, no empirical results) rather than an implemented/evaluated system — a genuine gap Extension Training would be filling.

**Kolt, N., Caputo, N., Boeglin, J., O'Keefe, C., Bommasani, R., Casper, S., Cuéllar, M.-F., Feldman, N., Gabriel, I., Hadfield, G. K., Hammond, L., Henderson, P., Kasirzadeh, A., Lazar, S., Reuel, A., Wei, K. L., Zittrain, J. — "Legal Alignment for Safe and Ethical AI." TMLR / arXiv 2601.04175, Jan 2026 (v2 Jun 2026).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04175
The best 2025–2026 candidate for "third closest prior" — a large, cross-institutional survey (co-authored by, among others, Iason Gabriel, Gillian Hadfield, and Seth Lazar) that names and systematizes an emerging field, "**legal alignment**," organized around three pathways: (1) designing AI to comply with the *content* of specific legal rules from legitimate institutions; (2) adapting *methods of legal interpretation* (statutory construction, precedent-following, purposive reasoning) to guide how models reason generally, not just about law; and (3) using legal *concepts* (due process, burden of proof, precedent, standards of review) as a structural blueprint for AI-system reliability and trust problems that have no natural non-legal analogue. Pathway (2) explicitly includes case-based/precedential reasoning as a template for how a model should generalize from examples rather than only from stated rules — the closest point of contact with Extension Training — though the paper is a field-defining survey (taxonomy + open questions), not a specific trained system.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — gives Extension Training a citable, high-authorship-credibility umbrella term ("legal alignment," pathway 2 specifically) and a survey of adjacent proposals to check the memo isn't duplicating unbuilt ideas; strongest as a framing/legitimacy citation rather than a technical source.

**Caputo, N. — "Alignment as Jurisprudence." *Yale Journal of Law and Technology* 27, 390 (Sept. 2025) / arXiv 2605.08416, May 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08416
A solo essay by one of the Legal Alignment for Safe and Ethical AI co-authors, putting jurisprudence and alignment into direct conversation: it argues that Dworkin's principle-oriented interpretivism (the view that legal rules are best understood via the principles that justify them, not their literal text) and Sunstein's positivist account of **law as analogical reasoning** (roughly: common-law reasoning is fundamentally reasoning by analogy between cases, with rules as after-the-fact summaries) map onto the live technical distinction between Constitutional AI (rule/document-based) and case-based-reasoning approaches to alignment, and argues the alignment field should take the CBR side of that jurisprudential debate more seriously rather than defaulting to constitutions.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill)** — the most directly philosophically sympathetic 2025–2026 paper to Extension Training's premise (case-based > rule-based alignment specifically because that's what legal theory itself concludes about how precedent works), useful for the memo's framing/motivation section even though, like Feng et al., it proposes no trained system or evaluation.

**Jin, Z., Levine, S., Gonzalez, F., Kamal, O., Sap, M., Sachan, M., Mihalcea, R., Tenenbaum, J., Schölkopf, B. — "When to Make Exceptions: Exploring Language Models as Accounts of Human Moral Judgment" (introduces **MoralExceptQA**). Directly the "distinguishing benchmark" named in the brief. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-eval), (T-data)** — MoralExceptQA is structurally a moral-domain version of "distinguishing": a general rule holds by default, and the task is to identify which situational features are strong enough reasons to carve out an exception, exactly the follow/distinguish decision Peter wants a legal-trained model to transfer to moral cases; it is a ready-made transfer-evaluation set for whether legal-mapping training generalizes.

**The SCOTUS "syllabus" as ready-made facts+holding data, evidenced by Heddaya, M., MacMillan, K., Malani, A., Mei, H., Tan, C. — "CaseSumm: A Large-Scale Dataset for Long-Context Summarization from U.S. Supreme Court Opinions." arXiv 2501.00097, Dec 2024.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00097

**Super-SCOTUS — Fang, B., Cohn, T., Baldwin, T., Frermann, L. — "Super-SCOTUS: A Multi-Sourced Dataset for the Supreme Court of the US." NLLP Workshop (EMNLP) 2023.** https://aclanthology.org/2023.nllp-1.20/ ; data: https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/POWQIT

The paper's own proposed task (predicting a case's ideological decision-direction from oral-argument or syllabus text, with group-disparity analysis by issue area/vote split/winning side) is not directly a mapping task, but the join itself is the valuable artifact.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-data)** — the closest thing found to an already-preprocessed, multi-view SCOTUS dataset (facts, argument, holding, structured metadata, all pre-linked per case) — likely the fastest starting point for a small-model mapping experiment rather than building the joins from CAP/CourtListener + SCDB + Oyez from scratch.

**Open substitute for proprietary citation-treatment labels (Shepard's/KeyCite/BCite): Demir, M. M., Canbaz, M. A. — "Validate Your Authority: Benchmarking LLMs on Multi-Label Precedent Treatment Classification." NLLP Workshop 2025 / ACL Anthology 2025.nllp-1.13.** https://aclanthology.org/2025.nllp-1.13.pdf
Directly answers the brief's "open citation treatment dataset" search. Built from **Paul Hellyer's 2018 hand-annotated corpus** that had itself been constructed to *evaluate* the big three commercial citators (Shepard's, KeyCite, BCite) against each other — i.e., the underlying ground-truth annotations of how one case actually treated another were made independently of any single proprietary vendor. The authors retrieve full-text opinions for these citing relationships via the CourtListener API (239 usable citing-relationships after filtering, ~286 label instances since a citation can carry multiple labels) and define two label schemas: a **16-category fine-grained schema** (Distinguished by, Criticized by, Overruled, Reversed by, Questioned by, etc.) and a **5-category high-level schema** (Limited-or-Distinguished, Criticized-or-Questioned, Invalidated, Conflict Noted, Neutral Citation), released openly on Hugging Face. Benchmarking frontier LLMs zero-shot: best high-level accuracy 79.1% (Gemini 2.5 Flash), best fine-grained accuracy 67.7% (GPT-5-mini) — respectable but well short of citator-grade reliability, and "Distinguished by" alone makes up nearly half the label instances (132/286), meaning the dataset is small and class-imbalanced.
[VERIFIED — fetched]
Relevance: **(T-data), (T-eval)** — the concrete open substitute the brief asked for, but a real limitation for Extension Training to know about: at ~239–286 labeled examples it is far too small on its own to train a mapping skill and would need to function as an eval/validation set (or a seed for LLM-assisted expansion) rather than the primary training-label source; its 5-category schema (which includes "Limited or Distinguished" as one option among several negative-treatment categories, not identical to a clean binary follow/distinguish split) is worth adapting rather than adopting wholesale.

**Mahari, R., Stammbach, D., Ash, E., Pentland, A. — "LePaRD: A Large-Scale Dataset of Judges Citing Precedents." arXiv 2311.09356, Nov 2023 (v3 Oct 2024).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09356
A large open dataset of U.S. federal judicial citations *in context* — the surrounding argumentative text around each citation, not just the citation edge — built for "legal passage prediction" (given an argument's context, predict which precedential passage a judge would cite next). The authors find retrieval-as-classification works best among tested approaches, but call the task still far from solved. A subset was released immediately with the full dataset released on publication.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-data)** — complements the CourtListener citation graph (which only gives the bare edge) by supplying the *textual context* around each citation at scale, which is closer to what a mapping-training example needs (not just "case A cites case B" but "here is the sentence doing the citing work").

**Synthesis — what's public domain vs. party-authored vs. proprietary:**
- **Public domain (U.S. government works, no copyright):** the opinions themselves (majority, concurrence, dissent), the official syllabus, and SCDB's own coding (Spaeth's coding scheme and data are separately released under an open license by the maintaining institution) are all free to redistribute and use for training without licensing risk — this is the load-bearing legal fact behind CAP's, CourtListener's, and Oyez/ConvoKit's openness.
- **Party-authored (copyrighted by the filer, not government works):** merits and amicus **briefs** are written by private parties/counsel and are not automatically public domain; supremecourt.gov and CourtListener/RECAP hosting them for reading does not itself grant a redistribution/training license, so briefs are a materially different (and murkier) data-licensing category than opinions and should be flagged as such if used.
- **Proprietary (commercial, licensed):** **Shepard's** (LexisNexis) and **KeyCite** (Westlaw) treatment signals — the actual "does this later case affirm/distinguish/criticize/overrule this earlier one" labels a working lawyer relies on — are commercial products, not freely redistributable, and the CourtListener citation API explicitly does not attempt to reproduce them (confirmed above). The Hellyer-corpus-derived open substitute above is the best current free stand-in, but is two to three orders of magnitude smaller than what a commercial citator covers.

## 5. Preprocessing ideas already in the literature

**Extracting facts / issue / holding / reasoning via "rhetorical role" segmentation.** Several groups have already built exactly this kind of preprocessing pipeline, though nearly all on Indian (not U.S.) case law:
- **Kalamkar, P., Tiwari, A., Agarwal, A., Karn, S., Gupta, S., Raghavan, V., Modi, A. — "Corpus for Automatic Structuring of Legal Documents." LREC 2022 / arXiv 2201.13125, Jan 2022.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13125 — Segments legal judgment documents into coherent parts, each labeled with a **rhetorical role** from a predefined list, and shows the resulting structure improves downstream summarization and legal-judgment-prediction performance versus using the raw, unsegmented opinion text. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
- **Malik, V., Sanjay, R., Guha, S. K., Hazarika, A., Nigam, S., Bhattacharya, A., Modi, A. — "Semantic Segmentation of Legal Documents via Rhetorical Roles." NLLP Workshop, EMNLP 2022 / arXiv 2112.01836, Dec 2021.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.01836 — The most directly on-point preprocessing paper found for "extract facts/issue/holding/reasoning": a corpus annotated (with legal-expert help) into **13 semantically coherent role labels including facts, arguments, statute, issue, precedent, ruling, and ratio** (ratio decidendi, i.e., the holding's reasoning) — almost exactly the fact/issue/holding/reasoning decomposition the brief asks about, plus a distinct "precedent" role tagging where a document explicitly engages a cited case. A multitask model using role-label-shift as an auxiliary signal beats single-task baselines, and the authors test domain-transfer and distillation under limited data. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
- **Nigam, S. K., Dubey, T., Sharma, G., Shallum, N., Ghosh, K., Bhattacharya, A. — "LegalSeg: Unlocking the Structure of Indian Legal Judgments Through Rhetorical Role Classification." NAACL 2025 / arXiv 2502.05836, Feb 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05836 — Scales the same idea up: the largest annotated rhetorical-role dataset found, 7,000+ documents and 1.4 million sentences across 7 roles, benchmarking BiLSTM-CRF, transformer, GNN, and an instruction-tuned "RhetoricLLaMA" model; finds models using broader document context and neighboring-sentence structure beat sentence-only classifiers, but class confusion between closely related roles and class imbalance remain open problems. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
- **Santosh, T. Y. S. S., Isaia, A., Hong, S., Grabmair, M. — "HiCuLR: Hierarchical Curriculum Learning for Rhetorical Role Labeling of Legal Documents." EMNLP 2024 Findings / arXiv 2409.18647, Sep 2024.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18647 — Proposes a curriculum (easy-to-hard document ordering, then coarse-to-fine role distinctions) for training rhetorical-role labelers, finding it outperforms non-curriculum training across four RRL datasets. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance (all four): **(T-skill), (T-data)** — this whole cluster is the direct methodological precedent for "preprocess an opinion into facts/issue/holding/reasoning" before mapping; the gap for Extension Training is jurisdictional (built almost entirely on Indian judgments, not U.S. federal/state opinions) and the label sets stop at *segmentation*, not at *which factor within the facts section carries the verdict* — segmentation is a necessary but not sufficient preprocessing step before mapping proper.

**Wang, S., Pobbathi, L., Chen, H. — "LAMUS: A Large-Scale Corpus for Legal Argument Mining from U.S. Caselaw using LLMs." *Journal of Computational Law and Legal Technology* / arXiv 2603.08286, Mar 2026 (v2 Jul 2026).** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08286
The most directly relevant preprocessing paper found for a **U.S.**-caselaw mapping pipeline: builds a sentence-level legal-argument-mining corpus from U.S. Supreme Court decisions and Texas criminal appellate opinions, formulating the task as **six-class sentence classification** — facts, issues, rules, analysis, and conclusions (the paper's summary lists "facts, issues, rules, analysis, and conclusions" as the functional components mined) — via a data-centric pipeline: large-scale case collection, **LLM-based automatic annotation**, then targeted human-in-the-loop correction. LLM-assisted verification catches and corrects nearly 20% of initial annotation errors, and final human verification reaches Cohen's κ = 0.85 (strong inter-annotator agreement) on the corrected labels; chain-of-thought prompting substantially improves zero/few-shot LLM performance on the classification task itself, and a supervised LegalBERT baseline is included for comparison. Code and data are on GitHub.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-data)** — this is close to a template pipeline Peter could adapt directly: U.S. caselaw, LLM-assisted sentence-role annotation with human-in-the-loop correction, and a published, open corpus and codebase — the natural next step beyond it for Extension Training is adding a *mapping* layer on top of the "facts/issue/rule/analysis/conclusion" segmentation this corpus already provides (i.e., LAMUS gets you facts-and-holding-separated; it does not yet say which facts carried the holding relative to a specific cited precedent).

**Legal case summarization with facts and holdings.** Already covered in depth in §4 via CaseSumm (Heddaya et al. 2024, arXiv 2501.00097) — 25,600 SCOTUS opinion/syllabus pairs, the largest open legal summarization dataset, first spanning back to 1815, with the key caution that automatic and LLM-judge summarization metrics did not track expert-identified hallucinations (precedent-citation errors, misstated facts) in generated summaries. Cross-referenced here because it is simultaneously a **data source** (§4) and a **preprocessing precedent**: it establishes that "facts + holding, official and at scale" is a solved *data availability* problem for SCOTUS specifically (the syllabus), while summarization *quality evaluation* for legal facts/holdings remains open — directly relevant to how a mapping judge would need to be validated, not just trained.
[VERIFIED — see §4 entry]
Relevance: **(T-eval)** — repeats the §4 point in preprocessing terms: don't trust automatic/LLM-judge metrics alone to validate that a preprocessed facts/holding pair (or a mapping built on top of it) is faithful; some expert spot-checking is load-bearing.

**Argument mining in briefs: Woo, J., Hashemi Chaleshtori, F., Marasović, A., Marino, K. — "BriefMe: A Legal NLP Benchmark for Assisting with Legal Briefs." ACL Findings 2025 / arXiv 2506.06619, Jun 2025.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.06619
Introduces three brief-writing tasks: **argument summarization**, **argument completion** (continue a brief's argument given its opening), and **case retrieval** (find the cases a brief-writer should cite). Frontier LLMs already do well at summarization and guided completion — beating human-written section headings by the paper's own metric — but perform poorly at realistic (unguided) argument completion and at retrieving the actually-relevant supporting cases, i.e., models can paraphrase/continue existing legal argument fluently but struggle to independently find and select the right precedents to argue from.
[VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-eval)** — briefs are where each side makes its own explicit follow/distinguish arguments about the *same* precedents (the adversarial structure HYPO's 3-ply argument, §1, was built to model); BriefMe's finding that models are good at argument *style* but bad at case *retrieval/selection* is evidence the missing skill is specifically precedent-selection-and-mapping, not argumentative fluency — directly targeting what Extension Training aims to train.

**Majority vs. dissent as contrastive training pairs.** No paper using majority/dissent opinion pairs explicitly as *contrastive training data* for a model (in the sense of a preference pair, or a "same facts, opposite reasoning, opposite verdict" training signal) was found in this search — this appears to be a genuine gap in the literature rather than an already-built resource to draw on. The closest adjacent work:
- **Xu, S., Santosh, T. Y. S. S., Ichim, O., Plank, B., Grabmair, M. — "Through the Lens of Split Vote: Exploring Disagreement, Difficulty and Calibration in Legal Case Outcome Classification." arXiv 2402.07214, Feb 2024.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07214 — Builds SV-ECHR, a case-outcome-classification dataset augmented with the *actual vote distribution* of European Court of Human Rights judges (not majority/dissent text, but the disagreement signal itself), and shows current legal-NLP models are poorly calibrated against genuine human label variation/disagreement — models' sense of a case's "difficulty" doesn't track judges' own split votes. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-data), (T-eval)** — confirms disagreement/split-vote signal is being studied as a first-class object in legal NLP (useful for framing a "no clean precedent"/contested-mapping category), but the specific idea of pairing a majority opinion's mapping against a dissent's *competing* mapping of the *same* facts (both sides implicitly disagreeing about which factors carry the verdict) as contrastive training signal appears to be original to Extension Training rather than already built — worth flagging to Peter as both an opportunity and a risk (no existing baseline/eval to compare against).

**Oral-argument hypotheticals datasets.**
- **Dickinson, G. M. — "A Computational Analysis of Oral Argument in the Supreme Court." *Cornell Journal of Law and Public Policy* 28 (2019), 449 / arXiv 2306.05373, Jun 2023.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05373 — Builds predictive models of judicial decisions from the actual *content* of justices' oral-argument questions (not just question counts or ideology), and extracts findings about individual justices' questioning styles and which questions are most central to the oral-argument dialogue. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
- **Zhang, K., Nadeem, N., Zheng, L., Stammbach, D., Henderson, P. — "AI-Assisted Moot Courts: Simulating Justice-Specific Questioning in Oral Arguments." CSLAW 2026 / arXiv 2603.04718, Mar 2026.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04718 — Directly tackles hypothetical-generation: builds and evaluates AI systems (prompt-based and agentic) that simulate a specific justice's likely oral-argument questions for moot-court practice, using a two-layer evaluation framework (realism + pedagogical usefulness) rather than a single correctness metric, since there is no single "correct" question for a given turn. Finds simulated questions are often rated realistic and achieve high recall of the real legal issues the actual justices raised, but suffer from low diversity of question *types* and **sycophancy** (echoing the advocate's framing rather than genuinely probing it) — failures a naive evaluation would miss. [VERIFIED — fetched via arXiv API]
Relevance: **(T-skill), (T-data), (T-eval)** — this is the modern, ML-native descendant of Rissland & Ashley's 1986 hand-built hypothetical-generation heuristics (§1): both papers confirm oral argument is a real, structured source of precedent-probing hypotheticals, that generating *good* (issue-covering, non-sycophantic, appropriately adversarial) hypotheticals is nontrivial even for current models, and that evaluating hypothetical/distinction quality needs a multi-criteria rubric rather than one accuracy number — directly informing how a mapping-validity judge should itself be built and evaluated, not just what it should reward.

## 6. Synthesis

**(a) What a preprocessed appellate training example for "mapping" would contain, given what the literature has already built.** No single existing resource produces this end-to-end, but every piece already exists somewhere and the assembly is mostly engineering, not research. A complete example would need: (1) the opinion text and its official **syllabus** (facts + holding, free, government work, available back to 1815 per CaseSumm, §4); (2) a **rhetorical-role segmentation** of the full opinion into facts/issue/rule/analysis/conclusion sentences (the LAMUS six-class scheme, §5, is the only one of these built specifically on U.S. caselaw rather than Indian judgments, and its LLM-annotate-then-human-verify pipeline, reaching κ = 0.85, is a directly reusable recipe); (3) one or more **cited precedents**, retrieved via CourtListener's citation graph or LePaRD's in-context citation data (§4), each with its own facts/holding pair; (4) a **factor-level diff** between the instant case's facts and each precedent's facts — this is the piece nothing in the current literature outputs automatically; CATO-style factors (§1) are the right representation, but every implemented factor-extraction system found was hand-built for a narrow domain (trade secrets, or Indian statutory schemes), so an LLM would need to be used to extract candidate factors the way LAMUS used one for rhetorical roles, with human/expert spot-checking given CaseSumm's and Large Legal Fictions' warnings that automatic and even LLM-judge quality checks miss exactly this kind of hallucination; (5) a **verdict label** over that factor diff — follow / distinguish / no-precedent, per Horty & Bench-Capon's a-fortiori test (§1) — augmentable with CaseFacts' fourth category, *overruled* (§2), and the Hellyer-corpus-derived multi-label treatment schema (§4) as an external, if small, validation set; and (6) ideally a **contrastive second mapping** from the case's dissent, if one exists, arguing the opposite verdict over the same facts — flagged above (§5) as something no existing dataset already provides, so it would be original construction, not adaptation.

**(b) Which data sources are open and sufficient for a small-model experiment.** The raw text layer is fully solved: CAP's embargo has expired and its ~6-7M-case corpus is now unrestricted and mirrored on CourtListener and Hugging Face (§4); SCOTUS specifically has official syllabi back to 1815 (CaseSumm) and oral-argument transcripts with outcome metadata back to 1955 (Oyez/ConvoKit); Super-SCOTUS (§4) already joins opinions, syllabi, oral argument, and 50+ SCDB structured variables for ~6,700 cases, and is very likely the fastest bootstrap starting point rather than re-joining CAP + SCDB + Oyez from scratch. The two things that are *not* solved and would need real construction work, not just download, are: the factor-diff/mapping layer itself (no open dataset provides it directly — nearest neighbors are LAMUS's rhetorical roles and the tiny 239-example Hellyer/Demir-Canbaz treatment-classification set, §4–5), and reliable treatment/outcome labels at any real scale (CourtListener's citation graph gives structure with no treatment signal; SCDB's "altered precedent" field gives one coarse bit per case, not per-citation). For a first small-model experiment, SCOTUS alone — via Super-SCOTUS or a custom CAP/CourtListener+SCDB join, LLM-annotated for rhetorical roles à la LAMUS, with mapping labels LLM-generated and then human-spot-checked against SCDB's precedent-alteration field and the open treatment-classification set as an eval — is sufficient and tractable; going to circuit-court volume (Pile of Law, §2, or full CourtListener) would only be needed to scale past a proof of concept.

**(c) The three closest priors the memo must cite.** **Case Law Grounding** (Chen & Zhang, §3) is the strongest *quantitative* prior — it directly shows conditioning decisions on retrieved precedents beats rule/constitution grounding by ~20 accuracy points on a real task, though it retrieves-and-conditions rather than training a mapping-writing skill. **Feng et al.'s Case Repositories** (§3) is the strongest *conceptual* prior — explicitly proposed as a CBR complement to Constitutional AI, with a dimension-elicitation-plus-hypothetical-generation pipeline that echoes CATO and Rissland/Ashley without citing them, but remains an unbuilt, unevaluated workshop proposal. For the best 2025–2026 LLM-precedent paper, **Zhang, Grabmair, Gray & Ashley's "Thinking Longer, Not Always Smarter"** (§2) is the clear choice over Case Law Grounding's own 2025 update or the Legal Alignment survey: it is co-authored by Kevin Ashley himself, formalizes distinguishing via CATO-style factor hierarchies exactly as Extension Training needs, and delivers the sharpest available quantitative bar to beat (below).

**(d) The main known failure mode, with numbers.** Two compounding failure modes, both quantified: **hallucinated precedent** — Dahl et al.'s Large Legal Fictions (§2) find LLMs hallucinate specific, verifiable facts about real federal cases 58% of the time (GPT-4) to 88% of the time (Llama 2), and CaseSumm (§4–5) separately confirms expert human review catches precedent-citation errors in LLM-generated summaries that automatic/LLM-judge metrics do not — and **rationalization masquerading as reasoning** — Zhang/Grabmair/Gray/Ashley (§2) show models achieve near-ceiling accuracy at spotting a surface-level factual distinction between cases (Task 1) but their accuracy on *correctly weighing* whether that distinction is significant enough to change the outcome collapses to 11.5%–34.0% (Task 3), while spending *more*, not less, inference-time compute on the wrong answers than the right ones. Read together: a naive mapping-training setup risks a model that fluently narrates a plausible-sounding factor diff (good surface performance, Task 1-level) grounded in a fabricated or misremembered precedent (Dahl et al.'s baseline rate), reaching a confidently-stated verdict that is not actually load-bearing on the facts it cites (Task 3's collapse) — which is exactly the "rationalization, not reasoning" risk a validity-checking judge, rather than an accuracy-only judge, exists to catch.
