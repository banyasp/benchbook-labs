# Strand 07 — Moral Exemplar Corpora, Rule-Exception Evals, and Parable-to-Case-Brief Preprocessing

Research memo for "Extension Training" (Peter Banyas). Compiled starting 2026-09-21. Every claim is tagged with a URL and either **[VERIFIED — fetched]** (the abstract/page was actually fetched — via WebFetch or the arXiv API — and the claim checked against it) or **[UNVERIFIED]** (not fetched in this session; flagged for the author to check before load-bearing use). Written in append-only sections of ≤3,000 words as research proceeds.

Scope recap: the author is building "Extension Training," a Constitutional-AI-style scheme where a model is conditioned on paradigm moral cases with reasons, must write a structured mapping from the paradigm case to a new situation (relations that carry over, disanalogies, verdict — follow / distinguish / no precedent), and a judge model rewards mapping *validity*. The content casebook is meant to be moral exemplars from classical Chinese philosophy (and possibly the Bible and other traditions); the analogical-mapping *skill* is meant to be trained separately on legal opinions (see Strand 05). This strand covers: (1) existing moral-case datasets and their item structure, (2) source-text corpora for the exemplar traditions themselves and their licensing, (3) how one would preprocess a parable into a structured "case brief," and (4) evals that specifically test *distinguishing* rather than rule-following.

## Part 1: Existing moral-case datasets — what the items actually look like

This section surveys machine-ethics/NLP datasets whose *item format* is a candidate template (or anti-template) for the "case" half of Extension Training. For each, the question that matters most for the author's design is: **does the dataset already carry something like a holding + reasons + distinguishing conditions, or is it just a scenario + label?** Most of the field, it turns out, is the latter (scenario → scalar or categorical judgment), which is exactly the gap a legal-style case-brief format would fill.

### 1.1 ETHICS (Hendrycks et al., 2020/2021)
Hendrycks, Dan; Burns, Collin; Basart, Steven; Critch, Andrew; Li, Jerry; Song, Dawn; Steinhardt, Jacob. "Aligning AI With Shared Human Values." *ICLR 2021*. arXiv:2008.02275 (v1 Aug 5 2020; latest v6 Feb 17 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02275 · code/data: https://github.com/hendrycks/ethics
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** ETHICS is a benchmark of contextualized scenarios spanning five ethical frameworks — justice, deontology, virtue ethics, utilitarianism, and commonsense morality — each scored as a separate sub-task with train/test splits and a "hard" adversarial-filtered subset. The stated goal is to test whether models can predict human moral judgments (not to teach ethics), and the paper's headline finding is that models are "partial but not yet fully competent." Relevant granularity: the **virtue** subtask presents a short scenario plus a candidate character-trait word (e.g., "compassionate," "cowardly") and asks whether the trait fits the agent's action — i.e., it operationalizes virtue ethics as trait-attribution over an action description, not as reasoning from a canonical exemplar's life. The **justice** subtask includes an "impartiality" split (would a reasonable person recognize this as fair/unfair) and a "desert" split (do actions match deserved treatment) — these are closer to rule-application than to case-based reasoning.
**Relevance:** Useful as a *negative* precedent for item design — ETHICS items are single-shot scenario→label pairs with no stated holding, no explicit reasons, and no case-to-case mapping structure. It is a good instrument for *measuring* whether a model's raw moral intuitions are already reasonable (a possible sanity-check eval for Extension Training's outputs) but not a template for the case-brief format itself, and the "virtue" subtask specifically shows the field's default way of using character/virtue language is impoverished relative to what an exemplar-based approach needs (trait words, not narrative exemplars with reasons).

### 1.2 Moral Stories (Emelin et al., 2020/2021)
Emelin, Denis; Le Bras, Ronan; Hwang, Jena D.; Forbes, Maxwell; Choi, Yejin. "Moral Stories: Situated Reasoning about Norms, Intents, Actions, and their Consequences." *EMNLP 2021*. arXiv:2012.15738 (posted Dec 31 2020; note the EMNLP proceedings entry is sometimes also linked as arXiv:2204.09438-adjacent work — the canonical preprint for *this* paper is 2012.15738). https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15738 · ACL Anthology: https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.54/ · data: https://github.com/demelin/moral_stories
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract + ACL Anthology page]**
**Summary:** A crowdsourced dataset of ~12,000 structured narratives, each with seven fields: **norm** (a rule-of-thumb, sourced from Social Chemistry 101 — see 1.4), **situation** (context), **intention** (the actor's goal), **moral action**, **immoral action**, and the **consequence** of each action branch. This is the closest thing in the existing literature to a "case" with an explicit governing rule attached to a concrete situation and a branching pair of outcomes. Tasks built on it include generating the norm-consistent action, predicting consequences, and generating an explanatory norm from an action pair (norm *induction*, not just application).
**Relevance:** High. The norm+situation+intention+action+consequence tuple is structurally close to (rule, facts, act, outcome) — a proto-case-brief — but it is missing two things Extension Training needs: (a) an explicit *verdict/holding* separate from the raw norm (the norm is stated up front, not inferred as a "holding" from the outcome), and (b) any notion of a *second* situation that the first case's norm must be *mapped onto*, with reasons for why it carries or doesn't. Moral Stories is single-case; Extension Training is fundamentally a *pair*-of-cases task (paradigm case → target situation). Also note a 2024 follow-up, "An Annotated Contrast Set of 'Moral Stories'" (arXiv:2407.05244), which perturbs Moral Stories items into minimal-pair contrasts — flagged for follow-up in Part 4 below.

### 1.3 Scruples (Lourie, Le Bras, Choi, 2020/2021)
Lourie, Nicholas; Le Bras, Ronan; Choi, Yejin. "Scruples: A Corpus of Community Ethical Judgments on 32,000 Real-Life Anecdotes." *AAAI 2021*. arXiv:2008.09094 (v1 Aug 20 2020; revised Mar 24 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.09094 · code: https://github.com/allenai/scruples
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** Scraped from Reddit's r/AmItheAsshole (AITA) and similar subreddits: ~32,000 real first-person anecdotes ("anecdotes" task) each paired with ~625,000 aggregate community ethical judgments (who was in the wrong), plus a second "dilemmas" task pitting two actions against each other. The paper's key finding, echoed by the author's brief, is that community judgments are often genuinely divisive (not clean majority verdicts), which forced the authors to build new metrics for label distributions rather than single ground-truth labels.
**Relevance:** Moderate-high, mainly as a *methodological* precedent rather than a content template. Two points transfer directly to Extension Training: (a) real moral cases (unlike constructed vignettes) often lack a single "correct" verdict, which argues for the judge model rewarding *mapping validity* (reasons a verdict follows or doesn't) rather than verdict-accuracy against a single gold label — exactly the design choice the author has already made; (b) AITA anecdotes are themselves informal, un-briefed "cases" (facts + implicit norm + community verdict + reasons in the comments) and could be a *raw material* source for constructing additional secular case pairs, or as a held-out transfer-test corpus of contemporary situations to map classical exemplars onto (see synthesis).

### 1.4 Social Chemistry 101 (Forbes, Hwang, Shwartz, Sap, Choi, 2020)
Forbes, Maxwell; Hwang, Jena D.; Shwartz, Vered; Sap, Maarten; Choi, Yejin. "Social Chemistry 101: Learning to Reason about Social and Moral Norms." *EMNLP 2020*. arXiv:2011.00620 (v1 Nov 1 2020; revised Aug 16 2021). https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00620 · code/data: https://github.com/mbforbes/social-chemistry-101
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** ~292,000 free-text "rules-of-thumb" (RoTs) distilled from everyday situations (e.g., "It is rude to run a blender at 5am"), each annotated along 12 dimensions (social judgment, moral foundation, cultural pressure, legality, etc.), yielding 4.5M+ structured annotations. A "Neuro Norm Transformer" model is trained to generate RoTs for novel situations and to reason about them.
**Relevance:** This is the pipeline the task brief specifically asks about — a **situation → rule-of-thumb induction** pipeline, i.e., turning a narrative/situation into an explicit norm statement, which is one of the two directions Extension Training needs (the other being norm/case → new-situation mapping). It is the direct data source for Moral Stories' "norm" field (1.2). For case-brief preprocessing (Part 3), Social Chemistry 101's crowd pipeline (situation description → elicited RoT → multi-dimensional annotation) is a concrete existing methodology to adapt: instead of eliciting a RoT from a modern situation, one would elicit a "holding" + "reasons" + "scope conditions" from a classical parable.

### 1.5 MoralExceptQA (Jin et al., 2022)
Jin, Zhijing; Levine, Sydney; Gonzalez, Fernando; Kamal, Ojasv; Sap, Maarten; Sachan, Mrinmaya; Mihalcea, Rada; Tenenbaum, Joshua; Schölkopf, Bernhard. "When to Make Exceptions: Exploring Language Models as Accounts of Human Moral Judgment." *NeurIPS 2022*. https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/b654d6150630a5ba5df7a55621390daf-Paper-Conference.pdf · code: https://github.com/feradauto/MoralCoT
**[VERIFIED — fetched, NeurIPS PDF]**
**Summary:** MoralExceptQA is a small (148-item) benchmark of vignettes across three norm types — cutting in line (66 items, ~50% rule-breaking-permissible), property damage/trespass (54 items, ~20% permissible), and cannonballing in a shared pool (28 items) — where each vignette systematically manipulates features like who benefits from breaking the rule, how much harm results, and whether the rule's underlying *function* is actually served or undermined by breaking it in this instance. Binary permissible/impermissible judgments are elicited from both humans and LLMs. The paper's "contractualist" model formalizes moral permissibility of rule-breaking as a function of (rule purpose, distribution of benefit, distribution of harm) — i.e., an explicit *distinguishing-conditions* model. The accompanying MORALCOT prompting strategy (chain-of-thought structured around these three factors) improves LM agreement with human judgments by 6.2 F1 over baselines.
**Relevance:** Very high — this is the closest existing dataset to what Extension Training's judge needs to reward. It operationalizes exactly the "follow vs. distinguish" decision as a factor model (purpose/benefit/harm), which is structurally close to a legal-reasoning "factors" analysis (see CATO/HYPO in Part 3) transplanted into moral vignettes. Its small size (148 items, 3 norm types) makes it a natural **held-out distinguishing-transfer eval** (see synthesis) rather than a training source, but its *factor-manipulation methodology* — systematically varying one distinguishing condition at a time across near-identical vignettes — is a template the author should copy when constructing target situations to pair with classical paradigm cases.

### 1.6 Delphi (Jiang et al., 2021/2022)
Jiang, Liwei; Hwang, Jena D.; Bhagavatula, Chandra; Le Bras, Ronan; Liang, Jenny; Dodge, Jesse; Sakaguchi, Keisuke; Forbes, Maxwell; Borchardt, Jon; Gabriel, Saadia; Tsvetkov, Yulia; Etzioni, Oren; Sap, Maarten; Rini, Regina; Choi, Yejin. "Can Machines Learn Morality? The Delphi Experiment." arXiv:2110.07574 (v1 Oct 14 2021; revised Jul 12 2022).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** Delphi is a deep neural network (fine-tuned on Commonsense Norm Bank, itself built on Social Chemistry 101 + Moral Stories + ETHICS + Scruples) that outputs a free-text ethical judgment given a short action description, deployed as the widely-publicized public "Ask Delphi" demo. The paper's own conclusion is candid about limits: strong apparent generalization to novel situations, but "pervasive biases and inconsistencies" (famously including cases where trivial rephrasing flips the verdict, and adversarial prompts eliciting absurd judgments).
**Relevance:** Moderate, mainly cautionary. Delphi is the highest-profile attempt to train a single model to just *emit* a moral verdict from a scenario with no explicit case structure — its well-documented brittleness (verdict flips under paraphrase, no traceable reasons) is a strong argument *for* the author's design choice of requiring an explicit reasoned mapping rather than a bare verdict, and worth citing directly as the failure mode Extension Training is trying to avoid.

### 1.7 MoralChoice / "Evaluating the Moral Beliefs Encoded in LLMs" (Scherrer, Shi, Feder, Blei, 2023)
Scherrer, Nino; Shi, Claudia; Feder, Amir; Blei, David M. "Evaluating the Moral Beliefs Encoded in LLMs." *NeurIPS 2023*. arXiv:2307.14324 (Jul 26 2023).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** Introduces "MoralChoice," a survey instrument of 1,367 two-option moral scenarios (680 ambiguous, 687 low-ambiguity), administered to 28 LLMs to statistically characterize each model's revealed "choice" distribution, its self-consistency, and its sensitivity to question phrasing. Ambiguous scenarios are specifically designed so that neither option is clearly better under common ethical theories.
**Relevance:** Low-moderate as content, useful as an eval-methodology precedent — the ambiguous/unambiguous split and the statistical (distributional, not single-answer) treatment of "belief" is another data point (alongside Scruples and DailyDilemmas) for treating moral judgment as inherently a distribution rather than a single gold label, reinforcing the design choice to reward mapping quality over verdict match.

### 1.8 Value Kaleidoscope / ValuePrism / Kaleido (Sorensen et al., 2023/2024)
Sorensen, Taylor; Jiang, Liwei; Hwang, Jena; Levine, Sydney; Pyatkin, Valentina; West, Peter; Dziri, Nouha; Lu, Ximing; Rao, Kavel; Bhagavatula, Chandra; Sap, Maarten; Tasioulas, John; Choi, Yejin. "Value Kaleidoscope: Engaging AI with Pluralistic Human Values, Rights, and Duties." *AAAI 2024*. arXiv:2309.00779 (v1 Sep 2 2023; revised Apr 2 2024).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** ValuePrism is a dataset of 218K values/rights/duties linked to 31K human-written situations (91% judged high-quality by annotators); Kaleido is a flan-T5-based model trained on it that, given a situation, generates and evaluates the *relevant* values in play (not a single verdict). Human raters preferred Kaleido's value-identification over GPT-4's on accuracy and coverage.
**Relevance:** Moderate-high as a methodology precedent for "situation → relevant considerations" extraction (a sub-step of case-briefing — see Part 3), and directly on-point for the pluralism problem: ValuePrism explicitly models *multiple, sometimes conflicting* values as first-class citizens of a situation rather than collapsing to one verdict, which is close to what a "relations that carry, disanalogies" mapping needs to represent when two traditions' exemplars would license different verdicts (see synthesis, conflict case).

### 1.9 NormBank (Ziems, Dwivedi-Yu, Wang, Halevy, Yang, 2023)
Ziems, Caleb; Dwivedi-Yu, Jane; Wang, Yi-Chia; Halevy, Alon; Yang, Diyi. "NormBank: A Knowledge Bank of Situational Social Norms." *ACL 2023*. arXiv:2305.17008 (v1 May 26 2023; v2 Jul 24 2023).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** 155K situational norms grounded in a "sociocultural frame" (setting, agents' roles, agents' attributes, physical/social/cultural constraints) with 63K unique constraint types drawn from an explicit taxonomy; norms are explicitly non-monotonic — a small change to the frame (e.g., country, age) can cancel an otherwise-applicable norm.
**Relevance:** High as a **structural template** for the "distinguishing conditions" component of a case brief: NormBank's frame slots (setting/role/attribute/constraint) are essentially a factor list, and its explicit non-monotonicity (frame perturbation cancels the norm) is the same phenomenon Extension Training's "distinguish" verdict needs to capture. Worth mining its taxonomy of constraint types directly when designing the case-brief schema's "distinguishing conditions" field (Part 3).

### 1.10 DailyDilemmas (Chiu, Jiang, Choi, 2024/2025)
Chiu, Yu Ying; Jiang, Liwei; Choi, Yejin. "DailyDilemmas: Revealing Value Preferences of LLMs with Quandaries of Daily Life." *ICLR 2025*. arXiv:2410.02683 (v1 Oct 3 2024; revised Mar 15 2025).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv abstract]**
**Summary:** 1,360 everyday two-action moral dilemmas, each tagged with the human values implicated by each action, analyzed against five philosophical/psychological frameworks (incl. Moral Foundations Theory, World Values Survey dimensions). Finds systematic, framework-describable value-prioritization differences across models (e.g., divergent weighting of "truthfulness"), and that neither OpenAI's Model Spec nor Anthropic's Constitutional AI principles fully predict actual model choices, and that end-users cannot reliably steer prioritization via system prompt alone.
**Relevance:** Moderate. Good as a **held-out transfer-eval candidate** (see synthesis) — contemporary, secular, everyday dilemmas that a classical-exemplar-trained model could be tested on for value-mapping quality — and the finding that stated constitutional principles don't predict behavior is a direct empirical motivation for training the *mapping skill* explicitly (as Extension Training does) rather than relying on stated principles alone.

### 1.11 STORAL — story/fable "moral of the story" datasets
Guan, Jian; Liu, Ziqi; Huang, Minlie. "A Corpus for Understanding and Generating Moral Stories" [dataset name: STORAL]. *NAACL 2022*. arXiv:2204.09438 (Apr 20 2022).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** STORAL is a bilingual (Chinese and English) corpus of human-written **moral stories paired with their morals** — i.e., narrative + explicit moral-of-the-story, the fable structure proper (not Moral Stories' norm-then-branching-actions structure). The paper defines two understanding tasks (does a story imply a given moral? which of several candidate morals fits?) and two generation tasks (write a story that implies a given moral; write the moral implied by a given story), and finds current models struggle to bridge concrete plot and abstract moral, especially regarding value-alignment (good/bad framing) between story and stated moral.
**Relevance:** Very high — this is the paper closest in spirit to the author's actual content (Confucian/classical fables). The existence of a **Chinese-language subset** is directly useful: it suggests either (a) STORAL's Chinese moral stories could be scanned for overlap with actual classical-canon parables (unlikely to be verbatim Mengzi/Analects material — STORAL stories are described as "human-written," likely modern folk/didactic stories, not classical excerpts — **flagged for the author to check the STORAL data directly**), or (b) at minimum its *task design* (story→moral induction, moral→story generation, and the story/moral-fit classification task) is a ready-made template for a preprocessing/QA task on classical parables. **[UNVERIFIED sub-claim: whether STORAL's Chinese stories include or overlap with classical philosophical parables — check dataset directly before citing.]**

### 1.12 TF1-EN-3M — synthetic moral fables at scale
Nadas, Mihai; Diosan, Laura; Piscoran, Andrei; Tomescu, Andreea. "TF1-EN-3M: Three Million Synthetic Moral Fables for Training Small, Open Language Models." arXiv:2504.20605 (v1 Apr 29 2025; v2 May 2 2026).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** Three million *LLM-generated* (not classical/human) English fables produced via a combinatorial prompt scaffold with six fixed slots — **character → trait → setting → conflict → resolution → moral** — generated by instruction-tuned open models ≤8B parameters, released with a reproducible quality-evaluation pipeline (LLM-judge panel scoring grammar, creativity, moral clarity, template adherence) under a permissive license.
**Relevance:** High as a **structural template**, low as content (it is synthetic, not classical-canon material, so it cannot itself be the exemplar casebook, but the author should not accidentally conflate it with a "real" fable corpus). Its six-slot scaffold (character/trait/setting/conflict/resolution/moral) is one of the more explicit, publicly-documented "narrative → structured fields" schemas in the literature and is a direct input to the case-brief schema design in Part 3 — notably it already separates **character+trait** (closest thing to an exemplar's virtue) from **moral** (closest thing to a holding), which maps well onto a Mengzian case-brief's "party/exemplar" and "verdict" fields respectively. Its permissive license and open generation pipeline also make it a plausible source of *synthetic distractor/negative* cases for eval construction (e.g., testing whether a judge model rewards spurious "follow" verdicts on fables the mapping shouldn't extend to).

### 1.13 MORABLES — fables as an abstract-moral-reasoning benchmark
Marcuzzo, Matteo; Zangari, Alessandro; Albarelli, Andrea; Camacho-Collados, Jose; Pilehvar, Mohammad Taher. "MORABLES: A Benchmark for Assessing Abstract Moral Reasoning in LLMs with Fables." *EMNLP 2025 Main Conference*. arXiv:2509.12371 (Sep 15 2025).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** A human-verified multiple-choice benchmark built from **real historical fables and short stories** (Aesop-tradition and other historical literature, not synthetic), where the task is to infer the moral (with carefully constructed distractors designed to defeat shallow extractive reading), plus adversarial variants to detect contamination/shortcut reliance. Key finding: even the largest, reasoning-enhanced models are brittle — they self-contradict on ~20% of items depending on how the same moral choice is framed, and scale (not "reasoning" post-training) is the main driver of what performance exists.
**Relevance:** Very high, on two fronts. First, as a **held-out eval**: MORABLES uses real historical fables (the same genre of material — parables with morals — as the author's proposed casebook, albeit Western/Aesopic rather than classical Chinese) and could plausibly serve as an analogous-genre transfer/distinguishing test once adapted (its current task is moral*-of-the-story* selection, not case-to-new-situation mapping, so it would need augmentation, not direct reuse). Second, its adversarial-framing finding (~20% self-contradiction under reframing) is exactly the failure mode — brittle, paraphrase-sensitive moral judgment without real distinguishing reasoning — that a mapping-validity reward is meant to fix, and is worth citing as direct motivating evidence in the author's introduction.

### 1.14 Exemplarist moral theory in AI — status of the literature
Zagzebski, Linda. *Exemplarist Moral Theory*. Oxford University Press, 2017. https://academic.oup.com/book/6293 ; Wikipedia summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exemplarist_Moral_Theory
**[UNVERIFIED — publisher/Wikipedia pages only, not the book itself]**
**Summary:** Zagzebski's theory grounds ethical concepts (the good, the virtuous, the admirable) in **direct recognition of exemplars** (persons we admire on reflection) rather than in prior theoretical definitions of "good" — moral concepts are defined *indexically*, by pointing at exemplars and generalizing from what makes them admirable, structurally close to how the author wants a model to reason from a paradigm *case* rather than from an antecedently stated rule.

Directly-AI-adjacent hits found this session (none is a mature "exemplarist AI alignment" research program comparable to, e.g., the ETHICS or Delphi lines — this appears to be a genuinely underdeveloped intersection, consistent with the author's instinct that this is a gap):
- Fitzner (or similar), "Moral exemplars for the virtuous machine: the clinician's role in ethical artificial intelligence for healthcare." *AI and Ethics*, Springer. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-021-00089-6 **[UNVERIFIED — title/venue only, not fetched]** — argues, in the healthcare-AI domain specifically, that human clinicians should serve as living moral exemplars whose judgment anchors an AI system's ethics, rather than the system being governed by explicit encoded rules alone. Domain-specific (clinical AI) but conceptually the closest hit found to "exemplarist AI alignment" as a named program.
- Han, W.D., "Why do We Need to Employ Exemplars in Moral Education? Insights from Recent Advances in Research on Artificial Intelligence." *Ethics & Behavior*, 2024. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10508422.2024.2347661 **[UNVERIFIED — abstract not fetched]** — appears to run the argument in the *opposite* direction from the author's (uses AI/LLM research as evidence *for* exemplar-based human moral education, rather than proposing exemplar-based training *for* AI systems) — worth checking directly, as it may still contain a usable literature review of the exemplarist-AI intersection.
- Billy Wheeler's "From control to character: a Confucian framework for AI personality" (already covered in depth in Strand 03, entry D2) is the closest **published, AI-alignment-framed** work using an exemplar/case-based (rather than exemplarist-labeled) Mengzian mechanism, though it does not cite Zagzebski by name per the Strand 03 fetch.
**Relevance:** This sub-area is thin. The author's framing ("exemplarist AI alignment") does not yet correspond to an established literature the way "Constitutional AI" or "RLHF" do — the closest adjacent work is domain-specific (healthcare, education) or comes at it from Confucian philosophy directly (Wheeler) without invoking Zagzebski's vocabulary. This is worth stating explicitly as a genuine gap/opportunity in the synthesis, while flagging that the author should still search Zagzebski citation trackers (Google Scholar "cited by") directly, since a 2025–2026 AI-specific application could easily have been missed by keyword search.

### 1.15 Legal case-based-reasoning precedent for "factors" (bridge to Strand 05)
Ashley, Kevin D. "Reasoning with Cases and Hypotheticals in HYPO." *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies*, vol. 34, no. 6 (1991), pp. 753–796 — codifying his 1987 UMass Amherst PhD dissertation (supervised by Edwina Rissland) on the HYPO system for US trade-secret law. Aleven, Vincent, and Ashley, Kevin D. "Toward an Intelligent Tutoring System for Teaching Law Students to Argue with Cases" (CATO), *Proceedings of ICAIL 1991*, pp. 42–52; Aleven, V. "Teaching Case-Based Argumentation Through a Model and Examples" (PhD dissertation instantiating CATO fully), 1997. Secondary source consulted: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HYPO_CBR
**[VERIFIED — fetched (Wikipedia secondary summary); primary IJMMS/ICAIL texts not directly fetched this session — flagged]**
**Summary:** HYPO represents each legal case as a bundle of **dimensions** — prerequisite facts, a "focal slot" indicating which party the dimension favors, and a range showing how the *degree* to which a fact is present strengthens or weakens a party's position — indexed across a small case base (30 cases, 13 dimensions in the original trade-secret domain) to generate 3-ply arguments (cite a favorable case, distinguish/counter with an unfavorable one, respond). CATO extended this into a *pedagogical* system with an explicit, human-authored hierarchy of ~26 abstract "factors" (stronger than dimensions: binary presence/absence of a legally relevant pattern) organized under intermediate legal concepts, used to teach law students to argue both *for* a side using favorable-factor cases and to *distinguish away* precedents using disanalogous factors.
**Relevance:** This is the foundational computational precedent for exactly the "relations that carry, disanalogies" move central to Extension Training, forty years before LLMs — CATO's factor hierarchy is arguably the best existing formal *schema* for a "case brief" in the structured, machine-usable sense the author needs (see Part 3), and the HYPO/CATO 3-ply argument structure (cite-distinguish-respond) is a template worth explicitly citing as the ancestor of the "follow / distinguish / no precedent" verdict taxonomy. Because this sits at the boundary between the legal-analogy-skill strand (05) and this content strand (07), the author should treat it as **shared infrastructure**: the case-brief schema recommended in Part 3 of this memo is essentially a moral-domain re-implementation of a CATO factor case.

### 1.16 Recent moral-competence evaluation frameworks (2026) — context for "verdict vs. reasoning" design choice
Two very recent (2026) papers directly validate the author's judge-on-mapping-validity design choice and are worth citing prominently:
- Haas, Julia; Bridgers, Sophie; Manzini, Arianna; Henke, Benjamin; May, Joshua; Levine, Sydney; Weidinger, Laura; Shanahan, Murray; Lum, Kristian; Gabriel, Iason; Isaac, William; et al. "A roadmap for evaluating moral competence in large language models." *Nature*, vol. 650, no. 8102, pp. 565–573 (Feb 18, 2026). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-10021-1
**[VERIFIED — fetched]**
**Summary:** Distinguishes *moral performance* (correct-looking output) from *moral competence* (correct output for the right, morally relevant reasons), naming three evaluation failure modes: the "facsimile problem" (imitating reasoning without understanding it), moral multidimensionality, and moral pluralism. Recommends adversarial/out-of-distribution cases that alter normative structure, parametric control of relevant vs. irrelevant considerations (directly resembling MoralExceptQA's factor manipulation, 1.5), range-based (not binary) correctness, and pluralistic/steerable outputs across subpopulations.
- Snoswell, Aaron J.; Kilov, Daniel; Lazar, Seth. "Beyond Verdicts: Evaluating Language Model Moral Competence." *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence*, vol. 40, no. 44 (2026). https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/41131
**[VERIFIED — fetched]**
**Summary:** A systematic survey of 69 LLM moral-evaluation studies (2020–2025) finding the field overwhelmingly tests verdict-matching on pre-curated scenarios and neglects "moral sensitivity" (recognizing ethically relevant features in messy, un-curated input), reasoning quality, and framework-steerability; proposes shifting evaluation toward realistic (uncurated) inputs, reasoning-quality and steerability assessment, and reasonableness/consistency rather than single ground-truth answers.
**Relevance:** Both papers are essentially field-level position statements arguing for the exact shift Extension Training's judge-on-mapping-validity design already embodies (reward *why* a verdict follows, not just *whether* it matches a label) — the author should cite both as evidence the field has independently converged on "verdict accuracy is the wrong target," strengthening the case for the project's core design choice. Neither paper proposes classical/religious exemplar casebooks or a legal-analogy-trained mapping skill specifically — the gap the author is positioned to fill remains open in both.

## Part 2: Corpora of the source texts themselves — availability and licensing

This section inventories the actual exemplar/parable *source texts* across traditions, focused on what is legally free to use (public domain, or clearly-licensed open text) versus what is still under copyright and would need to be excluded or separately licensed. **License distinctions matter a great deal for training-data purposes** — "freely readable online" is not the same as "public domain" or "CC-licensed," and several of the most scholarly-respected translations below are explicitly *not* usable without a license.

**[VERIFIED — fetched (Gutenberg/Wikisource listing pages, not full text)]**
**Summary:** Legge's translations (Analects, Mencius, and the other Four/Five Classics) are the standard 19th-century scholarly English versions, all published well before 1923 and confirmed public domain in the US (Gutenberg only hosts confirmed-PD texts). Watson, Burton, trans. *The Complete Works of Chuang Tzu*. ### 2.4 The Bible — public-domain English versions

World English Bible (WEB) — modern (1997 draft, completed 2020) update of the ASV, explicitly and entirely dedicated to the public domain by editor-in-chief Michael Paul Johnson and volunteer collaborators. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_English_Bible ; aggregator of open-license Bible texts in machine-readable XML: https://github.com/seven1m/open-bibles

**Summary:** Three clean, fully public-domain, English full-Bible options exist (KJV, ASV, WEB), with WEB being the most readable modern-English option while remaining unambiguously PD (unlike, e.g., NIV or ESV, which are copyrighted and license-restricted). ### 2.5 Buddhist Jataka tales
Cowell, E. B. (gen. ed.), *The Jātaka, or Stories of the Buddha's Former Births*, 6 vols., Cambridge University Press, 1895–1907 (translators per volume include Cowell, Rhys Davids/Chalmers, W. H. D. Rouse, H. T. Francis, R. A. Neil). Full scans/OCR text on archive.org, e.g. https://archive.org/details/jatakaorstoriesofb01cowe ; https://archive.org/details/complete-cowell-jataka-six-volumes-in-one ; earlier Rhys Davids translation of the Nidānakathā introduction: https://archive.org/stream/buddhistbirth00daviuoft/buddhistbirth00daviuoft_djvu.txt
**[VERIFIED — fetched, archive.org listings]**
**Summary:** ~547 Jataka tales (birth-stories of the Buddha in previous incarnations, each illustrating a virtue — generosity, patience, self-sacrifice, wisdom) in the standard scholarly English translation, published 1895–1907 and unambiguously public domain. Each Jataka has a highly regular internal structure inherited from the Pali canon: a frame story (a present-day situation prompts the Buddha to tell the tale), the past-life story proper, and an explicit identification of which present-day figure corresponds to which past-life character ("the Buddha was the [animal/king/merchant], Devadatta was the [antagonist]," etc.) — this is, notably, **already extremely close to a case-brief format**, since the tale's own frame narrative *is* an explicit act of analogical mapping from a past paradigm case onto a present situation.
**Relevance:** Very high, arguably underrated relative to the author's Mengzi/Analects/Zhuangzi/Bible list — the Jataka's frame-story convention (present situation → recalled precedent → explicit identity-mapping "onto" the present) is structurally the single closest pre-existing literary form to what Extension Training is trying to train a model to do. Worth strong consideration as a fourth or co-equal tradition in the casebook, both for content and because its native structure could nearly serve as a worked example of the target case-brief format with minimal reformatting.

Licensing (CC BY-NC) is compatible with research use but the author should confirm compatibility with any eventual commercial deployment.

The author should treat this exemplar as a deliberately chosen stress-test case rather than default casebook material.

## Part 3: Preprocessing a parable into a "case brief" — schema and prior art

The author's design requires each paradigm case in the casebook to expose, at minimum: **parties/roles**, **relations** between them, **the act** at issue, **the verdict** the case reaches (or that tradition/commentary attaches to it), **the reasons** for that verdict (stated in-text or reconstructed from context/commentary), **the distinguishing conditions** that would change the verdict, and **the level of generality** of the resulting "holding" (how broadly or narrowly the precedent should be read). No single existing dataset in Part 1 has exactly this schema, but several pieces of prior art supply usable fragments, and — notably — some source texts (Jataka tales, Mahabharata dharma debates, Mengzi's own dialogues) are *already* partially pre-briefed by their native narrative structure.

### 3.1 What existing pipelines already extract, mapped onto the target schema

| Situation/facts | Moral Stories' `situation` field (1.2); NormBank's frame `setting` (1.9) | Both are single-sentence/short; classical parables need multi-sentence facts with named parties |
| Parties/roles | NormBank's `agent roles` + `attributes` (1.9); CATO's case parties (1.15) | No existing moral dataset encodes relational role (e.g., *ruler/minister*, *parent/child* — the Confucian role-ethics dimension) as a first-class field |
| The act | Moral Stories' `moral action`/`immoral action` (1.2); Social Chemistry 101's situation (1.4) | Fine as-is; classical texts often narrate the act obliquely (dialogue, not action description) and need light extraction |

### 3.2 The Jataka tale as a native "pre-briefed" case
As flagged in 2.5, the Jataka's fixed structure — present-situation frame story → the Buddha's recollection of a formally analogous past-life precedent → explicit identity-correspondence mapping ("I was the [X], and [present person Y] was then the [Z]") — is not merely *analogous to* a case brief; it is arguably the closest thing in any pre-modern moral-literary tradition to *already being one*, since it explicitly performs the "cite a precedent case and map its parties onto the present situation" move as the story's own narrative device. A first case-brief schema pilot could productively use the Jataka's own frame-story convention as a worked example/scaffold to imitate when briefing Mengzi or Zhuangzi material, which lacks this built-in device.

### 3.3 Legal-AI precedent for LLM-assisted structuring, and validated agreement with human annotators
- Zhang, Li; Gray, Morgan; Savelka, Jaromir; Ashley, Kevin D. "Measuring Faithfulness and Abstention: An Automated Pipeline for Evaluating LLM-Generated 3-ply Case-Based Legal Arguments." *7th Workshop on Automated Semantic Analysis of Information in Legal Text, ICAIL 2025*. arXiv:2506.00694 (May 31 2025).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** Building on prior human-evaluation pilot work, this paper automates evaluation of LLM-generated HYPO-style 3-ply legal arguments (cite a favorable precedent → distinguish/counter with an opposing precedent → respond) by having a second LLM extract the CATO-style *factors* present in a generated argument and comparing them against ground-truth factors in the input case triple. Across 8 LLMs and three tests of increasing difficulty (standard 3-ply argument; argument with precedent roles swapped; recognizing when no shared factors exist and correctly **abstaining**), models were >90% faithful (little outright hallucination of invented factors) on the generation tests, but **systematically under-utilized the full available factor set**, and — most strikingly for Extension Training's "no precedent" verdict — **most models failed the abstention test**, generating a spurious argument even when instructed that no shared factors existed and abstention was the only correct response.
**Relevance:** Extremely high — this is essentially a working prototype of the exact judge-model evaluation Extension Training needs (does the model correctly identify when a case does/doesn't transfer, using an explicit factor-based ground truth), already built and tested on legal material by the same Kevin Ashley who created HYPO (see 1.15). The abstention-failure finding is a direct, empirically-grounded warning: models trained/prompted to produce a "follow" or "distinguish" verdict have a strong default bias toward producing *some* mapping even when the correct answer is "no precedent" — the author should design the judge's reward function and the training distribution specifically to counter this bias (e.g., ensuring a meaningful fraction of paired (case, situation) training examples have "no precedent" as the correct verdict, not just as a rare edge case).

- Kang, Xiaoxi; Qu, Lizhen; Soon, Lay-Ki; Li, Zhuang; Trakic, Adnan. "Automating IRAC Analysis in Malaysian Contract Law using a Semi-Structured Knowledge Base" [dataset: LegalSemi]. *Artificial Intelligence and Law*, 2025. arXiv:2406.13217 (Jun 19 2024; revised Jul 9 2025).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** LegalSemi is a 54-scenario benchmark, each expert-annotated according to the classic law-school **IRAC** framework (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion), paired with a structured knowledge base; experiments show LLMs perform better on issue-identification, rule-retrieval, application, and conclusion-generation when given the structured knowledge base as scaffolding versus unstructured text alone.
**Relevance:** IRAC is a second concrete, decades-old legal pedagogical schema (alongside CATO's factors) that the case-brief format can borrow wholesale or adapt: **Issue** ≈ the question the parable's case poses (what should X do, or was X right to do Y), **Rule** ≈ the norm/holding the case is read as establishing, **Application** ≈ how the rule's factors map onto the new situation's facts (the heart of Extension Training's mapping task), **Conclusion** ≈ the verdict (follow/distinguish/no precedent). This gives the author a second, independently battle-tested four-field skeleton to compare against a from-scratch schema design, and LegalSemi's small-but-expert-annotated size (54 scenarios) is itself a template for how large a high-quality seed casebook needs to be to validate a schema before scaling.

### 3.4 Narrative → norm extraction and validated agreement, more generally
No single paper in this survey performs "narrative → case brief" extraction in the author's exact sense, but three adjacent, already-cited pipelines collectively demonstrate the *pieces* are individually tractable and validated against human judgment:
1. **Social Chemistry 101** (1.4/3.1) validates crowd-elicited rule-of-thumb + 12-dimension annotation at scale (4.5M annotations over 292K RoTs) — proof that short, situation-grounded norm statements can be reliably elicited and multiply-annotated.
2. **Value Kaleidoscope/ValuePrism** (1.8) reports 91% annotator-judged quality on machine-assisted situation→relevant-values extraction at 218K scale, and shows a trained extraction model (Kaleido) can be preferred by human raters over raw GPT-4 prompting — evidence that a *purpose-trained* extractor beats prompting a general model, which bears on whether the author should fine-tune a dedicated "case-briefing" model rather than relying on judge-model prompting alone.
3. **STORAL** (1.11) directly tests story↔moral bidirectional consistency (does story X imply moral Y? generate a story implying moral Y) and finds current models "struggle to bridge concrete plot and abstract moral" — a cautionary data point that the hardest part of case-briefing (inferring the right *level of generality* for the holding) is empirically difficult even for the much easier task of inferring *a* moral, let alone a properly-scoped one.

No paper located in this session reports a systematic **human-vs-LLM inter-annotator-agreement (kappa) study specifically for narrative-to-structured-case conversion**; this appears to be a genuine open methodological step the author would need to run as a pilot (e.g., have 2–3 human annotators independently case-brief the same 20–30 classical parables using a draft schema, measure agreement, and only then scale via LLM-assisted extraction with spot-checked agreement against the human gold set) — **[UNVERIFIED/GAP: no existing published study found; flagged as original methodological work the author will likely need to do rather than cite].**

## Part 4: Evals that test *distinguishing* rather than rule-following

This section focuses specifically on instruments that measure whether a model correctly identifies *when a precedent does not transfer* — over-extension (wrongly following) and under-extension (wrongly failing to follow, or wrongly treating a genuinely analogous case as having no precedent) — as opposed to instruments that just measure verdict accuracy on isolated scenarios.

### 4.1 MoralExceptQA — already the field's primary rule-breaking/exception benchmark
Covered in full at 1.5. Re-flagged here as the *central* existing distinguishing-eval: its explicit purpose-benefit-harm factor manipulation across otherwise-near-identical vignettes (three norm types: cutting in line, property damage, cannonballing) is a direct, small-scale (148 items) precedent for testing whether a model's rule-breaking judgment tracks the *reasons* a rule exists rather than the rule's bare text.

### 4.2 An Annotated Contrast Set of "Moral Stories" (Fitzgerald, 2024)
Fitzgerald, Ben. "Some Issues in Predictive Ethics Modeling: An Annotated Contrast Set of 'Moral Stories.'" arXiv:2407.05244 (Jul 7 2024). Code/data: https://github.com/bfitzgerald3132/MoralStoriesContrastSet
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** An independent researcher builds minimal-pair "contrast sets" over Moral Stories (1.2) items — small, label-flipping perturbations to the situation description (as few as 3–5 words) — and shows a classifier that scored 99.8% accuracy on the original test set collapses to as low as 51% (near chance) under these contrastive perturbations; misleading-norm association drops accuracy to 98.8%(minor), while textual bias (phrasing that leaks the answer) drops it to 77%. The paper explicitly recommends re-examining how social norms are structured in training data, training models to seek context via defeasible reasoning, and filtering for textual leakage.
**Relevance:** Very high as a **methodological template for eval construction**: this is exactly the "systematically vary one distinguishing condition while holding the rest of the situation fixed" methodology (also seen in MoralExceptQA, 4.1) that the author should use to build target-situation variants for each paradigm case in the casebook — including deliberately constructing near-miss variants where the correct verdict is "distinguish" specifically because of a small factual change, to test whether the judge model is rewarding genuine sensitivity to distinguishing conditions or just surface pattern-matching on the paradigm case's vocabulary.

### 4.3 MoralAltDataset — imagining alternatives beyond a forced binary
Choi, Jongchan; Yang, Nari; Park, Sung Soo; Cho, Jaemin; Han, Seoyoung; Shin, Haerin; Park, Jun-Hyung. "Can LLMs Imagine Moral Alternatives Beyond Binary Dilemmas?" *Findings of EMNLP 2026*. arXiv:2606.31213 (v1 Jun 30 2026; v2 Sep 1 2026). Data: https://huggingface.co/datasets/jongchanch/MoralAltDataset
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** 307 "Advisor" and "Agent" dilemmas, each augmented with a *compromise* option and a *reframed* alternative beyond the original two forced choices; compares human and 15 LLMs' choices in binary vs. four-option settings, finding that offering alternatives shifts the aggregate choice distribution substantially (a compromise option is often preferred over either original horn) and that human-LLM agreement is *stronger* on the alternative options than on the original binary — but that LLMs favor GPT-5-authored alternatives over human- or other-model-authored ones at a rate humans don't replicate (a "who authored the option" bias not present in human judgment).
**Relevance:** High and directly on-point for the "no precedent"/reframe branch of Extension Training's verdict taxonomy: this is a rare dataset that operationalizes *moral imagination* (generating a genuinely new option rather than choosing between the two given) as a measurable capability, which is structurally adjacent to what "no precedent — neither follow nor distinguish, this needs a new answer" should look like in the author's verdict schema, and its authorship-bias finding (over-preferring self-generated-style alternatives) is a concrete failure mode to guard against in the judge model.

### 4.4 The Pluralistic Moral Gap — distributional alignment and value-diversity under-coverage
Russo, Giuseppe; Nozza, Debora; Röttger, Paul; Hovy, Dirk. "The Pluralistic Moral Gap: Understanding Judgment and Value Differences between Humans and Large Language Models." arXiv:2507.17216 (Jul 23 2025); also appears as *EACL 2026* long paper.
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** Introduces the Moral Dilemma Dataset (1,618 real-world dilemmas, each with a *distribution* of human binary judgments plus free-text rationales), and a 60-value taxonomy built from 3,783 extracted value-expressions. Finds LLM-human alignment holds only under high human consensus and deteriorates sharply as human disagreement rises, and that LLMs draw on a systematically narrower value palette than humans do. Proposes "Dynamic Moral Profiling" (Dirichlet-sampling conditioned on human value profiles) as a partial fix, improving distributional alignment by 64.3%.
**Relevance:** High for the *distinguishing* framing specifically: "alignment deteriorates as disagreement rises" is another way of saying models are worst exactly where genuine case-by-case distinguishing judgment is most needed (contested cases), and best on cases so easy that rule-following alone would suffice — i.e., existing models' apparent competence is concentrated precisely where the "follow vs. distinguish" skill matters least. This is strong motivating evidence that a model's moral competence should be measured on the *hard*, high-disagreement tail specifically, not aggregate accuracy, echoing Beyond Verdicts and the Nature Roadmap paper's "range-based, not binary" recommendation (1.16).

### 4.5 Over-/under-application of precedent — legal-domain analogs (bridge to Strand 05)
- Zhang, Li; Savelka, Jaromir; Ashley, Kevin. "Do LLMs Truly 'Understand' When a Precedent Is Overruled?" *JURIX 2025*. arXiv:2510.20941 (Oct 23 2025).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** Tests state-of-the-art LLMs on identifying overruling relationships across 236 real US Supreme Court case pairs; finds era-sensitivity (worse on historical cases — a training-data recency bias), shallow-heuristic reliance rather than genuine doctrinal comprehension, and context-dependent failures where models assert temporally impossible overruling relationships in complex open-ended settings despite passing simpler sanity checks.
- Fan, Wei; Zheng, Tianshi; Hu, Yiran; Deng, Zheye; Wang, Weiqi; Xu, Baixuan; Li, Chunyang; Li, Haoran; Shen, Weixing; Song, Yangqiu. "Legal Rule Induction: Towards Generalizable Principle Discovery from Analogous Judicial Precedents." *EMNLP 2026*. arXiv:2505.14104 (May 20 2025; revised Aug 27 2026).
**[VERIFIED — fetched, arXiv API]**
**Summary:** Formalizes "Legal Rule Induction" (deriving a concise, generalizable doctrinal rule from a *set* of analogous precedents) and builds the first LRI benchmark (5,121 training case-sets / 38,088 cases; 216 expert-annotated gold test cases, Chinese law). Explicitly finds SOTA LLMs "struggle with **over-generalization** and hallucination" — i.e., the induced "rule" is stated more broadly than the precedent set actually supports — and that fine-tuning on the LRI dataset measurably improves capturing "nuanced rule patterns across similar cases" (under-generalization/missed nuance being the implicit counterpart failure).
**Relevance:** Both papers, though built on real legal corpora rather than moral parables, are the most directly relevant *named instances* of over-extension (precedent-overruled paper: asserting a still-valid precedent has been overruled or vice versa; legal-rule-induction paper: stating the holding too broadly) versus under-extension (missing the precedent's actual scope) in the LLM literature, and both are recent enough (2025–2026) and specific enough about *which* failure mode (over- vs. under-generalization) to cite directly when motivating why Extension Training's judge needs to reward correctly-scoped holdings, not just correct top-level verdicts. Kevin Ashley's continued authorship across HYPO (1.15/1991), the 3-ply LLM-argument pipeline (3.3, 2025), and the precedent-overruled paper (2025) makes him arguably the single most relevant living researcher for the *legal-analogy-skill* half of the author's two-strand design (content: exemplars, this strand; skill: legal analogy, Strand 05) — worth flagging for Strand 05 as well if not already covered there.

## Synthesis

**(a) Recommended content casebook for a first experiment.** Favor breadth-of-license over breadth-of-tradition: build the first casebook from three fully public-domain, structurally complementary sources rather than the full list surveyed above. (1) **Mengzi and the Analects**, via Legge (2.1) — the anchor tradition, since the author's own framework (junzi, extension of the moral sprouts) is Mengzian, and Mengzi's own method (*tui* — "extending" compassion from a known case, e.g. the ox going to slaughter, to an unknown one) is arguably the *original* statement of the analogical-extension move the project is trying to train, making it uniquely apt as both content and methodological namesake. (2) **The Jataka tales**, via Cowell (2.5) — chosen specifically because their native frame-story structure is the closest pre-existing literary form to a worked case-brief (2.5, 3.2), giving the author both rich content *and* free scaffolding examples for the schema-design pilot. (3) **A small, deliberately curated set of biblical parables** (Nathan and David, the Prodigal Son, the Good Samaritan — via WEB or ASV, 2.4) chosen for their unusually explicit argumentative structure (Nathan's parable is *itself* a forced-analogy trap) rather than the full biblical corpus. Target size for a first experiment: small and hand-curated, not scraped at scale — on the order of 100–300 paradigm cases total (roughly 100 per tradition), each fully case-briefed and reviewed, rather than thousands machine-extracted with no quality control; every dataset surveyed in Part 1 that scaled past a few thousand items (Social Chemistry 101, NormBank, ETHICS) did so only *after* a validated small-scale schema and annotation pipeline existed, and Legal Rule Induction's 216-item *expert-annotated* gold test set (vs. 38K weakly-labeled training cases) shows the field's own convention of a small gold set plus larger weak-label set. **Deliberately include at least one cross-tradition conflict case**: the strongest, cleanest candidate found in this survey is Abraham's binding of Isaac (Genesis 22) against Mengzi's grounding of *ren* in the spontaneous, unrefusable horror at seeing a child about to fall into a well (Mengzi 2A6) — one tradition's paradigm case is an exemplar of obedience overriding the parent-child bond and the felt horror of harming an innocent, the other's paradigm case treats exactly that felt horror as the *foundation* of all moral extension; a model asked to map either case onto the other's domain should not be able to produce a clean "follow" verdict, and the judge's ability to reward a well-reasoned "distinguish" (or an honest "these traditions conflict, no single precedent holds") on this pair is itself a meaningful test of the whole system. A second, lower-stakes intra-tradition conflict (Mengzi vs. Xunzi on human nature, 2.3) is worth including as a gentler warm-up case before the Abraham/Isaac-vs-Mengzi pair.

**(b) Case-brief schema.** Synthesizing Part 3's table, the recommended schema per paradigm case has eight fields: **(1) Parties/roles** (named, with relational role specified — e.g., ruler/minister, parent/child, stranger/stranger — since role-differentiated obligation is load-bearing for a Confucian-framed system in a way it isn't for Western case schemas); **(2) Facts** (the situation, extracted/lightly paraphrased from source text); **(3) The act at issue**; **(4) Verdict** (what the case is read as endorsing or condemning); **(5) Reasons** (stated if the text argues explicitly — Mengzi's dialogues often do — reconstructed via commentary or inference if not, and flagged which of the two it is); **(6) Distinguishing conditions / factors** (a short factor list in CATO's sense — what facts, if changed, would flip or void the verdict — seeded initially from NormBank's frame-slot taxonomy and MoralExceptQA's purpose/benefit/harm triple, then extended with moral-domain-specific factors as the pilot annotation surfaces them); **(7) Scope of the holding** (an explicit statement, separate from the verdict, of how broadly the case should be read — borrowing HYPO/CATO's "dimension range" concept and IRAC's "Rule" field as the two clearest existing precedents for this, the single biggest gap identified in Part 3); **(8) Source and commentary trail** (citation to the primary text and, where used, which commentarial tradition supplied the reasons/scope reading in fields 5 and 7, since for material like the Talmud or Zhu Xi's Mengzi commentary the "holding" is itself a live interpretive dispute rather than a settled fact — this field is what lets the pluralism problem in (d) be represented rather than papered over).

**(c) Held-out transfer/distinguish sets.** Three tiers, by domain-distance from the training casebook: **near-domain** — DailyDilemmas (1.10, contemporary secular quandaries, good first transfer test of whether classical-exemplar training generalizes to modern situations at all) and Scruples (1.3, real AITA anecdotes, useful because their community judgments are contested rather than clean, testing calibrated "distinguish"/"no precedent" behavior on genuinely hard cases); **same-genre, different-tradition** — MORABLES (1.13, real historical Western fables) as a same-structure/different-content transfer test, and Aesop (2.6) directly, since both let the author ask "does training on Mengzi/Jataka/Bible transfer to structurally similar but culturally unrelated fable material?"; **adversarial/structural** — MoralExceptQA (1.5/4.1) and the Moral Stories contrast set (4.2) for factor-sensitivity under minimal perturbation, and the 3-ply legal-argument abstention test (3.3/4.5) adapted to the moral domain as a direct test of whether the judge over-rewards a confident "follow" verdict when "no precedent" is actually correct — given that paper's finding that most LLMs fail exactly this test in the legal domain, the author should assume the moral-domain analog needs explicit correction too, not assume it will fall out for free from mapping-validity training.

**(d) The pluralism risk, and how to state it.** This survey turned up no single existing benchmark, corpus, or schema that resolves the whose-exemplars problem — it is not a solved problem elsewhere that the author can inherit a fix for. State it plainly rather than engineering around it: (i) every tradition surveyed contains *internally contested* exemplars (the Twenty-four Exemplars' Guo Ju story, 2.12; Mengzi vs. Xunzi, 2.3; the Mahabharata's multi-voiced dharma debates, 2.10), so "classical Chinese moral exemplars" is not a univocal source even before cross-tradition conflict is considered; (ii) the field's most recent, most authoritative position papers on LLM moral evaluation (the Nature Roadmap, 1.16, and Beyond Verdicts, 1.16) both name pluralism as a first-order evaluation challenge, not a solved footnote, and both recommend range-based/steerable/subpopulation-aware evaluation rather than single ground truth — the author's schema field (8), source-and-commentary-trail, is the project's mechanism for making that steerability auditable rather than silent; (iii) the honest framing for the author's write-up is that Extension Training does not aim to produce *the* correct verdict on contested cases, but to produce a model that can correctly identify *which* considerations are in tension, state them, and reach a reasoned verdict *conditional on* a specified interpretive tradition or set of weights — with the Abraham/Isaac-vs-Mengzi pair in (a) serving as the project's own internal proof that it can represent "these two paradigm cases are in genuine conflict" as a first-class output rather than forcing a false resolution.
