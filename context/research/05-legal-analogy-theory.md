## 1. The Jurisprudence of Precedent and Analogy

### 1.1 Edward Levi — the three-step "reasoning by example"

Levi's foundational statement of common-law method was published first as a law-review article and then, in slightly expanded form, as the 1949 University of Chicago Press book *An Introduction to Legal Reasoning*. The article version is openly available:

- Edward H. Levi, "An Introduction to Legal Reasoning," 15 *University of Chicago Law Review* 501 (1948), https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5687&context=journal_articles — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Levi's central claim is that legal reasoning is not deduction from fixed rules but a recurring three-step move:

> "similarity is seen between cases; next the rule of law inherent in the first case is announced; then the rule of law is made applicable to the second case." — Levi 1948, quoted at https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5687&context=journal_articles **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

He states the method's name directly: "The basic pattern of legal reasoning is reasoning by example. It is reasoning from case to case." — same source, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Two features of this account matter for Extension Training:

1. **The rule is not given prior to its first application** — it is *announced* at the moment a court decides that case A resembles case B, so the "rule" is a byproduct of a similarity-judgment rather than an independent premise the similarity-judgment merely applies.
2. **The classification is "moving."** Levi writes that legal concepts must stay "ambiguous in order to permit the infusion of new ideas," and that "the kind of reasoning involved in the legal process is one in which the classification changes as the classification is made. The rules change as the rules are applied." — same source, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. This is the jurisprudential ancestor of the idea that a precedent's "scope" is not fixed at the moment of decision but is renegotiated by each subsequent mapping — directly relevant to how Extension Training should treat an exemplar's scope as something the mapping step contests rather than something read off a label.

### 1.2 Cass Sunstein — four features of analogical reasoning, and incompletely theorized judgments

- Cass R. Sunstein, "On Analogical Reasoning," 106 *Harvard Law Review* 741 (1993), pp. 741–791, PDF at http://www.horty.umiacs.io/courses/readings/sunstein-1993-analogy.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

At pp. 746–748 Sunstein gives the canonical four-feature list requested for this strand:

> "In law, analogical reasoning has four different but overlapping features: principled consistency; a focus on particulars; incompletely theorized judgments; and principles operating at a low or intermediate level of abstraction." — Sunstein 1993, pp. 746–748, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

- **Principled consistency**: like cases must be treated alike, which forces articulation of *some* general premise even when the parties disagree about ultimate theory.
- **Focus on particulars**: the reasoning proceeds bottom-up from concrete fact patterns rather than top-down from an antecedently chosen moral or political theory.
- **Incompletely theorized judgments**: participants can converge on a result and even on a mid-level rule without agreeing on — or fully articulating — the deep theory that would justify it. At p. 747: "Lawyers might firmly believe...that the state cannot regulate political speech without a showing of immediate and certain harm. But it is characteristic of reasoning by analogy...that lawyers are not able to explain the basis for these beliefs in much depth or detail, or with full specification of the theory that accounts for those beliefs." — Sunstein 1993, p. 747, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- **Low/intermediate-level principles**: the operative principles that carry a decision from precedent to new case are pitched below the level of grand theory (e.g., "not below constitutional first principles"), which is what lets people with very different foundational commitments still agree on the *mapping*.

Sunstein's larger thesis (p. 759, per the fetched text) is that analogical reasoning is not a degraded substitute for full moral theory but is often *better suited* to legal decision-making under social and political disagreement, precisely because it does not require litigants, judges, or the public to first resolve deep theoretical disputes — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. This is directly portable: it is an argument that a mapping-based extension policy does not need — and arguably should not need — the model to hold a fully worked-out ethical theory in order to extend a verdict responsibly.

- Frederick Schauer, "Why Precedent in Law (and Elsewhere) Is Not Totally (or Even Substantially) About Analogy," 3 *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 454 (2008), DOI page https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00090.x and SSRN abstract page https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1007001 — **[VERIFIED — fetched, abstract only]**. Schauer's thesis, corroborated by the SEP entry (Lamond, §2.1, discussed below, listing "Schauer 1989, 1991" among rule-based precedent theorists and noting "Schauer's suggestion of 'a presumption against distinguishing'") **[VERIFIED — fetched]**, is that binding precedent works because a later court is bound by the *rule* (or holding) the earlier court actually articulated — a rule that has a determinate, if contestable, scope — not because the later court independently judges the two fact patterns to be relevantly similar the way one judges any two analogous situations. On Schauer's account, ordinary analogical reasoning lets the reasoner pick which similarities matter; precedential constraint in a hierarchical, stare-decisis system takes that choice partly out of the later court's hands, because the earlier court's characterization of its own holding is itself binding (subject only to distinguishing). This yields a rule-like, source-based, authority-driven constraint rather than a similarity-driven, content-based one. **This specific formulation of "why" is Schauer's well-known and frequently cited position, consistent with all three sources above, but the exact wording above beyond the quoted abstracts is a paraphrase pending direct verification against the 1987/2008 full texts — flagged [UNVERIFIED] for verbatim quotation purposes.**

Per the fetched NDPR review, Alexander & Sherwin's central claim (their p. 3, as quoted in the review) is that "legal reasoning is ordinary reasoning applied to legal problems" — there is no sui generis "legal logic." — **[VERIFIED — fetched (secondary quotation of primary text)]** More specifically to analogy, the review reports (quoting A&S at their pp. 87, 104, 234) that Alexander & Sherwin hold that well-known modes of legal reasoning — "analogical reasoning, distinguishing cases, and reasoning by principles" — "are illusory" (p. 104): they do not exist as a *logically* distinct method (because on inspection they reduce to ordinary deductive/rule-based reasoning) and, insofar as they are treated as intuitive pattern-matching, they are "purely intuitive and therefore unreasoned and unconstrained" (p. 234) and "do[] not constrain the outcome of [a] new case in any predictable or even detectable way" (p. 87). — **[VERIFIED — fetched (secondary quotation)]** The reviewer, Priel, pushes back, arguing psychological research on analogy (e.g., structure-mapping) supports treating analogical reasoning as a genuine, non-reducible cognitive process, and that A&S's focus on *explicit* deliberation ignores the tacit dimension of expert judgment — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. This is a useful internal debate for the project: Alexander & Sherwin represent the "eliminativist" pole (analogy is either rule-application in disguise or noise) against which Sunstein, Brewer, and Weinreb (below) all push back from different directions.

### 1.5 Scott Brewer — abduction, confirmation, application

- Scott Brewer, "Exemplary Reasoning: Semantics, Pragmatics, and the Rational Force of Legal Argument by Analogy," 109 *Harvard Law Review* 923 (1996), PDF at http://www.horty.umiacs.io/courses/readings/brewer-1996-analogy.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Brewer's model, quoted at pp. 925–926:

> "reasoning by analogy is a three-step rule-guided process that legal reasoners typically deploy when they are in doubt about the scope of a legal concept or rule and want to use an analysis of examples to help resolve that doubt. The three-step process consists of an inference (of the type known as 'abduction') from chosen examples to a rule that could resolve the doubt; confirmation or disconfirmation, by a process of reflective adjustment, of the rule thus inferred; and application of the confirmed rule to the case that occasioned the doubt." — Brewer 1996, pp. 925–926, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

So Brewer's three moves are: **(1) abduction** — from the paradigm case(s) to a candidate rule that would explain why the paradigm case came out as it did; **(2) confirmation** — reflective testing of that candidate rule against other known cases and intuitions (essentially a coherence/reflective-equilibrium check); **(3) application** — applying the now-confirmed rule to the new, doubtful case. This is architecturally close to what Extension Training already asks the model to do (propose a mapping, have it judged, apply the verdict), and it gives a named, citable three-stage decomposition (abduction → confirmation → application) that is more fine-grained than "map then apply." Brewer's overall thesis, at p. 926: "legal reasoning by analogy, like analogical argument in logic, mathematics, and the natural sciences, can have a great deal of rational force when properly executed," positioning him against both radical skeptics (Alexander & Sherwin's ancestors, e.g. Posner) and uncritical "mystics" who treat analogy as self-justifying. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

### 1.6 Lloyd Weinreb — analogy as ordinary practical reason

- Lloyd L. Weinreb, *Legal Reason: The Use of Analogy in Legal Argument* (Cambridge University Press, 1st ed. 2005; 2nd ed. 2016). Archive.org catalog record: https://archive.org/details/legalreasonuseof0000wein — **[UNVERIFIED]** (catalog page only, not full text)

Per the NZLII-hosted review, Weinreb's thesis is that analogical legal reasoning is not logically defective or merely rhetorical; rather "it is the same as the reasoning used in everyday life and is dictated by the nature of law which requires the application of rules to particular facts." — **[VERIFIED — fetched (secondary quotation)]** Weinreb explicitly engages and rejects both the Levi/Sunstein "distinct legal method" framing and Judge Posner's reduction of analogy to disguised policy-making. ### 1.7 Grant Lamond's SEP entry — ratio/obiter, distinguishing, levels of generality, rules-vs-reasons

- Grant Lamond, "Precedent and Analogy in Legal Reasoning," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 20 June 2006; substantive revisions in later archived editions), https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/legal-reas-prec/ — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Key distinctions and quotes, all from the fetched entry:

- **Ratio vs. obiter** (§2.1): "The *ratio* of a case represents the 'holding' or 'ruling', i.e., the proposition of law for which the case is authority — it is the aspect of the case which is binding on later courts. *Obiter dicta*, by contrast, represent other statements and views expressed in the judgment which are not binding on later courts." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- **Distinguishing** (§2.1.2): "Distinguishing involves a precedent not being followed even though the facts of the later case fall within the scope of the *ratio* of the earlier case," subject to the formal constraint that "the factors in the *ratio* of the earlier case must be retained, and the ruling in the later case must be such that it would still support the *result* reached in the precedent case." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** This is an important structural point for Extension Training's own "distinguish" verdict: legally, distinguishing is not simply "this case is different"; it is a disciplined move that must still be compatible with the *result* the precedent reached, i.e., a valid distinguishing move cannot silently undercut the precedent's own correctness.
- **Levels of generality** (§2, using the *Donoghue v. Stevenson* "snail in the ginger-beer bottle" example): "What is the key characterisation of the vehicle of harm on these facts? The bottle of ginger beer is a beverage, but it is also a consumable, an article for human use and something capable of causing injury if negligently produced," and consequently "the level of generality will not be clear and it will not be possible to give a very precise account of the ruling." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** This is the SEP-level statement of exactly the "levels of generality" problem the assignment asks for, and it is a clean, portable illustration: any single fact pattern supports many nested candidate rules, and nothing in the precedent alone picks out the "right" level — later courts, arguing in both directions, do that work.
- **Rules vs. reasons** (§2.1–2.3): Lamond lays out three rival models of what a precedent obligates a later court to do. The rule-model (§2.1, associated with Schauer 1989/1991 and Alexander 1989) treats the *ratio* as a binding rule. The principles-model (§2.2) treats distinguishing as legitimate whenever "the *justification* for the result in the precedent does not apply to the different facts of th[e] case." The reasons-model (§2.3) goes further, treating the *ratio* not as a rule that presumptively settles later cases but as, in Lamond's words, providing "a *pro tanto* justification for such a disposition, i.e., other things being equal this is how the later case should be decided" — precedent as a weighed reason rather than a rule. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- Lamond also credits Sherwin (1999) with identifying indirect epistemic/institutional benefits of analogical/precedential reasoning independent of whether any given analogy is "correct": "exposing judges to a wider variety of fact situations than the particular set before them, making them consider the views of other judges in previous cases and exerting a conservative pressure on individual decision-makers." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** This is a distinct, institution-level argument for why courts reason this way (discipline and moderation of individual judges) that is separate from the argument that the resulting mappings are epistemically correct — a distinction the project's caution section (§6 below) returns to.

### 1.8 Dworkin's "gravitational force" of precedent

- Ronald Dworkin, "Hard Cases," ch. 4 of *Taking Rights Seriously* (Duckworth, 1977; enlarged ed. 1978) — primary text not independently fetched in this session (paywalled/not hosted); characterization below is via two secondary academic sources, both fetched:
  - Nicholas W. Barber, "Why Precedent Works," in *Philosophical Foundations of Precedent* (Oxford University Press, 2023), excerpt fetched at https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:026457be-614b-4757-b508-e58cce74b3e4/files/r0z708x294 — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Barber writes (p. 50 per the fetched excerpt) that he is borrowing "to steal a term from Ronald Dworkin, '**gravitational force**'" to describe how non-binding precedential material can exert persuasive weight on later judges even absent formal binding authority, and cites "R Dworkin, 'Hard Cases' in *Taking Rights Seriously* (Duckworth 1978)." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
  - A second, independent Harvard Journal of Law & Public Policy piece confirms the term's currency (used loosely, in a related but not identical sense, by Justice Kavanaugh as "gravitational pull") but was not itself useful for Dworkin's original definition: https://journals.law.harvard.edu/jlpp/jarkesy-and-gravitational-pull-the-supreme-courts-approach-to-precedent-and-its-implications-michael-showalter — **[VERIFIED — fetched, confirms the term is in wide circulation but does not quote Dworkin]**

Dworkin's own concept (as consistently glossed in the secondary literature, though the primary text should be checked directly by the author before quoting Dworkin verbatim — flagged **[UNVERIFIED]** for exact page-quotation) is that a decided case has two distinct kinds of force: (a) **enactment force** — the narrow, rule-like binding authority the decision has within its own jurisdiction/hierarchy, which is what a strict doctrine of stare decisis captures; and (b) **gravitational force** — a broader, principle-mediated pull the decision exerts on *related* cases, in proportion to how well the *principles* that best justify the earlier decision also bear on the new case, even where the earlier decision is not strictly binding (e.g., a persuasive decision from another jurisdiction, or a binding decision being extended by argument rather than by rule). On Dworkin's general jurisprudential picture (elaborated across *Taking Rights Seriously* and later *Law's Empire*), a judge deciding a hard case is meant to identify the scheme of principles that provides the best constructive justification ("fit" and "moral attractiveness") of the existing body of decisions, and it is *that* scheme of principles — not the precedent's literal holding — that determines how far its gravitational force extends into new facts. This maps closely onto "extension by principle" as opposed to "extension by rule," and is a natural theoretical anchor for why Extension Training's mapping step should be asked to articulate *which relation* (not just *that* a relation) carries the verdict over: Dworkin's framework says gravitational force *is* exactly the set of principles that both justify the precedent and reach the new facts.

### 1.9 The levels-of-generality problem in *Michael H. v. Gerald D.* — Scalia's footnote 6

- *Michael H. v. Gerald D.*, 491 U.S. 110 (1989), plurality opinion by Scalia, J. (joined in full by Rehnquist, C.J., and in part by O'Connor and Kennedy, JJ.). Full text: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/491/110 and https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/491/110/ — **[VERIFIED — fetched, both sources; footnote 6 text obtained in paraphrase/partial-quote form, not a single clean verbatim block — flagged below]**
- Secondary discussion confirming exact wording of the key line: Neil S. Siegel, "The Levels-of-Generality Game: 'History and Tradition' in the Roberts Court," Yale Law School faculty paper, https://law.yale.edu/sites/default/files/documents/faculty/papers/siegel.levels-of-generality-pub.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Footnote 6 (491 U.S. at 127 n.6) is the *locus classicus* for the levels-of-generality problem in constitutional/common-law reasoning. As captured across the fetched sources, Scalia's plurality states that in identifying whether an asserted liberty interest is protected by tradition, courts should "refer to the most specific level at which a relevant tradition protecting, or denying protection to, the asserted right can be identified" — **[VERIFIED — fetched, near-verbatim per Justia/Cornell excerpt]** — and gives a rule-of-law rationale for insisting on specificity: general traditions "provide such imprecise guidance" that they "permit judges to dictate, rather than discern, the society's views," quoted via the Siegel paper — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Justice O'Connor's separate concurrence (joined by Kennedy) pointedly declined to join footnote 6, saying she "would not foreclose the unanticipated by the prior imposition of a single mode of historical analysis" — **[VERIFIED — fetched, Justia excerpt]** — which is why footnote 6 commands only a plurality, not a majority. Justice Brennan's dissent argued that Scalia's most-specific-level methodology, applied consistently, would have doomed landmark precedents like *Griswold* and *Stanley*, since "if we had asked... whether the specific interest under consideration had been traditionally protected," those rights would not have been found — **[VERIFIED — fetched, Justia excerpt]**. Siegel's paper generalizes the lesson: the choice of generality level is itself outcome-determinative and value-laden — "[t]he selection of a level of generality necessarily involves value choices" (quoting Paul Brest) — so specificity is not a neutral, discretion-eliminating rule but simply relocates the judge's discretion to an earlier step (choosing the level) — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. This is one of the cleanest, most citable single illustrations in all of U.S. law of exactly the "scope" problem the Extension Training mapping step is meant to make explicit and arguable rather than silently assumed.

### 1.10 The legal-realist critique — Holmes and Llewellyn

- Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., *The Common Law* (1881), Lecture I, full text at Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2449/2449-h/2449-h.htm — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Opening lines, verbatim: "The object of this book is to present a general view of the Common Law. To accomplish the task, other tools are needed besides logic. It is something to show that the consistency of a system requires a particular result, but it is not all. **'The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience.'** The felt necessities of the time, the prevalent moral and political theories, intuitions of public policy, avowed or unconscious, even the prejudices which judges share with their fellow-men, have had a good deal more to do than the syllogism in determining the rules by which men should be governed." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

- Karl N. Llewellyn, *The Bramble Bush* (Oceana, 1930), excerpt pp. 70–76, hosted at University of Texas: https://la.utexas.edu/users/jmciver/357L/BrambleBush.PDF — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Llewellyn distinguishes two ways later lawyers/courts can read what an earlier case decided:
  - The **strict view** treats a precedent as authority only for the narrowest ground that was actually necessary to the result, and licenses later courts to "narrow the picture of what was actually before the court," in the extreme "confining the case to its particular facts." — **[VERIFIED — fetched (paraphrase quoting fragments)]**
  - The **loose view** treats a precedent as authority for whatever broader proposition the deciding court "chose to rest [the] case" on, or chose to pass on "after due argument" — including language stated more broadly than the facts strictly required, applied later "wholly without reference to the facts of the case which called the language forth." — **[VERIFIED — fetched (paraphrase quoting fragments)]**
  - Llewellyn's key realist point (as summarized from the fetched excerpt) is that *the same precedent simultaneously carries a maximum and a minimum authoritative content* — a narrowest holding and a broadest dictum-inclusive statement — and which one a later court invokes is a matter of argument and institutional skill, not something the precedent fixes on its own. This is the same phenomenon Lamond's SEP entry calls the levels-of-generality problem and that *Michael H.* dramatizes at the Supreme Court level, stated fifty-plus years earlier from an avowedly realist, sociologically-minded posture (law as what courts actually do, not as a self-executing logical system).

Together, Holmes and Llewellyn supply the strand's realist counterweight to Levi/Sunstein/Brewer's more rationalist accounts: legal analogical reasoning is not just an epistemic procedure for finding the right extension, it is a rhetorical and institutional practice within which skilled actors argue for the level of generality (Llewellyn's strict/loose) and the underlying "felt necessities" (Holmes) that favor their side — a point the caution section (§6) develops further with Schauer's institutional-constraint argument and the legal-realist "post-hoc rationalization" hypothesis.

## 2. The Cognitive Science of Legal Analogy

### 2.1 Holyoak & Thagard — multiconstraint theory, and its bearing on law

- Keith J. Holyoak & Paul Thagard, *Mental Leaps: Analogy in Creative Thought* (MIT Press, 1995). Publisher page: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262581448/mental-leaps/ — **[VERIFIED — fetched, publisher metadata only]**

Holyoak & Thagard's book is the standard cognitive-science reference for the "multiconstraint theory" of analogy: an analogical mapping between a source and target is judged good to the extent it jointly satisfies three pressures — **structural consistency** (a coherent, one-to-one correspondence between source and target elements, preserving relational structure rather than matching isolated surface features), **semantic similarity** (mapped elements should themselves be similar or related in meaning), and **pragmatic centrality** (the mapping should serve the reasoner's actual goals — highlighting the relations that matter for the inference being drawn, not just any true correspondence). This session was not able to independently fetch and quote the book's own worked legal examples (the publisher page returned only cover copy, not chapter text), so the claim that *Mental Leaps* contains a dedicated discussion of legal case reasoning is flagged **[UNVERIFIED]** pending the author checking a library copy or Google Books preview at https://books.google.com/books/about/Mental_Leaps.html?id=8ZRHYv59154C. The multiconstraint framework itself, however, is independently well-documented in Holyoak & Thagard's earlier peer-reviewed work, e.g. Holyoak & Thagard, "A Computational Model of Analogical Problem Solving," and is safe to cite as the cognitive-science baseline against which the legal-theory accounts in §1 can be read: Levi's "similarity is seen" is a structural/semantic mapping judgment; Brewer's "confirmation" step is a pragmatic-centrality check (does the inferred rule serve the point of deciding the doubtful case correctly); and Sunstein's "focus on particulars" is, in this vocabulary, a preference for structural/semantic fit over top-down pragmatic imposition of a pre-chosen theory.

### 2.2 Dedre Gentner — structure-mapping theory, and its (indirect) legal uptake

- Dedre Gentner, "Structure-Mapping: A Theoretical Framework for Analogy," *Cognitive Science* 7(2), 155–170 (1983), PDF at https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.803/pdf/gentner.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched, confirmed as a live, accessible copy of the canonical citation; full content not separately re-extracted since the theory is uncontroversial and widely restated]**

Gentner's structure-mapping theory holds that analogical comparison privileges **relational structure over surface attributes**: a good analogy carries over systems of connected, often higher-order relations (relations between relations) rather than isolated features, and the "structural consistency" (parallel connectivity, one-to-one mapping) and "systematicity" (preference for mappings embedded in larger coherent relational systems) principles predict which correspondences people find compelling. This is directly relevant to legal reasoning's own folk vocabulary: a "merely factual" or "surface" resemblance between two cases (e.g., both involve a bottle) is exactly what Gentner's theory predicts should *not* by itself drive a strong analogy, whereas a shared *relational* structure (e.g., "a manufacturer's failure to inspect created an unreasonable risk to a consumer who could not inspect for themselves") is what should drive it — which is precisely the distinction *Donoghue v. Stevenson*-style precedent doctrine is trying to capture when Lamond's SEP entry (§1.7 above) asks which "level of generality" characterizes "the vehicle of harm." **A direct, explicit application of Gentner's formal structure-mapping engine (SME) to a legal corpus was not located and verified in this session; the connection drawn here is the author's/this memo's inference from independently verified sources (Gentner 1983's content; Lamond's levels-of-generality discussion) rather than a claim that a cited legal scholar made this exact link — flagged accordingly. [UNVERIFIED as a claim about legal-scholarship uptake; VERIFIED as a description of Gentner 1983 itself.]**

### 2.3 Schauer & Spellman — legal expertise is not general analogical skill

- Frederick Schauer & Barbara A. Spellman, "Analogy, Expertise, and Experience," 84 *University of Chicago Law Review* 249 (2017), PDF at https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/12%20SchauerSpellman_SYMP_IC.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

This is the most directly relevant cognitive-science-meets-doctrine source found for the assignment, and it is co-authored by Schauer himself, tying §1.3 and this section together. Its central, counterintuitive empirical/theoretical claim: legal experts are *not* better than novices at domain-general analogical reasoning as such. As the authors put it, "there is in fact no evidence that analogical reasoning, as a domain-independent skill, can be the subject of expertise," and, more pointedly, "there are no experts in analogical reasoning" but "there are experts in law" (pp. 249–250). — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** What legal training actually confers, on their account, is not a sharper general-purpose analogy engine but a different, richer *stock of source cases and categories* that becomes available for retrieval and mapping: "it is not that legal experts are...more adept at analogical reasoning. It is that legal experts are...more likely to see connections of a certain type" (p. 265). — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** They give a concrete example: the "figurative opacity" of an automobile's mechanical workings — the reason it counts, post-*MacPherson*/*Donoghue*, as the kind of product whose latent defects a manufacturer must guard against — "becomes salient to tort experts via [the underlying] principles, though invisible to laypeople" (p. 264). — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** They also give a working definition of mapping itself, p. 254: "The move from the source to the target is technically known as mapping." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** And they state directly, p. 266, that "[p]rinciples are necessary preconditions to analogizing, but...people who draw analogies make the mental move directly from particular to particular" — a psychologically precise version of Levi's and Sunstein's "focus on particulars" claim. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

For Extension Training this is an important, slightly deflationary finding to sit alongside the jurisprudential material: it suggests that what should be trained is not a generic "find the analogy" capacity but a *domain-conditioned salience model* — the specific stock of principles, categories and paradigm cases that make certain relational structures pop out as legally (or ethically) relevant in the first place. That is closer to what "conditioning on paradigm cases with reasons" is already trying to do than to an unconditioned analogy module.

### 2.4 Kevin Ashley / HYPO — case-based reasoning operationalized as competing mappings

- Kevin D. Ashley, *Modeling Legal Argument: Reasoning with Cases and Hypotheticals* (MIT Press, 1990); overview at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HYPO_CBR — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

HYPO is the seminal AI-and-law system for case-based legal argument, working in the trade-secrets domain. Cases are represented via **dimensions** — "a hierarchical set of frames whose slots are important facets of the case" (thirty trade-secret cases indexed along thirteen dimensions, each with "prerequisites," "focal slots," and "range" information showing how factual variation strengthens or weakens a side's position). — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** HYPO's signature move is generating a **three-ply argument**: "it makes a point for one side, drawing the analogy between the problem and the precedent, responds with an argument for the opponent side, endeavoring to differentiate the cited case and citing other cases as counterarguments. Then it makes a final rebuttal, attempting to differentiate the counterarguments." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** It also generates hypotheticals specifically "to redefine old situations in terms of new dimensions, to create new standard cases when an appropriate one doesn't exist, to explore and test the limits of a concept." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** This is essentially a formalization, thirty-five years early, of exactly the adversarial two-mapping structure the assignment's synthesis section is meant to draw out: for any pair (source case, target case), there is always a same-side "follow" mapping and an opposing "distinguish" mapping available, and a well-formed legal argument is not one mapping but the structured contest between (at least) two of them plus rebuttal.

### 2.5 Empirical novice/expert studies

- Fleurie Nievelstein, Tamara van Gog, Henny P. A. Boshuizen, et al., "Effects of Conceptual Knowledge and Availability of Information Sources on Law Students' Legal Reasoning," *Instructional Science* 38, 23–35 (2010), https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-008-9076-3, fetched via https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-008-9076-3 — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Key finding, quoted: "reasoning about [law] cases is a very complex skill" that depends on prior conceptual knowledge; in a controlled comparison, "availability of the lawbook positively affected performance only for advanced students but not for novice students." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** Novices given access to the same reference material as advanced students did not benefit from it, because — consistent with Schauer & Spellman's account in §2.3 — they lacked the conceptual scaffolding needed to know *which* provisions or prior cases were even candidates for the current problem. A second, closely related paper by an overlapping author group, Nievelstein et al., "Instructional support for novice law students: Reducing search processes and explaining concepts in cases," *Applied Cognitive Psychology* (2011) — this session located the citation via search but could not retrieve the abstract (403 error from the publisher) — **[UNVERIFIED, citation only]** — appears from its title to extend the same finding into an instructional-design intervention (explicitly explaining legal concepts embedded in cases to reduce novices' unproductive search). The general pattern across both the cognitive-psychology literature (§2.3, §2.5) and the jurisprudential literature (§1) converges on the same point from two directions: what looks like a single "analogical reasoning" skill in law is largely a *conceptual-knowledge-gated retrieval and salience-detection* skill, not a separable, transferable inference engine — which is the strongest single argument in this strand for why Extension Training's paradigm-case conditioning (giving the model a rich, structured stock of exemplars with reasons) is doing real cognitive work, rather than being a dispensable scaffold around a generic analogy capability the model already has.

## 3. The Anatomy of an Appellate Opinion and of the Adversarial Process

### 3.1 The standard parts of an opinion

- Justia, "Reading a Supreme Court Decision" (guide), https://supreme.justia.com/reading-supreme-court-decision — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

The fetched guide confirms the standard anatomy: **syllabus** (non-binding summary), **majority opinion** ("the official opinion of the Court in the case... states the outcome... and explains how the Court reached that outcome"), **plurality opinion** (when fewer than five Justices join one rationale — governed by the *Marks* rule, under which "the holding of the Court may be viewed as that position taken by those members who concurred in the judgments on the narrowest grounds"), **concurring opinion** ("agrees with the outcome... but not the reasoning... explains the alternative grounds"), and **dissenting opinion** ("disagrees with the outcome... explains why the reasoning of the majority is flawed and why the Court should have reached the opposite result"). — all **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Facts, procedural posture, and question presented are not separately labeled sections in a published opinion the way syllabus/majority/dissent are; they are conventionally located inside the majority opinion's opening part(s) — this structural point is not separately sourced beyond ordinary familiarity with published opinions and is flagged **[UNVERIFIED]** as a claim requiring no further citation, since it is simply how opinions are laid out rather than a contested doctrinal claim.

### 3.2 The syllabus — and its own disclaimer

- U.S. Supreme Court, "Definitions" (Opinions page), https://www.supremecourt.gov/OPINIONS/definitions.aspx — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Verbatim: "The syllabus has been prepared by the Reporter of Decisions for the convenience of the reader and constitutes no part of the opinion of the Court." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- This disclaimer traces to *United States v. Detroit Timber & Lumber Co.*, 200 U.S. 321, 337 (1906): "The statement of the case may be a useful reference to counsel wishing to know quickly the scope of the decision. But the headnote is not the work of the court, nor does it state its decision... It is simply the work of the reporter, gives his understanding of the decision, and is prepared for the convenience of the profession." Confirmed via Wikipedia's article on the case, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Detroit_Timber_%26_Lumber_Co. — **[VERIFIED — fetched, but the exact wording above is Wikipedia's summary/paraphrase of the holding rather than a directly reproduced block quotation from the 1906 opinion; the modern syllabus boilerplate itself was independently confirmed on supremecourt.gov]** This is a strikingly direct legal-system analogue of a distinction the project already needs: the "syllabus" (a compressed, convenient restatement of what a case holds) is institutionally *not authoritative* — only the reasoning of the opinion itself is. An extension policy that trains on paradigm-case *summaries* rather than full reasoning risks reproducing exactly the failure mode the Court itself has warned against for over a century: treating the headnote as the holding.

### 3.3 Merits briefs as competing salience arguments

- Georgetown University Law Center, Guide to "Writing a Statement of Facts in an Appellate Brief," https://www.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/StatementofFactsinaBriefFinal.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

This is a direct, practitioner-facing statement of how the adversarial process turns "which facts are salient" into an explicit argument rather than a neutral given. Key quotes: "The goal of the Statement of Facts is to be persuasive without being extreme" — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**; advocates are told not to "utilize overly dramatic language" because "such language damages your credibility," and instead to "gently lead a reader to your desired conclusion" through the *selection* of specific, accurate, favorable details rather than through explicit characterization — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. The guide's illustration of word-choice framing (describing park access as "people were allowed to enter the park, unless..." to make entry read as the default) shows how even syntactic choices about what to present as background versus exception can shift perceived salience without any factual misstatement — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. And: "The Statement of Facts is most persuasive when it reads like a story," with strategic (not strictly chronological) ordering — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Structurally, then, a merits brief's Statement of Facts is not "the facts" in some neutral sense; it is one side's argument, constrained by truthfulness and professional-responsibility rules, about which facts are salient and how they should be sequenced — and the opposing brief supplies a competing Statement of Facts built from the same record. This is the real-world adversarial analogue of "two competing mappings" that the project's synthesis section draws on: before the parties even get to arguing which precedent controls, they are already arguing about which facts of the *current* case count as the relevantly similar ones.

### 3.4 Oral-argument hypotheticals as perturbation tests

- David R. Gibson, "Hyping the Hypothetical: Talk and Temporality in US Supreme Court Oral Arguments," *Qualitative Sociology* 47(2), 249–279 (2024), https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11133-024-09563-z — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Gibson's sociological analysis of oral-argument transcripts documents exactly the perturbation-test function the assignment asks about: justices use hypotheticals to "explore the consequences of a particular decision for future cases," and, "more tactically, a hypothetical may be used to challenge an advocate's position, by identifying an undesirable consequence." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** In the *Citizens United* exchange Gibson studies in detail, the Court uses a chain of hypotheticals (culminating in an imagined book ban) to press the advocate past the narrow rule his brief actually defended, "set[ting] the stage for converting an as-applied challenge... into a facial one that went beyond it" by forcing successive concessions. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** Mechanically, this is a live, real-time version of exactly the operation Extension Training wants the model to perform on itself: propose a rule (mapping) that would justify the result in the paradigm case, then vary the facts along one dimension at a time and ask whether the proposed rule still delivers a result its proponent is willing to accept. Where the advocate balks — where the hypothetical's answer feels wrong — that identifies either (a) the *true* boundary of the rule the advocate should have proposed, or (b) a defect in the rule itself. HYPO's dimension-varying hypothetical generator (§2.4 above) is the AI-and-law field's formal analogue of the same move.

### 3.5 How a holding's level of generality gets narrowed or broadened later

This restates and cross-references material already independently verified in §1: Lamond's SEP entry (§1.7, the *Donoghue* "vehicle of harm" discussion) and Llewellyn's strict/loose distinction (§1.10) both describe the same phenomenon from different angles — a holding is not self-interpreting, and later courts (through distinguishing, restatement, or extension) actively choose the level of generality at which they will read it. No new sourcing is needed here beyond §1.7 and §1.10; the point is flagged again in this section because it is also, institutionally, a feature of *how opinions get read* by later courts and citators (§3.6), not just a feature of precedent theory in the abstract.

### 3.6 Citator treatment vocabulary

- Wikipedia, "Citator," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citator — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- Florida Coastal / library-guide summary of Shepard's/KeyCite signal categories, https://fclawlib.libguides.com/ohiosecondary/signals — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Modern citators (Shepard's Citations, Westlaw KeyCite) attach a controlled vocabulary of editorial treatment phrases to every case that cites an earlier one, so a researcher can tell at a glance how the law around a precedent has moved. The fetched library guide confirms the phrase-set and its rough valence grouping: **negative** signals include cases "not good law for at least one of its points (overruled or reversed)"; **questioned** signals mean "[c]iting opinions question continuing validity or precedential value of case because of intervening circumstances"; **caution/yellow** signals include "some negative history (limited, criticized)"; and **positive** signals include "affirmed, followed." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** The broader editorial phrase-set listed on the same page includes "followed, criticized, distinguished, harmonized, explained, affirmed in part and reversed in part, abrogated" — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**, though the guide itself does not define each term one-by-one (flagged: individual glosses below are standard legal-research usage, not verbatim-quoted from a single fetched source, and should be treated as **[UNVERIFIED]** for exact wording even though the phenomenon and phrase-list are verified):
- *Followed* — a later court applied the precedent's rule to reach a like result on materially similar facts (the "follow" verdict in Extension Training's own vocabulary).
- *Distinguished* — a later court held the precedent's rule did not control because a fact the later court treats as material was different (the "distinguish" verdict).
- *Limited* — a later court narrowed the scope/level of generality at which the precedent will be read going forward, without overruling it (Llewellyn's "strict view," applied prospectively by a different court).
- *Questioned* — a later court expressed doubt about the precedent's continued soundness without formally overruling it, often flagging tension with intervening authority.
- *Overruled* — a court with the power to do so (typically the same court, or a superior court) has withdrawn the precedent's authority entirely; it is no longer valid law even for its original facts.

This vocabulary is a ready-made, real-world taxonomy of exactly the verdict space Extension Training's mapping-and-judge step needs (follow / distinguish / no precedent, plus the diachronic categories limited/questioned/overruled that describe how a precedent's authority can *change* after the fact) — and it is worth noting for the project that legal citators track this at the level of *individual points of law within a case*, not the case as an undifferentiated whole, i.e., a single precedent can be simultaneously "followed" on one holding and "limited" or "distinguished" on another.

## 4. Legal Pedagogy as Training of the Extension Skill

### 4.1 Langdell and the case method

- Russell L. Weaver, "Langdell's Legacy: Living with the Case Method," 36 *Villanova Law Review* 517 (1991), https://digitalcommons.law.villanova.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?params=%2Fcontext%2Fvlr%2Farticle%2F2739%2F&path_info=17_36VillLRev517_1991_.pdf — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

Christopher Columbus Langdell, as Dean of Harvard Law School starting in 1870, replaced treatise-based lecturing with a method built on three elements, per the fetched article: **casebooks** (curated compilations of appellate opinions, so students work from primary sources rather than a professor's or treatise-writer's summary of the law); **student-driven analysis** (students, not the professor, are responsible for extracting the operative rule from the raw opinion); and **Socratic dialogue** (the professor questions students about facts, holding, reasoning, and consistency with other decisions rather than delivering conclusions). — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** Weaver quotes Langdell's own rationale for treating law as a "science" amenable to this method: "Law, considered as a science, consists of certain principles or doctrines... is what constitutes a true lawyer; and hence to acquire that mastery should be the business of every earnest student." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** The fetched excerpt of this particular article did not itself discuss the "notes and questions" apparatus or hypothetical-generation that fills modern casebooks between cases — that later editorial layer is a distinct, later addition to the Langdellian format and is described in §4.2 below via a different, general source rather than this one; flagged so the two are not conflated.

### 4.2 The modern casebook format: paradigm cases + notes and questions + hypotheticals

- Harvard Law School, "The Case Study Teaching Method," https://casestudies.law.harvard.edu/the-case-study-teaching-method/ — **[VERIFIED — fetched, page content confirms general description below]**

The modern law-school casebook descends from Langdell's format but adds an editorial layer he did not originate: each principal (paradigm) case is typically followed by "notes and questions" — short, non-case prose that (a) supplies the immediate procedural aftermath or subsequent history of the case just read, (b) poses questions asking the student to restate the holding at different levels of generality, and (c) offers hypothetical variations on the facts ("suppose the defendant had instead...") designed to probe exactly where the rule the case seems to state would break down or need qualification. This is the textbook analogue of the oral-argument hypothetical (§3.4) and of HYPO's hypothetical-generation (§2.4): a deliberate, repeated perturbation of the paradigm case's facts, used pedagogically to teach students where a candidate rule's boundary actually lies rather than to state the boundary for them. The Socratic method itself functions as a live, real-time version of the same perturbation exercise — a professor's cold-call questioning is, in form, a sequence of "what if the facts were X" moves directed at whatever rule a student has just proposed, structurally identical to what Gibson (§3.4) documents justices doing to advocates.

### 4.3 IRAC / CREAC as the mapping template

- Wikipedia, "IRAC," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRAC — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**
- Columbia Law School Writing Center, "Organizing a Legal Discussion: IRAC / CRAC / CREAC," https://www.law.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2022-06/WC%20Handout%20IRAC,%20CRAC,%20CREAC.revised%205.22.pdf — located via search, not independently fetched in this session — **[UNVERIFIED, citation only]**

Per the fetched Wikipedia entry, IRAC (**Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion**) is "a methodology for legal analysis" used pervasively in law-school exam answers, bar-exam essays, and legal memoranda: **Issue** identifies the legal question the facts raise; **Rule** states the governing rule(s), typically synthesized from statute and/or the relevant paradigm cases; **Application** — the step doing the actual mapping work — applies the stated rule to the specific facts at hand, which in practice always means arguing which facts of the new situation correspond to which elements of the rule and why; **Conclusion** answers the question. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** CREAC (**Conclusion, Rule, Explanation, Application, Conclusion**) is described as a refinement that front-loads the conclusion, and inserts an explicit **Explanation** step between stating the rule and applying it — typically explanation *of the rule via the paradigm cases that generated it* (why the rule is what it is, illustrated through how it operated in the precedent), which is then followed by Application to the new facts. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** Structurally, IRAC/CREAC's Rule(+Explanation)/Application pairing is a direct pedagogical analogue of Extension Training's own mapping template: state the candidate rule/verdict a paradigm case stands for (with the reasons that justify it — the "Explanation" step), then explicitly map that rule onto the new facts rather than asserting the conclusion. The Wikipedia entry also notes competing formats exist — MIRAT, ILAC, CRuPAC — reflecting the same underlying disagreement legal pedagogy has always had about how rigidly to formalize this template, without disputing that some Rule→Application mapping step is the load-bearing part of the exercise. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

## 5. Circuit Splits and Their Resolution

- SCOTUSblog, "The art of the circuit split: an explainer" (2026), https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/02/the-art-of-the-circuit-split-an-explainer/ — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

A circuit split is, per the fetched explainer, "a disagreement between the federal courts of appeals... on the legal issue on which they are asking the [Supreme] court to weigh in" — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** — concretely, two or more of the thirteen federal circuit courts have read the same statute, constitutional provision, or line of Supreme Court precedent and reached incompatible rules, so that "a law or legal principle applies differently throughout the country" depending on which circuit a case arises in. — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** Circuit splits matter procedurally because they are, per the article, "among the top factors that the court considers when determining whether to grant review," precisely because the Supreme Court hears only a small number of cases each year (historically on the order of 60–80) and a single decision resolving a split "does more than bring clarity to a single case; it can clear up confusion that persists nationwide." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**

The fetched explainer did not itself elaborate a doctrine of "reconciling" or "harmonizing" conflicting lines of precedent as a distinct analytical move — flagged **[UNVERIFIED]** as a gap in this particular source, not as a claim this memo makes and fails to support. What can be said without further sourcing, as a structural description rather than a quoted claim: when the Supreme Court resolves a split, it does not usually simply pick one circuit's answer and discard the other's reasoning wholesale. The more common pattern — visible across many resolved splits, though not independently re-verified case-by-case here — is that the Court articulates a rule that (a) is consistent with the *result* in as many of the conflicting circuit decisions as possible (treating them as correctly decided on their own facts even where their stated rules conflicted), while (b) explaining why the circuits' stated *rules* diverged — typically because each circuit, reasoning by analogy from a different anchor precedent or a different aspect of the same anchor precedent, generalized its holding at a different level (exactly the levels-of-generality problem of §1.7 and §1.9). "Reconciling the lines" in this sense is not accepting one line and rejecting the other but restating a single rule at the level of generality that both lines' *results* turn out to instantiate as special cases — structurally the same move as harmonizing two locally correct extensions that were pitched at different scopes, rather than treating one as simply an error. This is flagged as the memo's own structural characterization, offered for its evident fit with the levels-of-generality material already verified in §§1.7, 1.9, 3.5, rather than as a quotation from any single fetched source.

## 6. Two Cautions

### 6.1 Legal analogy's force is partly institutional, not (only) a function of the analogy's quality

This restates and extends material already verified above rather than introducing new primary sources. Schauer's core thesis (§1.3) is precisely that precedent *constrains* — has normative force on a later decision-maker — largely because of the institutional structure surrounding it (hierarchy, a rule requiring lower courts to follow higher courts' holdings, and a general practice of stare decisis that makes departure costly) rather than because each later court has independently verified that the earlier court's reasoning was analogically sound. The Schauer 2011 abstract (§1.3, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**) frames this directly as one of the piece's organizing questions: "the normative and institutional design questions of when a system of precedential constraint is desirable and when it is not" is treated as a separate question from whether any *particular* precedent's analogy to a new case is a good one. Lamond's SEP entry supplies an independent, convergent data point (§1.7, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**): citing Sherwin (1999), it identifies *institutional* benefits of precedential/analogical practice — "exposing judges to a wider variety of fact situations than the particular set before them, making them consider the views of other judges in previous cases and exerting a conservative pressure on individual decision-makers" — that hold regardless of whether any single analogy drawn under the practice is epistemically the best one available. In other words: the practice can be justified, and can bind, on grounds that are orthogonal to the local quality of the mapping — finality, predictability, the discipline of having to justify a decision by reference to something outside oneself, and hierarchical authority (a Court of Appeals panel must follow *this* circuit's precedent even if a panel member privately thinks a sister circuit's contrary rule is analogically better reasoned). This is the caution the assignment specifically flags, and it is corroborated (not merely asserted) by two independently fetched sources describing precedent's force in these institutional terms rather than purely in terms of analogical soundness.

### 6.2 Opinions may be post-hoc rationalizations — legal realism and empirical judicial-decision-making research

- Oliver Wendell Holmes, "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience" (§1.10 above, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**) and Llewellyn's strict/loose distinction (§1.10, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**) already supply the classical realist half of this caution: a written opinion's chain of "reasoning by example" may be constructed *after* a result has been reached on other grounds (Holmes's "felt necessities of the time... intuitions of public policy, avowed or unconscious, even the prejudices which judges share with their fellow-men"), with the mapping to precedent serving as the opinion's public justification rather than as the actual causal process that produced the decision.

- Shai Danziger, Jonathan Levav & Liora Avnaim-Pesso, "Extraneous Factors in Judicial Decisions," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 108(17), 6889–6892 (2011), https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1018033108 — **[VERIFIED — fetched]**. Abstract/finding, as fetched: the study examined 1,112 parole decisions by eight experienced Israeli judges over ten months and found that "the percentage of favorable rulings drops gradually from ≈65% to nearly zero within each decision session and returns abruptly to ≈65% after a break," a pattern the authors attribute to decision fatigue, with the paper's framing explicitly pitched as evidence "support[ing] legal realism over formalism." — **[VERIFIED — fetched]** This is the strongest empirical version of the realist caution: whatever the parole board's written rationale for each denial, an extraneous, session-timing variable predicted outcomes with far more power than any principled account of the underlying legal/factual merits plausibly could.

- Critiques and reanalyses, all confirmed via a fetched Wikipedia summary (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungry_judge_effect, **[VERIFIED — fetched]**) that itself cites the primary critique sources:
  - Keren Weinshall-Margel & John Shapard, "Overlooked Factors in the Analysis of Parole Decisions," *PNAS* 108(42), E833–E834 (2011) — argues the case *ordering* is confounded, not random: unrepresented prisoners tend to be scheduled last in a session and represented prisoners' order is influenced by strategic/administrative factors, so what looks like a hunger effect may substantially reflect "the order of case presentation" rather than the judges' physiological state. — **[VERIFIED — fetched (via Wikipedia summary)]**
  - Andreas Glöckner, "The Irrational Hungry Judge Effect Revisited: Simulations Reveal That the Magnitude of the Effect Is Overestimated," *Judgment and Decision Making* 11(6), 601–610 (2016), also independently locatable at https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/irrational-hungry-judge-effect-revisited-simulations-reveal-that-the-magnitude-of-the-effect-is-overestimated/61CE825D4DC137675BB9CAD04571AE58 — simulation work suggesting scheduling priorities (harder, more time-consuming cases being pushed to the end of a session, before a break) can generate much of the observed pattern, and that "the magnitude of the effect is overestimated" — **[VERIFIED — fetched (via Wikipedia summary); title itself independently confirms the finding]**
  - Daniel Lakens (2017, as reported in the same Wikipedia summary) argued the original effect size is implausibly large on statistical grounds. — **[VERIFIED — fetched (via Wikipedia summary); primary Lakens source not independently located]**
  - The original authors, Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso, "Reply to Weinshall-Margel and Shapard: Extraneous Factors in Judicial Decisions Persist," *PNAS* 108(42) (2011), https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112190108 — located via search but not independently fetched and quoted in this session — **[UNVERIFIED, citation only]** — defended the original finding against the ordering-confound critique.

The net methodological lesson for this strand, stated carefully rather than overclaimed: the *direction* of the realist/post-hoc-rationalization caution is well supported by both the classical jurisprudential literature (Holmes, Llewellyn) and at least one high-profile empirical study: legal reasoning-by-analogy as written in an opinion is not guaranteed to be the actual causal process by which the decision was reached, and institutional/situational factors external to the merits can move outcomes. But the *specific* Danziger et al. finding is itself contested on methodological grounds by researchers working within the same empirical tradition — so it should be cited in training material as "a widely-discussed and non-trivially disputed data point for the realist thesis," not as a settled demonstration that legal reasoning is generally epiphenomenal. This nuance (real effect, disputed magnitude/mechanism) is itself a useful model for how Extension Training's own judge should treat contested empirical claims about analogical/mapping quality: cite the finding, but represent its contestedness rather than its take-away as flatly settled.

## 7. Synthesis — What Transfers to Extension Training, and Where Law Meets Quan

**Portable.** The core *operation* the legal paradigm teaches is exactly the one Extension Training already targets: articulate the facts of the new situation, identify which features of the paradigm case are salient (not all its features — Gentner's structural/semantic criteria and Schauer & Spellman's finding that expertise is domain-specific salience-detection rather than generic analogical skill both point the same way, §§2.2–2.3), construct a mapping that carries a stated *reason* (Levi's "rule announced," Brewer's abduction step, §§1.1, 1.5) from source to target, and either follow or distinguish. Three finer-grained structural imports are worth building into the training corpus specifically: (1) **the adversarial two-mapping structure** — law never produces a single mapping in isolation; a Statement of Facts and a merits brief on each side (§3.3), and HYPO's three-ply for/against/rebuttal architecture (§2.4), show that a mapping's quality is best judged against its best available *competitor*, not in a vacuum, which argues for training the judge on paired competing mappings rather than single ones scored in isolation. (2) **The hypothetical as a perturbation test** — oral argument (§3.4) and the casebook's notes-and-questions (§4.2) both use "what if the facts were X" to find a proposed rule's true boundary; this is a cheap, generative way to stress-test a candidate mapping's stated scope before trusting it. (3) **The holding's level of generality as an exemplar's declared scope** — Lamond's SEP entry, Llewellyn's strict/loose distinction, and Scalia's footnote 6 in *Michael H.* (§§1.7, 1.9, 1.10) all converge on the same lesson: a paradigm case never comes with its correct level of generality attached, and pretending it does (rather than making the mapping step argue for a level of generality) hides exactly the judgment call that most determines whether an extension is sound.

**Not portable, or portable only with translation.** Several sources of legal analogy's *force* are institution-specific and should not be imported into a general reasoning policy: (a) **finality and hierarchy-as-authority** (§6.1) — a circuit panel follows its own circuit's precedent even over a better-reasoned sister-circuit rule, and a lower court follows the Supreme Court even where the mapping is contestable, because the system needs one settled answer, not because the mapping is thereby shown correct. An AI's extension policy has no comparable need for one binding answer across users/contexts, and treating "this was how a similar-looking precedent case was decided" as authoritative in the way stare decisis is would import a spurious source of certainty. (b) **Outcome-as-truth** — a court's holding is treated as correctly decided by definition within its hierarchy (subject only to appeal/overruling by a superior authority), which is a legal-institutional fact about who gets the last word, not a truth-tracking property of the reasoning; the realist caution (§6.2) is precisely the reminder that a well-mapped-sounding opinion can still be a rationalization, so the training signal should reward mapping *validity* (does the stated relation actually carry the reasons over) rather than reward matching a labeled "correct" verdict the way one would defer to a binding holding.

**Where quan and "reconciling the lines" meet.** Confucian *quan* (weighing/discretion that departs from the fixed rule *li* when circumstances demand, while remaining answerable to the deeper end the rule serves) and the common-law practice of resolving a circuit split by restating a rule at the level of generality that both conflicting lines' *results* instantiate (§5) are structurally the same move: both refuse the binary of "keep the old rule exactly" or "discard it," and instead ask what deeper, more general principle both the rule-as-stated and the seemingly deviant case are actually serving, then restate *that*. Both treat an apparent conflict between a precedent/norm and a hard case not as proof one side is simply wrong, but as evidence the rule was previously stated at the wrong level of generality — exactly the diagnostic this strand keeps returning to.
