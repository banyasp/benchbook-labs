                     Artificial Intelligence Imperative in Modern Warfare
The Army stands at a critical turning point. Much like gunpowder and aviation redefined conflict
in past eras, artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transitioning from a futuristic concept into a
present-day tool that is actively reshaping how the U.S. Army plans, prepares, executes, and
assesses military operations. For corps and divisions, AI offers a significant opportunity to
accelerate decision making, integrate complex actions with greater precision, and enhance
sustainment planning and execution.
However, competitors are reshaping the strategic landscape. They view AI not merely as an
efficiency tool but as the foundation of future military power. Foremost among these competitors
is China, the United States’ pacing threat. China’s “Next Generation Artificial Intelligence
Development Plan,” announced in 2017, clearly declares its intent to become the world’s leading
AI power by 2030.2 This strategy, characterized by a state-led civil-military fusion approach
enables rapid development cycles without the ethical and legal constraints that challenge the U.S.
Army.
The People’s Liberation Army (PLA) aggressively operationalizes this national strategy,
embedding AI into every facet of its warfighting doctrine. The PLA’s stated goal is to achieve a

decisive advantage in what the PLA terms “intelligentized warfare,”3 where victory goes to the

and “intelligentized” missile guidance to predictive logistics and AI-driven command systems,
the PLA builds a force designed for a new era of warfare.
Given that the PLA’s progress remains difficult to assess because of secrecy and the PLA’s
multi-year head start, the U.S. Army must strategically assume that it is, at best, at parity in the
AI race. This necessary strategic assumption guards against technological surprise and hopefully
encourages a bias for action. Falling behind in the development and integration of AI risks
ceding the initiative on the future battlefield. Consequently, this publication provides a roadmap
for corps and division commanders and their staff. It offers a structured implementation
framework, actionable best practices, and a clear examination of the challenges ahead. However,
given the rapid pace of AI advancement, the best practices and challenges outlined here cannot
be exhaustive as corps and divisions will inevitably learn, discover, and add to this foundation
through their own implementation experiences. The goal is to equip commanders with the
knowledge required to overcome integration hurdles, harness AI’s full potential, and ensure
corps and divisions secure a decisive strategic advantage in an increasingly AI-enabled world.
Principled Framework for AI Integration
To move from ambition to reality, units must adopt a structured, repeatable, and doctrinally
familiar approach to AI integration. Relying on episodic individual Soldier or staff efforts
and ad-hoc experimentation, while valuable for learning, remains insufficient for enterprise-level
transformation that requires institutional commitment and standardized processes. The Defense
Data Orchestration Framework (DDOF) (Figure 1.) provides a clear six-step roadmap that is
nearly analogous to the military decision-making process (MDMP). Just as skipping a step in
MDMP could have negative second and third order affects in subsequent steps, corps and
divisions should follow each DDOF step in the prescribed order. For additional details, see the
How-to-guide: Implementing the Defense Data Orchestration Framework, Annex A in this
publication.

Step 1: Problem framing. Problem framing serves as the first and most critical step in the
DDOF Framework. Before gathering any data or developing AI prompts, teams must clearly and
precisely define the mission-critical objectives and operational questions that AI will address. A
poorly framed problem, such as “improve sustainment,” will lead to vague and unhelpful outputs
from AI, or any software development and thus negatively impact trust in AI. A well-framed
problem, such as “provide recommendations to reduce the incidence of critical equipment failure
for our forward-deployed armored brigade combat team by 25 percent by developing a predictive
maintenance model,” is specific, measurable, and directly tied to an operational outcome.
Effective problem framing requires collaboration between commanders who understand and can
clearly articulate the mission and data professionals who know the art of the possible.
Step 2: Data provisioning. Data provisioning is a significant challenge in the Army, where data
is often segregated into discrete silos. Separate systems manage personnel, logistics, intelligence,
operations, and other functions. This step involves creating detailed data inventory, establishing
pathways to access the data, and ensuring the data’s relevance and accuracy. The G-6 and the
unit’s data team play crucial roles in navigating the technical hurdles of data access and
integration.

Step 3: Data wrangling. Raw data quite often is not ready for analysis. What is called wrangling
is the process of cleaning, transforming, and preparing this raw data. This could mean correcting
misspellings, standardizing location data, or addressing incomplete or corrupted data. This
tedious but critical work ensures data quality, because without it, even the most advanced AI
models will produce flawed outputs, further undermining trust.
Step 4: Development. This step involves building, training, and refining specific data products
and AI models. These can include:
•   Using historical data to forecast future events such as predicting enemy courses of action
    (COAs), forecasting ammunition and fuel consumption rates, or estimating equipment failure
    probabilities.
•   Creating interactive applications that allow staffs to explore data, identify hidden patterns,
    and wargame the what if…scenarios to support decision making.
Step 5: Test and evaluation (T&E). Units must thoroughly test AI systems before fielding
them. The T&E phase validates that the data product meets mission objectives. This involves
both developmental testing (e.g., does the tool work as intended?) and, more importantly,
operational testing (e.g., does the tool help the staff solve the problem during an operation or
exercise?). T&E must involve the staff officers and Soldiers who will use the tool. This is to
ensure the tool is intuitive, effective, and that its outputs are accurate. Metrics for success must
be clearly defined, measuring not only technical accuracy but also the tool's impact on staff
product quality and commander decision speed.
Step 6: Operations and refinement. Deployment is not the end of the process but the beginning
of a continuous cycle of feedback and refinement. As the staff uses the AI tool in their daily
activities, they will discover the tool’s strengths, weaknesses, and other unexpected issues. A
formal feedback mechanism captures these observations and relays them back to the data team.
This enables an iterative process where the data team constantly updates, retrains, and improves
the AI model based on real-world performance. This ensures that AI tools evolve and adapt to
meet the changing demands of the operational environment.
Best Practices for Successful Integration
A framework provides the what, but best practices provide the how. Successful AI integration is
as much a cultural and leadership challenge as it is a technical one. The following best practices
are essential for corps and divisions seeking to harness AI’s full potential.
1. Command-driven leadership and buy in. AI integration will not succeed as a grassroots-
only effort. It requires command emphasis from the top.5 Corps and division commanders must
establish AI integration as a clear command priority, setting the tone for their entire organization.
When AI integration is a true command priority, the necessary allocation of time, personnel, and
resources will naturally follow as will the commander’s personal involvement.

To drive this transformation, commanders must embed an AI-focused dialogue into their battle
rhythm. During planning meetings, commander’s update briefings, and after-action reviews,
commanders can assess and guide progress by asking pointed questions. Examples include:
•   We are receiving reports from multiple intelligence feeds and our organic intelligence,
    surveillance, and reconnaissance assets. How are we integrating this data to provide a single
    comprehensive common operational picture or are we still looking at three different maps?
•   What AI-enabled tools are we currently using to identify adversary patterns, predict their
    most likely course of action, or use to recommend friendly COAs? Are we leveraging the full
    capabilities of these tools?
•   How quickly can we process and present relevant data for time-sensitive decisions such as a
    dynamic targeting opportunity? What are the bottlenecks and how can AI help minimize or
    eliminate them?
•   How are we training the staff to effectively use data analytics and AI tools to support their
    warfighting function? Is this training planned for and scheduled for our daily activities?
A key enabler for this is the assignment of a dedicated AI integration team. Organizations must
source this team internally. The team should include data professionals, operations research
analysts, and motivated and technically proficient Soldiers from various warfighting functions.
These specialists bridge the gap between technical experts and the staff by translating the
commander’s intent into problems that data and AI can address to help staff sections use new
tools and maintain a continuous feedback loop for refinement. Since operational requirements
differ across corps and divisions, commanders should position the team where it best serves their
command’s specific needs.
Finally, leaders must proactively address the natural concern that AI will replace human
judgment or, worse, jobs. Leaders must frame AI as a force multiplier designed to enhance, not
replace, the staffs’ expertise. As LTC Blaire Wilcox noted during an Army Mad Scientists
podcast on hybrid intelligence and sustaining adversary overmatch, “[AI] makes professionals
better. It does not necessarily make amateurs or the inexperienced [into] professionals.”6 By
framing AI as a tool that automates tedious tasks to free up time for critical thinking and
providing deeper insights to inform (but not making) decisions, leaders can build the trust and
confidence essential for adoption.
2. Data readiness and a culture of data management. AI is only as effective as the data on
which it has been trained or is provided. Even the most advanced systems require a solid
foundation of high-quality, authoritative, and curated data. Without it, they will produce outputs
that are flawed, biased, or nonsensical. These faulty outputs, while very believable, will
ultimately destroy user trust. Therefore, establishing rigorous data governance is the non-
negotiable foundation for all AI initiatives. This includes creating a comprehensive data

An ontology is a way to represent knowledge within a specific domain by defining a set of
concepts and categories, along with the relationships between them, helping organize
information so that both humans and computers can understand it more effectively.8 Units should
leverage existing work to save time and effort. MAJ Dustin Berry, data scientist in CAC's
CDAO, defines these existing works, known as Ontology Context in Vantage, as “the semantic
layer that organizes raw data into objects, relationships, and actions allowing users and AI (AIP
Agent/Logic)9 to interact with the data.” According to MAJ Berry, “Army users have established
a curated corpus of ontology object types ranging from operational lessons learned from the
Center for Army Lessons Learned to authoritative doctrine from the Army Publishing
Directorate.” Corps and division staff can use this feature to rapidly pull doctrinal templates or
analyze trends from thousands of historical after-action reports. However, they must exercise
caution and validate that any pre-built ontology is appropriate for their specific task.
Ultimately, this requires fostering a culture of data. Every Soldier must understand that they are
data generators and that the quality of their reports, from a simple spot report to a detailed battle
damage assessment, directly impacts the accuracy and effectiveness of the AI tools.
3. Phased and iterative implementation. A single large-scale approach to AI integration is very
likely to result in poor outcomes. As with any tool, AI is only as effective as the people using it
and building widespread proficiency and trust will take time. Corps and divisions should
implement AI in phases, starting with low-risk applications to build confidence and familiarity.

•   Phase 1: administrative and analytical augmentation. Begin by using AI for time-consuming,
    lower-risk tasks where human review can catch any errors before they impact
    operations. This includes summarizing long reports, transcribing meetings, identifying trends
    in historical data, or drafting routine correspondence. Success in these areas builds
    confidence and frees up the staff’s time for more complex, higher priority work.
•   Phase 2: mission-critical support. As the staff becomes more proficient, expand AI’s use to
    mission-critical functions. This could involve using AI to help develop and wargame
    multiple COAs, recommend priorities based on the commander’s guidance, or predict
    maintenance needs for key combat systems. In this phase, AI serves as an assistant and
    subject matter experts must always validate AI outputs.
•   Phase 3: time-sensitive decision support. In the most advanced phase, AI can support time-
    sensitive decisions in active operations, such as identifying and recommending action for
    dynamic targets. As in phase 2, human validation of AI outputs remains paramount.
This phased approach allows the organization to learn and adapt, builds organizational
confidence, and ensures that the risks associated with AI get managed deliberately. With these
foundational practices in place, corps and divisions can begin applying AI across specific
warfighting functions.
Tactical Applications Across the Warfighting Functions
Organizations realize AI’s true value when it becomes integral to their core processes. For corps
and division staff, this means integrating AI into the warfighting functions:
1. Intelligence. AI can dramatically accelerate the intelligence preparation of the operational
environment (IPOE) process, which underpins all operations:
•   AI can analyze vast datasets of historical intelligence reports, doctrinal templates, and
    observed enemy tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) to build a highly detailed threat
    model, identifying patterns that a human analyst might miss.
•   AI can generate multiple plausible enemy COAs based on enemy doctrine and current
    disposition, and then wargame those COAs against friendly plans to identify potential points
    of friction and opportunities.
Beyond IPOE, AI can identify key personalities and relationships from unstructured text and
perform initial pattern recognition, freeing human analysts to focus on providing essential
context, judgment, and predictive analysis.
2. Movement and maneuver. AI can provide commanders with more options by identifying
approaches that might be missed under manual processes. AI-enabled tools can rapidly process
large datasets to include intelligence reports and logistical factors to highlight patterns,
relationships, and potential opportunities that staff planners might overlook due to time
limitations or information overload. This enables staff to present commanders with more
thoroughly vetted options (though human judgment remains essential for evaluating tactical risk)
for assessing enemy intent and making final decisions.
3. Fires. Commanders can integrate AI into the targeting process of decide, detect, deliver, and
assess (D3A) to accelerate the delivery of effects:

•   Decide: AI can assist with target prioritization by applying the commander's guidance to
    provide rapid and comprehensive recommendations on which targets to engage and in what
    sequence.
•   Detect: AI can quickly sift through hundreds, if not thousands, of reports, to identify
    potential targets.
•   Deliver: The principle of having humans in the loop is non-negotiable for all lethal
    engagement decisions. AI can support weaponeering by analyzing target characteristics,
    available munitions, and desired effects to recommend the optimal weapon-target pairing for
    each engagement.
•   Assess: AI can rapidly analyze battle damage assessments from various sources to determine
    the effect on the target, accelerating the assessment phase, and allowing for rapid re-attack if
    necessary.
4. Sustainment. AI has the potential to transform sustainment into a more predictive process:
•   By analyzing maintenance data and historical failure rates from vehicles and equipment, AI
    algorithms can forecast component failures before they occur. This allows maintainers to
    replace parts proactively, thus improving readiness and reducing unexpected breakdowns.
•   AI can optimize resupply recommendations by analyzing historical consumption rates,
    current operational tempo, and planned future operations. This ensures that the right supplies
    (e.g., Classes I, III, and V) deliver to the right place at the right time, reducing waste, and
    minimizing the risk of logistical shortfalls.
•   For sustainment convoys, AI tools can analyze road conditions, weather, and real-time threat
    intelligence to plot the safest and most efficient routes which improves efficiency and force
    protection.
5. Command and control (C2). AI can rapidly process thousands of text-based reports to
identify critical information requiring the commander’s immediate attention. By consolidating
reports and highlighting key decision points, AI helps commanders consider more options within
available time. This human-machine teaming approach augments commander decision making
and enables operations at an increased tempo.
Ethical and Operational Challenges
Corps and divisions must develop clear policies governing the use of AI in military operations
addressing issues such as data sharing, algorithmic transparency, and ethical use of AI. These
policies must align with Department of War directives while providing specific guidance tailored
to their unique organizational requirements.
1. Accountability and the law of armed conflict. The rise of AI, particularly autonomous
systems, introduces significant ethical dilemmas. Who bears accountability when an AI-enabled
or autonomous system makes a mistake that leads to fratricide or civilian casualties? Department
of Defense Directive 3000.09, “Autonomy in Weapon Systems,” is very clear in this regard. The
directive mandates that all weapon systems must “allow commanders and operators to exercise
appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force.” As such, the staff judge advocate
must help refine AI models to include safeguards that prioritize legal compliance with the
principles of distinction and proportionality. An AI targeting tool could be designed to flag any

potential target near a protected site (e.g., hospitals, religious sites, or schools) and require
multiple levels of human confirmation before the target can be validated and engaged.
2. Algorithmic bias. AI models learn from data, and if that data is biased, the model’s outputs
will be biased. This can have dangerous and unintended consequences. For example, if an
imagery recognition AI used for targeting is trained primarily on images of enemy tanks in desert
environments, the AI may fail to identify tanks accurately in a forest or arctic environment. Or
tanks that have been heavily modified can be hard to identify to counter drone threats, creating a
critical operational vulnerability. Similarly, if the data that analysts use to predict insider threats
contains unintentional demographic biases, the resulting AI could unfairly single out certain
groups of people.
Commanders and staff must deliberately and continuously work to mitigate bias. This involves
using diverse and representative training data, conducting regular audits to actively search for
biases, and maintaining transparency in how the model works so that operators can understand
the model’s potential limitations.
3. Operational risks: over-reliance and skill atrophy. A significant long-term risk is that
commanders and staff will become overly reliant on AI, thus degrading their ability to think
critically through complex problems and make decisions under the pressures of large-scale
combat operations. This over-reliance creates a dangerous de-skilling across the force. Essential
analog competencies (e.g., manual IPOE, map reading without digital aids, hand-calculated
sustainment planning, etc.) will atrophy without regular practice. If AI systems, and other C2
systems, were to fail or be degraded by enemy action, corps and divisions could be faced with
significant risk to force and risk to mission.
Commanders must guard against this by insisting on a culture of critical engagement with AI
outputs. Staff should be trained to critically question AI recommendations, understand the data
and assumptions behind recommendations, and be prepared to operate without AI. Training plans
must incorporate exercises where units must execute their mission using only manual and analog
methods, ensuring the preservation of these critical skills.
Joint and Multinational Dimension
Future conflicts will require the U.S. Army to operate in joint or multinational forces, adding
complexity to AI integration. Corps and divisions must ensure their AI systems work seamlessly
across coalition partners.
This requires a concerted and deliberate effort to develop AI capabilities that can function
effectively in a coalition environment. Key technical and policy challenges must be addressed
and resolved:
•   For allies to share AI-driven insights, they must first be able to share data. This requires
    establishing common data exchange formats and standards.
•   AI tools often need access to vast amounts of data at multiple classification levels.
    Developing advanced cross-domain solutions and classification guards is essential to share
    AI outputs without compromising sensitive sources and methods.

•    Design AI systems with interoperability in mind from the outset and conduct regular joint

•    Nations have different legal and ethical policies regarding AI and autonomy. During
     multinational operations and exercises, clear protocols must be established that define which

                                               Conclusion
Integrating AI into corps and division operations is no longer optional, it is an imperative for
victory in modern warfare. Commanders who embrace the framework and best practices outlined
herein can transform their units into agile intelligent forces capable of outpacing and out
deciding any adversary. Success requires balance by aggressively leveraging AI’s capabilities
while preserving the irreplaceable human judgment, intuition, and ethical leadership that defines
the American military profession. Units that master this synergy will gain decisive advantages in
operational tempo, decision quality, and combat effectiveness.
Ultimately, future conflict will be a contest of speed, not just of weapons systems. It will be a
contest of decisions themselves. Victory will favor those who most rapidly execute Boyd’s
observe, orient, decide, and Act (OODA) loop,10 and AI is the key to accelerating that cycle
beyond what previous generations thought possible. Corps and divisions can seize this decisive
edge and dominate the future battlefield through structured, ethical, and commander-driven
integration.

                              Annex A
 How-To-Guide: Implementing the Defense Data Orchestration Framework
                                             (DDOF)

                                 Last updated: 21 April 2025
                                        Introduction:
This playbook provides detailed “how-to” guidelines for utilizing the Defense
Data Orchestration Framework (DDOF). The DDOF is the operational
mechanism that enables activities to achieve two of the strategic goals in the
Department of Defense (DoD) Data, Analytics, and Artificial Intelligence Adoption
Strategy: 1) advance the data, analytics, and AI ecosystem; and 2) deliver
capabilities for enterprise business and Joint warfighting impact. Initially
conceived at the Army Office of the Chief Data and Analytics Officer (CDAO), the
DDOF was designed to implement the Army Data Management and Analytics
Strategy (ADMAS), transforming its conceptual vision into actionable processes.
This framework ensures the effective creation, deployment, and continuous
refinement of data products aligned with Army doctrine, systems, and mission
requirements. It is a structured, iterative lifecycle designed to deliver mission-
critical insights and enable decision dominance.
Currently, the DDOF approach is being adopted across the Military Departments
(MILDEPS) with several successfully implemented use cases, as shown in Table 1
in Appendix A.
                             Overview of the DDOF Phases:
The DDOF consists of six core phases (See Figure 1 below, and Figure 2 in the
Appendix):
   1. Problem Framing: Defining the problem and identify the analytical solutions required
      to reach desired outcomes.
   2. Data Provisioning: Gathering and organizing the necessary data for solving the
      problem identified in phase 1.
   3. Data Wrangling: Cleansing, transforming, and integrating authoritative data to
      address the problem scope.
   4. Development: Building mission-driven data products (models, dashboards, etc.) in
      approved data platforms (See Table 2 in the Appendix A for DoD approved data
      platforms and their usage).
   5. Test & Evaluation: Validating the accuracy, reliability, and fitness for purpose.
   6. Operations: Integrating data products into Army decision-making processes for operational
      use.

                How is DDOF supporting the DoD Data Strategy?
Data has long been recognized as a strategic asset for the DoD. However,
leveraging and maximizing its full potential requires an effective framework to
support our vision for a data-driven decision-making environment. The DDOF is
one such method, with a proven track record of successful implementation in the
Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, and National Guard. The DDOF
transforms data from its source of origin to its required destination, enabling DoD
senior officers to make accurate, reliable, and informed decisions by providing
the highest quality data products.
   1. Advance the Data, Analytics, and AI Ecosystem: The DDOF advances the data, analytics,
      and AI ecosystem by providing a structured process that ensures data is accurately
      defined, gathered, cleansed, and integrated, resulting in high-quality, reliable data. This
      data is then used to develop mission-driven data products, such as dashboards in
      Common Operating Pictures, which are rigorously tested and validated. By embedding
      these data products into decision-making processes, the DDOF fosters a cohesive and
      robust data ecosystem that supports advanced analytics and AI applications, driving
      innovation and enhancing the DoD's analytical capabilities.
   2. Deliver Capabilities for Enterprise Business and Joint Warfighting Impact: The DDOF
      delivers capabilities for enterprise business and Joint warfighting impact by
      transforming raw data into actionable insights through a systematic, iterative process.
      By clearly defining problems, provisioning and wrangling data, and developing and
      validating data products, the DDOF ensures that high-quality, reliable data products
      are integrated into decision-making processes. This enables DoD senior officers to
      make informed, data-driven decisions that enhance operational efficiency, support
      enterprise business functions, and improve joint warfighting capabilities, ultimately
      driving mission success.
                  The Scope and Extent of DDOF: What DDOF is:
   •   This framework is process-oriented, and its greatest strength lies in enabling an
       iterative process of data product development that accurately aligns with operational
       objectives and mission requirements.
   •   It conforms to data standardization practices highlighted in the Multi-Service Tactics,
       Techniques, and Procedures (MTTP) for Data Orchestration by allowing for the
       incorporation of metadata and ontology, enabling interoperability between disparate
       systems, tools, and networks.
   •   The DDOF is intended to deliver data (often fueled by platforms like VAULTIS) into
       products that satisfy consumer needs on the demand side, while ensuring supply-side
       activities leverage the most out of governance, Enterprise services, and DoD data
       platforms.
   •   The DDOF is designed to ensure data products are not just built but are
       demonstrably valuable and usable within Army operations.

                                    What DDOF is NOT:
   •   The DDOF is not limited by any specific data platform, network, tool, capability, feature, or
       resource.
   •   The DDOF is not designed to be confined by the scope or magnitude of any project
       required to develop a data product.
   •   The DDOF is not a one-time assessment or certification. It’s a continuous process of
       evaluation and improvement throughout the data product lifecycle.
   •   The DDOF is not simply about technical compliance. While adherence to standards is
       important, the ultimate goal is mission impact and usability.
   •   The DDOF is not intended to replace existing data governance structures. Instead, it
       complements and strengthens them by providing a structured framework for
       evaluating data products within those structures.
   •   The DDOF is not a "rip and replace" exercise. It’s designed to work with existing
       systems and data, identifying opportunities for optimization and integration rather
       than wholesale changes.
   •   The DDOF is not solely the responsibility of data scientists or IT professionals.
       Successful implementation requires collaboration across the entire data
       lifecycle – from problem framing to operational use – involving stakeholders
       from various disciplines.

      Implementing DDOF in Practice – Phase-by-Phase Guidelines
1. Problem Framing – Define the mission-critical problem and align data
                   needs with operational objectives
  •   Sub-steps: Understanding the decision, Analytical approach
  •   Tasks:
         ▪     Clearly articulate the operational challenge requiring data-driven insight.
         ▪     Define success criteria: What constitutes a successful solution?
         ▪     The FDMs submit a Problem Framing Intake form on Army Data Catalog (ADC).
         ▪     The C2DAOs review and approve the problem statement and analytical approach in
               ADC.
         ▪     Facilitate the understanding of the decision context, gather relevant
               knowledge, and ensure alignment with organizational objectives.

         ▪     Conduct preliminary data exploration to assess data availability related to the
               problem.
         ▪     Document the problem statement, success criteria, and initial data assessment
               to communicate to the Data Provisioning Team.
         ▪     Propose potential analytical approaches (e.g., spatial analysis, machine
               learning, time series forecasting).

       ▪     Assess the feasibility of each approach based on available data and resources.

•   Deliverables: Approved Problem Statement, Analytical Approach Document,
    Preliminary Data Assessment.

2. Data Provisioning – Gather, validate, and organize data from
                  internal and external sources
•   Sub-steps: Data Requirements, Data Selection
•   Tasks:
       ▪     Verify data source authority and lineage.
       ▪     Ensure data compliance with Army data governance policies (e.g., data security
             classifications, privacy regulations).
       ▪     Identify potential data sources, including academic institutions and
             external subject matter experts, and facilitate data access.

       ▪     Translate the data needs outlined in the Analytical Approach step
             into specific data requirements.
       ▪     Build and maintain the ETL pipelines to ingest data from identified
             sources into the data platform, ensuring data freshness and reliability.
       ▪     Provide subject matter expertise on relevant data sources and data elements.
       ▪     Assist in validating the accuracy and completeness of authoritative data.
       ▪     Identify key data sources (e.g., logistics databases, sensor data, personnel
             records) relevant to the problem, documenting their access methods and
             limitations.
       ▪     Evaluate the quality and completeness of potential data sources, determining
             their suitability for robust data modeling to solve the problem.
       ▪     Obtain the data validation insights from data analysts on data quality checks
             to make informed decision on feasibility of available data for integration.
       ▪     Document data quality metrics and limitations.
•   Deliverables: Data Requirements Document, Data Source Inventory, Data Access Plan.

3. Data Wrangling – Clean, transform, and integrate data to ensure
             usability in the given Army data platform
•   Sub-steps: Data Understanding, Data Preparation
•   Tasks:
       ▪     Perform exploratory data analysis (EDA) to understand data characteristics,
             identify anomalies, and assess trends.

        ▪    Develop and implement data wrangling scripts (e.g., Python, SQL).

•   Tasks:

        ▪    Provide context and insights to enhance the interpretability of data products.
