                                          Forward

The purpose of this guide is to assist small unit leaders and Soldiers with the emerging principles,
training strategies, techniques, and other considerations needed for gaining technological fluency.
Given the rate of technological advancements, units need to adapt by leveraging and integrating
new technologies into the fight faster than the enemy. The fundamentals and traditional training
strategies and approaches like the 8-step training model or the crawl, walk, run methodology
remain relevant. However, transformation in contact (TiC) requires a paradigm shift, including
innovative and flexible training methodologies. We need to think in terms of “stacking” innovative
technologies to modify existing tactics, techniques, and procedures. In many cases a clever
technological solution may very well be a game changer on the battlefield. Every leader and Soldier
in every unit has a role in leveraging technology to solve tactical problems. We need every Soldier
and leader to become technologically fluent. The bottom line is that the battlefield is constantly
evolving. We are empowering small unit leaders and Soldiers to help our Army in developing
emerging tactics, techniques, and procedures with new technology to maintain our competitive
advantage and, if needed, decisively winning future wars.

                                           Introduction

The U.S. Army Research Institute for the Behavioral and Social Sciences (ARI) designed and developed
this guide to assist units in integrating new technologies. The evidence-based strategies and learning
content within this guide are based on extensive field research in collaboration with Soldiers and
leaders assigned to transforming in contact (TiC) units, as well as innovation labs personnel and other
subject matter experts. This product is part of a larger research effort focused on developing models of
technological fluency, methods and measures to assess and develop technological fluency across Soldiers’
careers, and technologies to maximize technological fluency resilience and performance. For more
information on ARI, see https://research.ari.army.mil/Our-Research/ .
HOW TO USE THIS GUIDE:
•   Critical Questions: This guide begins with a list of critical questions that are relevant when presented
    with the challenge of training and integrating novel technology. Leaders must think critically. A key to
    success is to ask the right questions.
•   The graphic novel consists of eight chapters and provides an illustrative vignette for how a small unit
    leader organizes and leads subordinates through a unique process of receiving, learning, becoming
    proficient, and integrating new technologies into their formation and mission set. This illustrative
    vignette allows leaders to think about how novel technology affects the way they should plan, prepare,
    execute and assess as part of the operations process.
•   Glossary of Terms: The glossary provides definitions of some terms essential for gaining technological
    fluency (TF). These terms are annotated in bold throughout the graphic novel and include terms from
    the Technological Fluency Leader’s Card.
•   The TF Leader’s Card (nine components) is a one-page guide of key factors for the leader to consider
    when fielded with new technologies and the best practices to integrate those new technologies more
    rapidly with greater proficiency. This TF Leader’s Card summarizes key factors described within
    the graphic novel. Readers should use the TF Leader’s Card as a quick reference guide to support
    transformation in contact.
•   The TF Resources page provides additional articles and documents for professional development. This
    page includes important Center for Army Lessons Learned documents from recent transformation in
    contact (TiC) units. Small unit leaders and their subordinates should engage in professional and self-
    development to keep up with best practices and adapting to the rapid pace of technological change.
CRITICAL THINKING: ASKING THE RIGHT QUESTIONS TOWARDS GAINING TECH
FLUENCY
Leaders must think critically. A key to success is asking the right questions. A list of critical questions is
provided below. Review these questions to gain appreciation for all the things a leader must consider when
fielding, training, and experimenting with novel technology. Refer to these questions to help you plan,
prepare, execute, and assess the integration of novel technology into training.
Understanding Contextual Factors and the Big Picture
•   Do I fully understand how the technology influences my unit beyond two levels up?
•   What new technology is arriving and when?
•   What problem is this technology solving?
•   Will this technology perform as stated by the manufacturer and under various conditions (e.g., terrain,
    climate, elevations, weather)?
•   How do I best integrate learning new technology into currently planned training?
                                                                                                                 3
    Seeing Yourself (knowledge, skills and abilities)
    •   What do I need to personally learn about this new technology (e.g., specifications, capabilities,
        employment methods)?
    •   Do I need to adjust anything with my leadership style (e.g., transformational)?
    •   What knowledge, skills, and abilities do I need to help my unit integrate the new equipment?
    •   What can I do to prepare myself and my subordinates?
    Mitigate the Barriers and Biases
    •   How will we logistically support the technology through repair parts and maintenance?
    •   What policies or regulations prevent full application of this technology (e.g., intended use or other
        applicability)?
    •   What may stand in the way of success and how do I mitigate the impact?
    Set the Climate
    •   How do we promote a learning and collaborative environment within the unit?
    •   How can we develop and maintain a culture of experimentation and innovation?
    Select Soldier Talent
    •   Who are the best individuals within the unit to learn and train others with this piece of technology?
    •   How can we empower Soldiers to adopt a self-learning mentality?
    Leverage Resources Available
    •   Where can I seek out information on this piece of technology (e.g., other units, internet, manufacturer,
        manuals)?
    Apply Emerging Principles
    •   How do we best maintain our fundamental warfighting skills and introduce new technology into our
        battle drills and collective tasks?
    Implement Best Practices
    •   Which training technique provides an individual with the best learning environment?
    •   How do we incorporate documentation and feedback into our training methodology?
    Assess and Document
    •   How do we best assess operator proficiency with this piece of technology?
    •   What documents require revisions/updates (e.g., TTPs, TACSOP, Manuals)?

     Borehenti Ability to solve problems: The capacity to identify issues, analyze them, and generate effective
     solutions.
     Adapt: The ability to adjust quickly and intelligently to new situations or challenges.
     Adapt or die mentality: A mindset emphasizing that continuous adaptation is necessary for survival and
     relevance.
     Administrative inhibitors: Bureaucratic or policy-related barriers that hinder progress or adaptability within
     an organization.
     Army networking: Communication and data-sharing systems available within Army infrastructure for
     operations and training to include intra unit collaboration. The search for additional units with knowledge of
     or experience with a specific technology.
     Assessing: The process of evaluating the effectiveness, efficiency, or performance of a person, system, strategy,
     or operation, often to determine areas for improvement or to measure success against objectives.
     ASVAB mechanical score: A score from the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery assessing mechanical
     knowledge and aptitude.
     Bottom-up solutions: Problem-solving approaches that originate from grassroots or operational levels, relying
     on the insights and initiatives of those closest to the issue rather than top-down authority.
     CALL: Center for Army Lessons Learned, the Army’s proponent for lessons learned integration.
     Capabilities and limitations: Describes what a system, unit, person, or process can and cannot do, in various
     tactical environments (weather, terrain, and climate).
     Center of Excellence (COE): A specialized team or facility that provides leadership, best practices, research,
     and training in a specific branch of expertise.
     Communicate effectively: The clear and purposeful exchange of information that ensures mutual understanding
     and supports collaboration and informed decision-making.
     Competing priorities: Multiple tasks or goals that vie for the same time and resources, often requiring leaders
     to make critical decisions about where and how to place emphasis on incorporating new technologies into
     existing training schedules.
     Critical thinking: The ability to objectively analyze information, evaluate evidence, and form reasoned
     judgments.
     Creative and curious: A mindset that encourages innovative solutions, problem-solving, and continuous
     learning.
     Cross training/collaboration: Teaching different individuals to learn multiple roles or skills to enhance
     flexibility and teamwork across domains.
     Data literacy: The ability to read, understand, analyze, and communicate data accurately to make informed
     decisions.
     Discovery learning: A process where learners gain knowledge through experiences, exploration, and inquiry
     rather than from institutional or classroom instruction.
     Doctrine: A formal set of principles, policies, or procedures that guide military forces in operations, planning,
     and decision-making. Doctrine serves as a foundation for training and operational execution.
     Empathy: The ability to understand and share the feelings, perspectives, and experiences of others.

Employ and integrate: The coordinated use and combination of capabilities, personnel, systems, or strategies
to achieve a desired effect or mission outcome.
Empower: To give someone the authority, confidence, or means to do something innovative with technology.
Empowerment through intent: Providing clear guidance and purpose to allow subordinates to act independently.
Experiment: A test or trial to discover something unknown or to validate a hypothesis.
Failing forward: Learning from mistakes and using failure as a steppingstone to future success.
Field Service Representative (FSR): A professional who supports and services equipment or systems at
customer sites.
Flatten the organization: A management practice aimed at reducing hierarchical levels within a unit. Find the
best person to operate the system and train others. Increase the connectivity between the operator and the
leader.
Flexible and adaptable: The ability to shift strategies, roles, or behaviors in response to changing conditions.
Frequency spectrum: The entire range of electromagnetic wave frequencies used to transmit signals, including
radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays. Often refers specifically to the
range of radio frequencies (RF) used for communication, radar, broadcasting, and other technologies.
Fundamentals: Core principles or essential skills required for success in a particular domain.
General culture: The shared values, beliefs, and behaviors that shape an organization’s environment and
decision-making.
General skepticism: A tendency to doubt new ideas or approaches, often due to past experiences or entrenched
beliefs.
Goal: A desired result or outcome that an individual or group aims to achieve.
Guided exploration: A learning method where individuals investigate topics with direction or support.
Hands on training: Practical, experiential learning involving direct engagement with tools, equipment, or
scenarios.
Humility: A leadership quality that involves recognizing one’s limitations and being open to input and
feedback from others.
Industry provided: Technology, training, or support services supplied by commercial or private-sector
partners.
Internet: Global network to access remote expertise, support, or resources, often in real time.
Innovation lab: A proactive environment focused on developing forward thinking solutions through individual
ideas or concepts.
Interest and willingness: The level of enthusiasm and openness an individual has toward participating or
learning.
Integrate and document: The act of combining various components (such as systems, teams, or functions)
into a unified whole to improve coordination, efficiency, effectiveness, and recording them for continuity.
Leader receptivity: A leader’s openness to feedback, innovation, and the ideas of others.
Leader support and buy-in: Active engagement and endorsement from leadership, critical for the success of
initiatives.
Learning fast and on the fly: Learning quickly without the benefit of formal training or learning in real time
during execution with novel technology.

     Lethality: The capacity of a force, system, or weapon to effectively neutralize or destroy a target. It is a key
     metric in evaluating combat effectiveness.
     Limited logistical support: Constraints in transportation, supply chains, or material availability that affect
     mission execution.
     Limited training support: Inadequate resources such as land, time, personnel, instructions, or equipment for
     conducting training.
     Mental agility: The ability to think quickly, adapt to changing situations, and switch between tasks or ideas
     with ease.
     Manufacturer: A company or entity that produces equipment, materials, or systems, often used in defense or
     industrial sectors.
     Manufacturer’s manual: An instructional guide provided by a product’s maker detailing its use and maintenance.
     Mental modeling: The process of building internal representations of how systems work to predict outcomes
     and guide actions.
     Mobile contact team (MCT): A deployable asset tasked with supporting units in forward or remote locations.
     Motivated and proactive: Acting with energy and initiative rather than waiting to be directed.
     NET/NEF: Acronyms commonly used in military contexts; refers to New Equipment Training / New
     Equipment Fielding.
     Old-school mentality: An approach or mindset rooted in traditional methods, often resistant to change.
     Openness: A person’s general willingness to be receptive to new ideas, emotions, and experiences, encompassing
     qualities like creativity, curiosity, and intellectual depth.
     Outcomes-Based Training Evaluation (OBTE): A training assessment methodology that measures the results
     or impacts of training rather than just the process, focusing on whether learning objectives and operational
     goals are achieved.
     Previous experience: Knowledge or skill gained from past involvement in tasks or roles.
     Procedural knowledge: Step-by-step instructions or processes that guide task completion.
     Protection: Measures taken to guard personnel, equipment, and infrastructure from enemy threats such as
     direct attack, the compromise of physical security, intelligence operations, and cyber defense.
     Rate of technology advancement: The speed at which new technologies are developed and adopted.
     Reasoning by analogy: Applying knowledge from one domain to solve problems in another based on
     similarities.
     Receptive: Openness to new ideas and the ability to generate innovative solutions.
     Reduce cognitive load: Minimizing unnecessary mental effort so learners can focus on essential information.
     Redundancy: Creating backups or overlaps in systems or roles to ensure reliability and resilience.
     Resourceful: Able to find quick and clever ways to overcome difficulties.
     Return on investment: A performance metric that compares net gain or loss relative to the resources invested.
     Risk acceptance: Willingly taking on calculated risks to achieve mission success.
     Risk aversion: Avoiding actions that could lead to failure or harm, often resulting in overly conservative
     decision-making.

Scaffolding or guided exploration: Instructional support that helps learners build knowledge step-by-step before
they work independently.
Select / develop trainers: Choosing and preparing the best individuals to teach others effectively, rather than
relying on the traditional Army instructor and education models. SMEs are not necessarily the leaders with
the most Army experience, they are the individuals with the most exposure to the technology. Also, part of the
8-step training model.
Self-explanation: The practice of articulating one’s reasoning to reinforce learning and understanding.
Self-learning mentality: A mindset oriented toward independently acquiring knowledge and skills.
Shifting the paradigm: Changing the fundamental approach or underlying assumptions of a system or practice.
Moving from a top-down management style to a collaborative, team-based approach.
Signature emissions: Identifiable electromagnetic, acoustic, thermal, or other detectable outputs that a system,
object, or platform produces—often unintentionally—that can be used to detect, identify, or track it.
Situational context and relevance: Aligning training or operations with the specific environment or mission
conditions.
Speed: The pace at which actions or decisions are executed.
Stacking: Integrating new technologies and systems into existing collective tasks and battle drills for enhanced
effect and performance.
Subjective assessments (outcomes based): Evaluations that rely on qualitative judgment rather than objective
scoring, focused on desired end results.
Subgoal learning: A learning strategy that breaks complex tasks into smaller, manageable objectives (subgoals)
to help improve understanding, focus, and mastery of the overall task.
Sunk costs: Past investments (time, money, effort) that cannot be recovered and should not dictate future
decisions.
Tactical knowledge: Understanding and applying doctrine for specific actions, methods, or decisions used to
achieve short-term objectives in a tactical environment. It involves knowing how to respond effectively in real-
time, often under pressure, based on the conditions and goals at hand.
Tactical Standard Operating Procedures (TACSOP): A unit-level document that outlines standardized procedures
for tactical operations, ensuring consistency and effectiveness in the execution of missions.
Technical: Specialized knowledge or skills related to equipment, procedures, or systems.
Time constraints: Limited availability of time to complete tasks or training.
Tinker: To attempt small repairs or improvements by experimenting and learning informally.
Training techniques: Methods or strategies used to educate and prepare individuals.
Transformation in Contact: The US Army’s approach to rapidly adapt to battlefield needs by integrating new
technologies and tactics during ongoing operations
Trial and error: A problem-solving method involving repeated attempts and learning from mistakes.
Understand its purpose: Comprehend the reason or function behind an action, object, or idea to solve a problem.
Vendor: Company or individual that sells goods or services.
