Purpose
Since the morning of October 7th, 2023, the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), Israel National
Police, and other security forces have been involved in a wide spectrum of active
combat operations that have spanned the range from Large Scale Combat Operations
(LSCO) to internal security and stability. Prior to the war the Israeli medical services had
some significant differences to their U.S. counter parts but shared many of the same
assumptions and approaches. The attacks on October 7th provided a shock to the
system and forced transformation in contact. Since then, the process of transformation
has continued. Although there are some differences between the operational
environment in which Israel has fought and the future fight envisioned by the U.S. Army,
Israel’s wars continue to provide a critical real world battle lab from which there is as
much to learn now as when observations of the 1973 War transformed U.S. Army
Doctrine. This is as true for military medicine as for other arms.
Introduction

This document synthesizes critical lessons from the October 7th attacks and the
subsequent IDF response during the Swords of Iron War to inform U.S. force
preparation for Large-Scale Combat Operations (LSCO). It is the nature of the military
system that while it focuses on medical support it captures initial lessons on a range of
issues including armored platforms, command and control systems, and the importance
of specialist combat driving courses and driver proficiency. It highlights that effective
medical support extends far beyond clinical and tactical proficiency and that adaptability
is a paramount requirement for all forces including medical support. It provides lessons
ranging from technical procedures that can be instituted at the Soldier and provider level
to broad structural and platform focused observations.

The observations and lessons in this document result from a mix of sources including
presentations and materials provided by the IDF during the 2025 Shoresh Military
Medical Meeting as well as interviews during the one-week period around the meeting.
These interviews included a wide range of interviewees from paramedics who were
active on the ground on October 7th through the commander of police and gendarme
medical services and every level in between. They also included the divisional surgeons
for most IDF maneuver divisions as well as the chief medical officers for every theater
command and medical officers and operators from SOF Units.

The IDF experience demonstrates that while mastery of casualty triage, treatment,
evacuation, and medical logistics is foundational, U.S. Forces – particularly medical
assets – must rapidly identify both critical gaps and emerging threats, adapt processes
and procedures, and then proactively integrate novel techniques and technologies to



                                                                                           3
mitigate risk. Initial plans will be disrupted, success hinges on rapid assessment,
enabled decision-making, and proactive problem-solving.

The observations include lessons from areas of IDF success. For example, IDF’s
success in rapid ground and air MEDEVAC, even in highly contested environments, was
central to the IDF’s approach for medical services. This relied on threat-appropriate
evacuation platform use, real-time intelligence, operational prioritization, evacuation
team training, and the integration of civilian medical assets. This indicates that U.S.
Forces must refine MEDEVAC procedures, leveraging technology (digital mapping, real-
time tracking), enhancing training, and fostering interoperability with civilian partners.

Ruggedized tablets captured casualty care documentation in real time from the Point of
Injury (POI) or Casualty Collection Point (CCP) through MEDEVAC and the roles of
care. Not only did digital casualty care documentation improve care but force-wide data
capture informed the development of TTPs, as well as equipment and policy changes.
This indicates the value for U.S. Forces of prioritizing the development of integrated
digital medical record that enables rapid documentation and data transfer to improve
both individual casualty care and inform Commanders and the Joint Trauma System.

When it comes to adaptive medical logistics & resupply, the IDF rapidly adapted
resupply routes and methods under fire, utilizing civilian infrastructure and innovative
delivery systems (drones, all-terrain vehicles) when traditional channels were
compromised. It suggests that U.S. Forces must prioritize redundant supply chains, pre-
positioning of critical medical supplies, and the ability to rapidly procure and deploy
novel solutions.

For rapid and accurate casualty treatment the document notes that the IDF significantly
improved survival by updating trauma algorithms to immediately identify and treat
casualties in hemorrhagic shock, minimize airway and respiratory procedures in the field
and prioritize rapid evacuation. Therefore, U.S. Forces must evaluate treatment
algorithms and prioritization of blood administration vs. airway and respiratory
interventions in both evacuation-permissive and prolonged field care settings.

It also observes that the IDF’s low case fatality rate, despite higher injury scores from
prior wars, highlights the prioritization of whole blood as far forward as possible through
classic tactical supply chains, policy updates, and novel storage and delivery solutions
(vehicle fridges, air drop from drone) and that as a result U.S. Forces should prioritize
the distribution, storage, and administration of whole blood to appropriate casualties as
far forward as possible.

Given the challenges of Acute Stress Disorder (ASD) and Post-Traumatic Stress
Disorder (PTSD), it is noteworthy that the IDF’s proactive commitment to psychological
health – through commander training, timely support, and frontline mental health officer

                                                                                           4
deployment – resulted in significantly lower PTSD rates than expected. This
demonstrates the importance of prioritizing mental wellbeing alongside physical health
and that U.S. Forces should achieve forward psychological health support by pre-
positioning Combat Operational Stress Control (COSC) teams and establishing
comprehensive, multi-tiered support networks, with specific attention to high-risk groups
and the unique challenges of moral injury.

In the fields of operational health and force readiness, the IDF executed proactive public
health measures – including robust biosurveillance, forward-positioned preventive
medicine teams, and stringent food safety protocols – to mitigate health threats and
maintain operational readiness. This indicates that U.S. Forces should prioritize
integrating Operational Public Health throughout all operational phases, investing in
innovative disease control technologies, fully utilizing highly trained Field Sanitation
Teams, adhering to established food safety and sanitation standards, and ensuring
readily available resources to proactively safeguard the force and enable mission
success.

When it comes to employing veterinary capabilities as force multiplier, the IDF
effectively integrated veterinary support into their medical operations, ensuring the
health and wellbeing of military working dogs (MWD) to maintain their operational
capabilities. To replicate this success, U.S. Forces must prioritize veterinary capabilities
– including robust training, data collection, and telemedicine – to ensure MWD medical
readiness and maximize their critical contributions to mission success.

These are but a summary of some of the observations and lessons contained within this
document. Adapting the full range of lessons in this document requires a strategic
investment in technology, continuous improvement, proactive engagement, and robust
planning. This is not all or nothing document. While some lessons would require
substantial upgrades in capabilities or changes across the range of the DOTMIL
spectrum, others can be instituted today with few to no additional resources.

Despite Israel’s preparations it had to undergo a painful process of transformation in
contact that U.S. now has the opportunity to avoid. The time for preparation is now.
Simply possessing clinical and tactical skills is insufficient. From the observations of the
Middle Eastern Wars, if the US Army is to prevail in the complex and challenging LSCO
environment, it must actively cultivate a system capable of rapid adaptation and the
preservation of force health – encompassing documentation, treatment, MEDEVAC,
medical logistics, FHP, psychological wellbeing, and veterinary support – to ensure the
well-being and operational effectiveness of its forces.

Summary of Recommendations

These recommendations are informed by lessons learned from the IDF, particularly their
innovative approaches to medical support in high-intensity conflict, and are tailored for
applicability to LSCO. The full report contains detailed analysis and supporting data for
each recommendation.

Blood Management: Effective blood management and casualty evacuation are critical,
especially given anticipated evacuation delays in LSCO. Key actions include:
   •   Assessment & Enhancement of Blood Supply: Conduct an Army-wide
       assessment of blood storage and transport platforms. Explore retrofitting field
       ambulances with refrigeration. Expand the DoD blood supply, testing capacity,
       and international agreements within the European theater. Consider
       hardened/underground storage for strategic blood reserves.

   •   Frontline Blood Availability: Prioritize solutions for distributing and storing blood
       products at the point of injury (POI) and forward echelons.

   •   Innovative Delivery Methods: Invest in research and collaboration with the IDF on
       Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV)-delivered blood capabilities, particularly for
       delivery to Role 1 medical personnel. Assess the feasibility of a casualty
       exchange point resupply system mirroring the IDF model.

Psychological Health & Resilience: Prioritizing proactive psychological health
intervention is essential for maintaining combat effectiveness.

   •   Leverage Partner Nation Expertise: Collaborate with the Israeli National Police
       (INP) and IDF to leverage lessons learned regarding psychological health,
       particularly their rapid implementation of medical training and resilience programs
       post-October 7th.

   •   Forward Behavioral Health Support: Consider rotational COSC deployments to
       AO Victory for operational experience and pre-positioning. Prioritize forward
       deployment of COSC Behavioral Health Teams near the Forward Line of Troops
       (FLOT) for rapid response to Potential Traumatic Events (PTEs) and early
       intervention. Consider incorporation of an in-theater Rehabilitation and Recovery
       Center into OPLANs to ensure rapid access to intensive behavioral health
       support, accelerating Soldier recovery and return to duty.

   •   Comprehensive Training: Implement mandatory Operational Resilience Training
       (ORT) for all USAREUR-AF Soldiers deploying to AO Victory, with a specific
       focus on iCOVER training for peer intervention during acute stress reactions.
       Train leaders in debriefing and risk identification. Ensure medics receive

                                 Chapter 1
                 Command and Control: Tactical Computer Tablets

Overview
The IDF utilizes three distinct tablet systems for command and control (C2) purposes.
Platoon and squad leaders are equipped with standalone, non-networked tactical
computer tablets featuring satellite maps and other essential information, including
capabilities for commanders to add operational overlays (for more see Center for Army
Lessons Learned, Initial Impressions Report: Israel-Hamas War Observations).
Company commanders and heavy vehicles (such as the Namer) employ networked
tablets that enable real-time updates, unit communication, coordination, and the ability
to call for effects. The focus of this discussion is the third type: the medical tablet. As
part of the IDF’s ongoing wartime transformation, the next planned development phase
involves integration of medical functionality into the maneuver command tablet. This will
enhance coordination between medical units, local forces, and higher echelons, and
streamline the process of establishing landing zones for medical evacuation.

Observations:

   •   Initially, communication between IDF medical assets relied on a variety of
       systems- cell phones, military networks, secret networks, and top-secret
       networks. Through transformation in contact, by the mature phases of the war, in
       addition to standard military communications which remained the most common
       mode of communication, every medical evacuation vehicle and rotary wing
       evacuation aircraft had a top-secret radio for communications. Beginning at the
       battalion level, treatment teams also had a non-networked tablet to document
       casualty care and then relay the documentation to the next echelon of evacuation
       (rotary wing aircraft) by near field communication. However, these tablets do not
       communicate with civilian hospitals. In addition, those in Namers and the senior
       officer of the evacuation teams have a networked ground forces tablet.

   •   The utilization of hardened tablets to complete the digital casualty care card
       evolved significantly over the course of the war. Before October 7th, the IDF
       documented casualty care on field cards similar to our DD1380 (TCCC Card).
       The initial version (Version 1) required generating and scanning QR codes for
       data transfer, but experienced issues with unreliable connections. Version 2
       enabled data transfer between tablets through near field communication by
       holding the tablets together for 20-30 seconds, but this process delayed air
       MEDEVAC departures. The current version (Version 3) allows for immediate
       transfer of casualty care documentation by tapping the tablet against a magnetic

                                                                                         10
    card carried by the casualty. These magnetic cards are costly and are therefore
    secured to the casualty (typically via a lanyard around the neck) by the first
    medical personnel encountered.

•   The IDF is currently working to integrate the tablets with the secured IDF network
    to enable real-time information sharing across the force and to facilitate
    communication with receiving civilian hospitals, allowing for tracking of care
    rendered and casualty conditions prior to arrival. Future developmental efforts
    also include integrating speech-to-text capabilities and generative AI into the
    tablets.

•   The implementation of tablets for documenting casualty care data increased field
    documentation compliance rates threefold. This electronically captured data is
    input into the IDF Trauma Registry and casualty dashboard, enabling analysis of
    injury patterns and treatment effectiveness. The IDF is developing a live feed
    mechanism for data into the Trauma Registry. (See Evacuation, Hospital, and
    Treatment sections for related recommendations).

                             Medical Vehicle Observations

Overview
Israel employs a range of ground vehicles to conduct medical evacuation. The
effectiveness of these platforms is further enhanced by Israel’s combat driver and
advanced combat mobility courses. These ground vehicles encompass heavy and light
platforms, fulfilling distinct roles. Heavy platform ambulances provide life-saving
capabilities, including whole blood, and enable medical evacuation even in heavily
contested environments, allowing the IDF to initiate evacuation and treatment within
minutes of injury. During the war, the IDF retrofitted many of its light vehicles, including
the HMMWVs, to accommodate stretchers. Furthermore, both heavy and light platforms
were modified with blood refrigerators with extended power supply, oxygen cylinders in
an armored case for shrapnel protection, and vibration- and shock-resistant medical
device mounts. Evacuation vehicles are routinely equipped with three units of whole
blood. These modifications were performed in the field by field technicians and
maintainers, ensuring the continued operational readiness of these platforms throughout
the conflict.

Heavy Platforms

       •   The heavy armored platforms proved key to Israeli doctrine, enabling rapid
           casualty evacuation. They allow advanced treatment capabilities and supplies
           to quickly reach casualties and evacuate them even under intense enemy fire.
           Currently, Israel operates two heavy platforms, the Achzarit and the Namer
           armored personnel carriers (APCs). The Achzarit is currently being phased
           out.

       •   Namer – Based on the chassis and utilizing the identical armor to the
           Merkava IV tank, the Namer ambulance can incorporate active protection
           systems such as Trophy and Windbreaker. It also includes limited weaponry
           for self-defense when necessary, enabling it to operate in the most intense
           combat situations to reach casualties. The Namer can store and transport
           whole blood and is integrated into the Israeli C4ISR Network at echelons,
           including the networked tablets (see Command and Control discussion). This
           integration facilitates the coordination of protection, evacuation routes, and
           helicopter landing zones. The Namer is deployed in areas where enemy anti-
           tank systems, drones, and other advanced capabilities pose the greatest
           threat.


                                                                                         12
      •   Achzarit – Based on the chassis of a T-55 tank but with significant
          improvements in armor and capabilities, the Achzarit provides armored
          protection and operates similarly to the Namer.

Light Platforms

      •   HMMWV – The IDF employs HMMWVs and other modified light vehicles for
          primary casualty evacuation from lower-threat areas, or as a secondary
          evacuation stage. Heavy vehicles from the lower echelon tactical units can
          transfer casualties to specialist light vehicle evacuation units held by the
          Brigade, Division, and Theater commands. This practice has been particularly
          important in Lebanon where distances to landing zones or ground
          ambulances are greater. These drivers are often graduates from driver or
          combat mobility courses, enabling them to drive at higher speeds at night
          using night vision alone.

      •   Mercedes Sprinter/Ford 450/550 – These ambulances are utilized for onward
          transport for casualties from tactical casualty evacuation units at casualty
          transfer points in the rear area.

Merkava 4 and 4B

      •   The Merkava 4 and 4B both possess the capability to transport casualties
          under certain conditions (requiring locking the turret). This was a standard
          practice for casualty evacuation in the 2006 Lebanon War. However, with the
          acquisition of Namer ambulance, the IDF moved away from this procedure,
          and it was not employed during the current war. Instead, a Merkava 4 or 4B is
          frequently partnered with a Namer or Achzarit, with the Merkava providing
          escort to facilitate the Namer’s access to casualties, demonstrating the IDF’s
          emphasis on rapid casualty evacuation.

Rotary Wing Evacuation Platforms

      •   Unit 669 is the Israel Air Force's tactical rescue unit which employs the
          Blackhawk, and the Sea Stallion helicopters as evacuation platforms.

      •   For more on 669 training and operations, see the section on casualty
          evacuation.

                                      Chapter 3
                            Medical Structures and Assets

Overview
At the beginning of the war, the experience level of medical providers varied
significantly. Many providers were new conscripts with minimal experience and no
combat experience. Consequently, the IDF relied heavily on the Reservists for their
clinical expertise and combat experience. Junior providers rapidly gained both clinical
expertise and combat proficiency, leading to more effective and efficient casualty
treatment. Initially, battalions had 8-10 paramedics, but this number was reduced to 3-4
as paramedics gained experience.

At the division level, the Division Surgeon oversees medical operations. These
Surgeons found it crucial to empower physicians on the ground to make decisions, as
they possessed the most complete information; maintaining trust with their junior
physicians was critical to success.

At the BDE level, a medical company (resuscitation field company) consists of
numerous medical personnel, including a surgeon, anesthesiologist, and an emergency
room (ER) physician. This field hospital has surgical capability and ICU capability but
lacks radiologic capabilities. During the Swords of Iron War, this hospital was not
utilized, as 95% of casualties were evacuated to civilian hospitals for advanced
treatment within 47 minutes. Instead, the medical personnel were redistributed to
augment BN evacuation teams

Specialist evacuation teams (light and heavy) are at the BN level (see section on
evacuation in this report).

At the forward line of own troops (FLOT), each company has a company medical team
consisting of two vehicles, each staffed with one senior field caregiver (a paramedic or
physician) and two medics. The staffing level was increased to five personnel during
high-risk operations.

Following neutralization of the significant, active threat from Hamas, a primary care
clinic was established near the Gaza border (outside of enemy lines). This site also
functioned as a blood distribution center and vaccination clinic. The clinic was a mobile
facility (trailer) with a generator, co-located with a logistics center on an established
FOB. Due to the large female population, a Reservist Gynecologist was available on-
site twice weekly. This clinic primarily saw Disease Non-Battle Injury (DNBI) patients but



                                                                                       14
also received some minor trauma cases not requiring evacuation to a civilian hospital
(e.g. minor wounds, lacerations, etc.).

Psychological support was also available at this site, but this was reportedly
underutilized, likely due to the reliance on first aid psychological care on the field and
Social Workers located at FLOT with the units during the conflict. As the war
progressed, the PCM clinic was staffed with six physicians. Paper records were utilized
at this clinic.

                            Casualty and Medical Evacuation

Overview
The IDF prioritizes casualty survival and swift evacuation as a core demonstration of
commitment to its Soldiers, and as a critical factor in maintaining support from both a
mobilized population and a conscript military. This prioritization directly shapes civil
military-relations within the country, leading the IDF to build an evacuation-centric
system. To support this system, the IDF developed dedicated platforms, structures, and
procedures, continually refining them through transformation in contact throughout the
conflict. While the full replication of this system may not be feasible for the U.S. military
– due to the IDF’s reliance on civilian hospital infrastructure and geographical
considerations – the IDF’s prioritization of evacuation, and the investigation of its
supporting structures, training, and platforms, offer valuable insights for consideration.

Observations:

Core Principles and Commander Investment

       •   Rapid Evacuation = Survival: Timely evacuation is critical to casualty survival
           and a foundational principle of IDF medical doctrine.

       •   Commander Buy-In: Commanders quickly recognized the importance of rapid
           evacuation and medical care after initial combat experiences, directly
           impacting force maneuverability. Because heavy evacuation vehicles were
           paired with main battle tanks (MBTs), the expeditious return of MBTs
           facilitated by rapid evacuation further incentivized commander support.

       •   Minimizing Transfers: The guiding principle throughout the evacuation
           process is to minimize the number of casualty handoffs.

System Structure and Echelons of Care

       •   Company Level: Each company maintains a company medical team staffed
           with an Advanced Life Support (ALS) provider (a paramedic or physician), two
           medics, and a commander. This is increased to five personnel during high-risk
           operations. This team may or may not have designated medical vehicles.

       •   Battalion and Above Level: Specialist evacuation units exist at each echelon
           (BN, BDE, DIV, Theater). The specialist evacuation teams were an innovation

                                                                                           16
         prior to the war and the addition of theater level teams were a result of
         transformation in contact during the war. At the BN level, these teams typically
         consist of heavy (tracked, armored, self-defense and active protection
         capabilities) and light (HMMMWV) platforms. The teams most often consist of
         two to four vehicles. Team composition can adjust based on mission context.
         In rare cases where a given mission involves only infantry outside of a
         combined arms context, the BN evacuation team may consist solely of light
         platforms. Similarly, if a mission is expected to be in a particularly heavily
         contested area, the BN evacuation team will gain additional heavy platforms,
         and other echelons may provide additional teams to the BN. For more on the
         platforms see the section on C2 and Platforms. These teams are equipped
         with whole blood.

     •   Echelon Staffing: Evacuation teams include an ALS provider (physician or
         paramedic), three to four combat Soldiers, one to two medics per vehicle, one
         driver per vehicle, and one medical team commander (a combat officer). At
         the Brigade level, these evacuation teams also include surgical, anesthesia,
         and/or ICU specialists.

(U) Evacuation Flow

     •   Initial Treatment and Transport: At point of injury (POI), company medical
         teams provide initial treatment and transport casualties to a designated
         Casualty Collection Point (CCP).

     •   Battalion Evacuation: If available, the battalion evacuation team (heavy or
         light) assumes responsibility for onward evacuation from the POI or CCP. If
         not, the company medical team will continue to transport to the CCP for
         transfer.

     •   Treatment En Route: Medical treatment continues during evacuation.

         Evacuation Options: The battalion evacuation team can transport casualties
         directly to a helicopter landing zone (HLZ), to a higher echelon military ground
         evacuation team, or, in some cases, to a hospital.

     •   Rapid HLZ Transfer: Transfers to rotary wing evacuation at HLZs are
         conducted within 30 seconds for security and protection.




                                                                                       17
     •   Final Transport: Rotary wing or ground evacuation teams transport the
         casualty to a civilian hospital.

     •   Direct Extraction: In certain circumstances, the rotary wing evacuation team
         can land near the POI, extract the casualty directly and take them to the
         civilian hospital.

Key Innovations and Adaptations

     •   “Black Cat” Ground Extraction Teams: The addition of Air Force 669th “Black
         Cat” Ground Extraction Teams (five operators with specialized light vehicles)
         at the BDE level significantly reduced ground time (from 51 seconds to 29
         seconds), minimizing risk to the rotary assets, medical force, and casualties.
         (For more on this see the Medical Force Protection Section). These teams
         identify appropriate HLZs and evacuation capability requirements,
         communicating directly with the pilots to provide situational awareness near
         the HLZ. These teams are assigned as needed; the vast majority of battalions
         and brigades were not augmented with 669th personnel.

     •   Formalized AAR Process (669th): During the war, the 669th developed a
         formalized AAR Team, composed of five experts that analyzed video footage
         and recorded communications after each evacuation mission. This team held
         a debriefing with the team within 5 hours, providing rapid feedback and
         training updates, further accelerating learning. Based on common themes, the
         AAR Team developed a lessons-learned brief for the next team on shift,
         ensuring that all teams were briefed. The AAR team also used the lessons
         learned to develop training scenarios for the crew coming on shift the
         following week. This greatly facilitated transformation in contact.

Personnel and Training

     •   Critical Role of the Driver: Specialist evacuation team drivers are graduates of
         the IDF driver course and, in higher echelons, of the combat mobility course.
         This tactical proficiency allows them to drive very fast, through obstacles, at
         night (using binocular night vision devices) in no/low light conditions. This
         approach reduces evacuation time and helps protect from observation and
         certain types of UAV threat.

     •   Navigation Expertise: Some teams are augmented with expert navigators,
         particularly valuable in complex urban environments.

          movements, the severity of casualty determines whether evacuation is

                               Medical Force Protection


Overview
The protection of medical forces and evacuation operations is central to the IDF’s
system of rapid casualty and medical evacuation. To facilitate this, the IDF establishes
the evacuation routes and a corresponding protection scheme during the planning
phase of all operations. Once an evacuation is initiated, a dedicated team is assembled
at the brigade or division headquarters, operating under the rear area/secondary
commander of the formation higher than the one whose assets are conducting the
evacuation. Should a helicopter be required, securing the landing zone immediately
becomes the intelligence and fires priority for the sector. Furthermore, in contested
areas, an escort unit is often assigned to provide additional protection for the evacuation
team. However, one of the key lessons learned by the IDF is that evacuation teams
should possess organic protection capabilities.


Observations:

   •   Technological Suppression: The IDF employs anti-drone assets, armed UAVS,
       and EW capabilities to create windows of opportunity for rotary-wing touchdown
       and casualty collection.

   •   Integrate Fires: Fires assets are utilized to provide suppressing fire during
       evacuation operations.

   •   Obscuration and Night Operations: Smoke and other modalities of obscuration
       are often used simultaneously to facilitate deception, and IR modalities are used
       for night landings.

   •   Strict Movement Control: Evacuation assets are protected by strict movement
       control, with battalion and brigade commanders establishing control lines
       requiring approval one to three levels up for passage.

   •   Pre-Established Defensive Populations: Longstanding routes have established
       berms to take cover.

   •   Dedicated Route Reconnaissance: During the war in Gaza, once the route was
       secured, the evacuation teams conducted route familiarization independent from
       evacuation missions, to establish familiarity with the complex urban terrain.

                                                                                        23
  •   Rapid Transfer Protocols: The speed of casualty transfer is critical as part of
      medical force protection. By reducing the ground time of helicopters to less than
      30 seconds, the IDF reduced vulnerability to enemy fire and UAV threats.

Recommendations:

  •   Integrate Medical Planning Across All Operations: Medical planners must be
      incorporated into development of all OPLANs and training exercises, not solely
      medical-focused events, to ensure that Commanders proactively integrate
      medical force protection into their overall operational plans.

  •   Optimize Evacuation Protection Measures: The Army should explore ways to
      optimize protection in the case of casualty evacuation. Tactical evacuation units
      should possess organic protection capabilities.

  •   Prioritize Rapid and Secure Casualty Transfer: Focus on optimizing casualty
      transfer procedures to minimize the exposure of rotary-wing assets, prioritizing
      speed and utilizing techniques that reduce vulnerability to enemy fire and
      detection.

                                     Medical Logistics

Overview
The IDF has demonstrated effective medical logistics practices during recent conflicts
that could significantly benefit the U.S. Army. Their integrated brigade logistics,
dependable medical supply chain, and combined resupply/evacuation approach ensure
medical teams receive the support they need. Establishing a forward logistics hub
further streamlined sustainment. By adopting similar strategies – prioritizing medical
resupply in planning, exploring technologies like drones, and standardizing resupply
packages – the U.S. Army can improve the speed and consistency of critical medical
supply delivery in the field.

Observations:

   •   Dedicated Brigade Logistics: Unlike the U.S. Army, the IDF maintains a logistics
       coordination battalion within each brigade. This structure was developed after
       sustainment challenges experienced in the Second Lebanon War and are
       proving critical for efficient supply distribution.

   •   Reliable Supply Chain: During the war, medical teams consistently reported
       receiving all requested Class VIII (medical supplies), indicating a robust and
       responsive supply chain.

   •   Resupply at Casualty Transfer: A key logistical practice during the war (in Gaza
       and Lebanon) involves conducting medical resupply concurrently with casualty
       transfer, providing responding medical assets (e.g. medical company or light
       evacuation team) with equipment and supplies directly from the evacuation
       platform (e.g. 669th rotary wing aircraft or ground platform).

   •   Forward Logistics Hub: After the significant, active threat from Hamas was
       neutralized in Gaza, a dedicated logistics sustainment battalion established a
       central logistics hub in proximity to the Gaza fence line (Israeli territory), on an
       established FOB.

Recommendations:
  • Sustain Medical Logistics Integration: The Army should prioritize the integration
     of medical resupply into operational plans. Prioritizing critical Class VIII and
     exploring innovative delivery solutions (e.g. drones, etc.,) can provide rapid and
     safe delivery.

•   Standardized Resupply Packages: The Army should investigate and implement a
    standardized medical resupply package for handover evacuation units in the
    field, streamlining logistical support and ensuring consistent resupply capabilities.

               Physical and Psychological Casualties and Treatments

Injuries and Treatments:
Injury patterns on October 7th highlighted the vulnerability of those who self-mobilized
without adequate body armor or weaponry. The analysis of the subsequent conflict is
based on 4,541 casualties with complete records included in the IDF’s assessment.
Throughout the war, the IDF continuously refined its medical treatment techniques,
contributing to a broader transformation in battlefield care. This, coupled with rapid
evacuation times, adjusted field protocols, and high-quality treatment, resulted in a
significant decrease in the case fatality rate (CFR) during IDF combat operations from
14.8% (in 2006) to 7.1% (in 2024), despite a concurrent increase in injury severity score
(ISS). In 2006, only 8% of casualties had an ISS >15 but in 2024, 23% of casualties had
an ISS >15. This CFR is lower than those seen in US COIN operations during OIF and
OEF. The IDF prioritized assessing for and treating hemorrhagic shock with whole
blood, minimizing airway or breathing interventions in the field, and rapid evacuation.
Due to the difficulty of fully assessing the extent of blast injuries without advanced
imaging, all casualties with shrapnel were evacuated to civilian hospitals for further
evaluation, even if clinically stable.

Observations on Injuries:

   •   Injury Patterns and Mechanisms: The recent conflict presented a distinct injury
       profile. Among surviving police forces (including Gendarme and SOF) casualties
       from October 7-8, limb injuries were predominant (84%), with a significant
       proportion (9%) also sustaining abdominal/thoracic trauma. These injuries
       frequently involved combined shrapnel and gunshot wounds, with blast being the
       primary mechanism. These casualties also experienced compound fractures with
       severe bleeding, major peripheral nerve injuries, and resulting neurological
       dysfunction. Across the entirety of the fighting in Gaza, shrapnel accounted for
       the majority of injuries (62%), followed by blunt trauma (21%), gunshot wounds
       (12%), burns (9%), blast injuries (7%), inhalation injuries (5%), and crush injuries
       (2%). Shrapnel injuries, particularly those to the torso (42% of ISS >15) and head
       (26% of ISS >15), were strongly correlated with more severe outcomes.

   •   Injury Severity and Surgical Interventions: Overall, the distribution of Injury
       Severity Scores (ISS) among survivors (see Figure 1 for impact factors and injury
       percentages) was: mild (ISS 1-8) – 55%; moderate (ISS 9-15) – 22%; severe
       (ISS 16-24) – 11%; and critical (ISS 25-75) – 12%.

Observations on Casualty Treatment

  •   Rapid Evacuation and Initial Care: Evacuation times from the point of injury
      during the war were rapid, particularly for more severe casualties. (For more on
      evacuations see evacuation section of this document.) Time from point of injury
      (POI) to an Advanced Life Support (ALS) provider (a paramedic or a nurse)
      decreased from 10-25 minutes (2006) to 0-4 minutes during Swords of Iron War.
      Company medical teams, equipped with paramedics or physicians positioned
      close to the POI were able to provide immediate life-saving interventions
      including hemorrhage control, whole blood administration. Detailed air
      evacuation times by ISS category are as follows:

      ➢ Patients with ISS > 14: average evacuation time of 62 minutes, with 48% of
        these casualties arriving at the hospital within an hour of injury.

      ➢ Patients with ISS from 9-14: average evacuation time of 69 minutes, with 36%
        of these casualties arriving at the hospital within an hour of injury.

      ➢ Patients with ISS from 1-8: average evacuation time of 76 minutes, with 24%
        of these casualties arriving at the hospital within an hour of injury.

  •   Analgesia: During the war, analgesics were administered to 30% of conscious
      casualties, with 26% receiving transmucosal fentanyl, 12% ketamine, and 5%
      morphine. There is some evidence that the use of ketamine may reduce the
      psychological trauma to a casualty. Ketamine also has the added benefit of being
      able to be used for military working dog casualties.

                                                                                      29
•   Treatment Protocols and Resuscitation Strategies:

    ➢ Casualty treatment prioritized hemorrhage control, identification of casualties
      in hemorrhagic shock and immediate initiation of Low Titer O Whole Blood
      (WB), also as Remote Damage Control Resuscitation (RDCR).

    ➢ The IDF protocol differs from the U.S.’s “MARCH” (Massive hemorrhage,
      Airway, Respiration, Circulation and Hypothermia/Head Injury) algorithm for
      initial casualty assessment. The IDF’s protocol begins with identifying and
      treating massive hemorrhage, followed by a rapid Shock Survey to assess for
      hemorrhagic shock and immediately start blood if present. Next is a Primary
      Survey assessing airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure, and then
      a Resuscitation Survey to re-assess the effect of interventions on circulation,
      airway and breathing.

    ➢ Prior to the Swords of Iron War, the IDF’s protocol for volume resuscitation for
      bleeding casualties focused on Freeze Dried Plasma (FDP). During the war,
      WB became the resuscitation fluid of choice at the battalion and above level
      for bleeding casualties, followed by FDP and then packed red blood cells
      (PRBCs). Crystalloids are very rarely administered. FDP remained highly
      relevant at the company-level where WB was not commonly available.

    ➢ The prioritization of rapid administration of WB to casualties in shock at POI
      reduced the time of WB administration from 20 minutes (2018-2023) to 5
      minutes (2024).

    ➢ Indication for administering WB is profound shock, described as systolic blood
      pressure <90 mmHg or, when BP cannot be measured, absence of a radial
      pulse or a change in consciousness not related to a head injury or
      medications.

•   Hemorrhage Control Techniques: The IDF used the following methods for
    hemorrhage control: elastic trauma dressing (20.2% of cases), tourniquet (13.1%
    of cases), combat gauze (2.4% of cases), and foley catheter (0.3% of cases).

•   Blood Product Utilization in the Field: Significant quantities of blood products
    were administered directly in the field during ground maneuvers. 368 injured
    patients were treated with blood products in the field, with 297 of these receiving
    whole blood.

    protocols, and equipment to address the unique challenges of a contested
    environment. It is imperative that processes to expedite research, development,
    and acquisition of force protection and medical treatment innovations be
    established.

Psychological Casualty Prevention and Treatment
Both the Israel National Police (including its Gendarme Battalions, and its SOF
elements) and the IDF demonstrated a strong commitment to minimizing psychological
casualties by proactively adapting their approaches throughout the Swords of Iron War.
The IDF’s well-planned and rapidly evolving psychological support program proved
highly effective. Notably, 975 Mental Health Officers (MHOs) were activated, including
123 psychiatrists, with the vast majority activated within the first two weeks following
October 7th, demonstrating a proactive commitment to minimizing psychological harm.
Anticipating a 15-40% conversion rate from Acute Stress Reactions to PTSD based on
historical data, the program achieved a remarkably lower rate of only 5% PTSD
development among the 17,000 Soldiers initially presenting with ASRs. The success
underscores the critical importance of robust, adaptable mental health support systems
and could serves as a model for the U.S.

Preventive Treatment:
   • Commander Empowerment and Early Intervention: Recognizing commanders as
     first responders, the IDF Mental Health Officers (MHO) provided training for
     commanders to equip them to lead initial interventions. This approach gained

                                                                                      33
       buy-in through demonstrated evidence that early intervention and treatment
       facilitated faster return-to-duty rates for Soldiers.

   •   Targeted and Timely Debriefings: The IDF utilized debriefings as a core
       component of psychological resilience. All combat, combat support, and other
       relevant teams conducted a routine debriefing after each mission to process
       events and foster team cohesion. Following high-intensity missions, skilled
       MHOs conduct specialized “active interference” debriefings within 24 hours.
       Crucially, MHOs were deployed to the frontlines, enabling rapid response to
       acute incidents.

   •   Integrated Rest and Restoration Programs: Recognizing a cumulative impact of
       prolonged operations, the IDF implemented a “Refresh” program providing
       Soldiers with dedicated time for physical and mental restoration after extended
       combat missions or every 3-5 weeks. Soldiers were met with a complement of
       medical providers to help restore their bodies and minds. This period also
       incorporated essential vehicle and equipment maintenance.

   •   Optimized Rotational Schedules: Psychological wellbeing was actively integrated
       into operational planning through optimized rotational schedules. Initially,
       rotations were extended (up to 6 months) but the IDF later transitioned to fixed
       rotation cycles with clearly defined end-dates, significantly improving morale and
       resilience. Demobilization followed a structured process: one week of
       psychological debriefings, one week at home, and one week preparation for
       subsequent missions.

   •   Decentralized Expertise with Specialized Units: Within the 669th Squadron, a
       dedicated MHO (psychologist) trained team leaders to conduct initial mission
       debriefs, identify high risk events and warning signs, and understand when to
       escalate concerns to the MHO for specialized intervention.

Notes on the patterns of psychological casualties:

   •   High Risk Groups: Analysis revealed that the highest risk groups for
       psychological trauma were 1) medical teams, 2) snipers, and 3) heavy machinery
       operators from non-organic units with a very heavy OPTEMPO.

   •   Staff Exhaustion: The IDF also identified that Brigade and Division staff, including
       Brigade Surgeons, faced unique challenges with exhaustion and diminished


                                                                                        34
       psychological resilience due to a lack of scheduled breaks, unlike other Brigade
       personnel.

   •   Moral Injury: The IDF recognizes Moral Injury as a distinct condition from PTSD,
       requiring separate diagnostic and treatment approaches. The IDF identified three
       primary categories of triggering events for Moral Injury: 1) Acts committed
       (personally perpetrating actions violating moral beliefs), 2) Acts of omission
       (failing to prevent actions violating moral beliefs), and 3) Perceived betrayal by
       leadership (experiencing a breach of trust in leadership’s moral decision-making).

Observations on Psychological Resiliency (pre-Acute Stress Reaction)
Proactive Training and Early Intervention:

   •   IDF Paramedic Resilience Training: The IDF provides all paramedics (who are
       generally very young) with a dedicated 2-day behavioral health course focused
       on enhancing psychological resilience.

   •   Israeli National Police Comprehensive Resilience Program: On October 7th, the
       Israeli National Police (INP) formed much of the initial response. Over the next
       several weeks, the force was responsible for victim identification and war crimes
       investigation, which also was psychologically difficult. During the subsequent war
       Gendarme forces and Police SOF continued to engage in high tempo combat
       operations. Following October 7th, the Israeli National Police (INP) and its
       associated Gendarme implemented a robust psychological resilience program
       centered on self-care, command influence, and mitigating stress transmission.

   •   Widespread Team Therapy Training: The INP trained over 10,000 officers
       (approximately one-third of its personnel) in fundamentals of conducting and
       leading team therapy.

                Living Conditions and Food Safety affecting Soldiers

Overview:
Soldier morale and health remain are fundamentally linked to the quality and safety of
their food and living conditions. Prior to the commencement of large-scale ground
maneuvers, the IDF successfully maintained adequate health protection in both areas.
However, as the operation continued, increased reliance on field rations, a relaxation of
safety standards, and evolving living conditions led to a rise in disease incidence
alongside a decline in morale. Recognizing this, the IDF rapidly adapted its approach,
transforming not only how resources were provided but also how personnel responsible
for food safety interacted with the deployed forces. These adjustments proved effective,
reversing both the negative trends in health and morale as the conflict progressed.

Food and Living Conditions Observations:

   •   Rapid Force Expansion and Initial Health Protection: Following the events of
       October 7th, the IDF rapidly expanded its forces, quadrupling in size within
       weeks. Despite this rapid growth and logistical challenges, proactive preventive
       medicine teams successfully prevented any foodborne illness outbreaks during
       the pre-operational period, demonstrating effective early intervention.

   •   Initial Reliance on Field Rations and Morale Impact: The commencement of
       ground maneuvers into Gaza initially relied heavily on manot krav, field rations
       consisting primarily of canned goods. While providing necessary sustenance, this
       dependence negatively impacted Soldier morale due to limited variety and
       palatability of these rations. In addition, prolonged reliance on manot krav also
       resulted in insufficient caloric intake for Soldiers over time, potentially impacting
       operational effectiveness and overall health.

   •   Introduction of Hot Meals and Subsequent Outbreaks: Responding to
       Commander requests for improved meals, the IDF began supplementing rations
       with hot meals 2-3 times a week. Unfortunately, this well-intentioned effort led to
       several significant foodborne illness outbreaks due to deviations from established
       food safety protocols, including sourcing from non-approved civilian vendors and
       inadequate time and temperature controls.

   •   Corrective Actions and Return to Food Safety Standards: Recognizing the
       severity of the outbreaks, the Medical Corps Chief Surgeon presented data of
       these outbreaks to the theater level commander, resulting in prohibition of

                                                                                         40
      prohibited foods and a comprehensive public awareness campaign. This
      campaign, featuring prominent public figures, discouraged unsolicited food
      donations to Soldiers, emphasizing the importance of controlled supply chains. A
      return to strict adherence to established food safety policies and procedures
      effectively halted further major outbreaks.

  •   Mitigation Strategies and Safe Alternatives: To mitigate reliance on solely field
      rations, preventive medicine teams provided guidelines on safe alternatives for
      Soldiers, such as cooking rice and pasta and avoiding meat and dairy products.
      Additionally, safe food products suitable for self-preparation were supplied to
      minimize the risk of illness.

  •   Evolving Living Conditions and Initial Sanitation Challenges: Living conditions
      also evolved throughout the operations; initially, Soldiers slept in tanks or directly
      on the ground. As operations progressed, they began occupying buildings,
      presenting new logistical and sanitation challenges. Early maneuver phases
      involved the use of manure bags for fecal waste disposal, discarded along
      routes, highlighting the immediate need for established sanitation protocols in the
      absence of functional sewage systems.

  •   Establishment of Waste Management and Pest Control: Once temporary bases
      were established, designated waste areas were implemented, supported by
      proactive pest control measures from preventive medicine teams. Prior to full
      team access to certain areas, some Soldiers were trained to utilize commercial
      “OTC” pest control products. As areas became more secure and enduring,
      established logistics supply routes enabled regular waste removal via dedicated
      trucks, improving overall sanitation and hygiene.

Recommendations:

  •   While reliance on field rations has a negative impact on morale, and the
      commander-requested initiative to provide hot meals the frontlines are
      understandable, maintaining strict adherence to food safety policies and
      procedures is critical. Soldiers will inevitably seek to supplement rations and
      prepare their own food, increasing the risk of foodborne illness. Therefore,
      sustained command emphasis on food safety, coupled with proactive education
      from preventive medicine teams regarding high-risk foods and risk mitigation
      tools, is essential to prevent outbreaks that can significantly compromise
      readiness and pose a risk to mission success. To facilitate the provision of
      morale-boosting hot meals while mitigating risk, commanders desiring contracted

                                                                                         41
catering services should task Food and Water Risk Assessments (FWRAs) to
Medical Detachment Veterinary Service Support (MDVSS) teams or certified
Environmental Science and Engineering Officers (ESEOs). Veterinary Corps
Officers and trained ESEOs can conduct these FWRAs to provide commanders
with a comprehensive assessment of the risks associated with contracted hot
meals, along with actionable recommendations to reduce those risks and the
resulting residual risk. As FWRAs are required when contracting food for
Soldiers, utilizing this mechanism allows for improved morale through hot meals
while simultaneously upholding food safety standards and protecting operational
readiness.

                        Preventive Medicine and Public Health

Overview
The wars following October 7th presented significant public health and disease
prevention challenges. These stemmed from both the rapid mobilization of reserve
divisions into a combat zone and the prolonged, multi-front nature of the fighting, as the
IDF forces transition between areas with differing disease threats. The intense urban
combat environment further exacerbated these issues, creating conditions of low
sanitation, potential contact with local populations, collapsed infrastructure, degraded
living conditions in abandoned and barren territories, animal migration, and
compromised water and food supplies. These threats are highly relevant to potential
LSCO for the U.S. military, regardless of the theater or operation. The IDF’s success in
mitigating these challenges was largely attributable to advanced preparation and the
demonstrated ability to rapidly adapt and transform in response to evolving conditions.

Observations:

   •   DNBI Rates and Proactive Force Deployment: The Swords of Iron War
       demonstrated a successful public health response, resulting in relatively low
       hospitalizations due to Disease Non-Battle Injury (DNBI) compared to previous
       conflicts where DNBI rates often equaled or exceeded battle injury rates. This
       success was largely attributable to a proactive and robust preventive medicine
       force, initiated by the immediate activation and deployment of all 40 preventive
       medicine teams by the IDF’s Deputy Surgeon General following the attacks on
       October 7th.

   •   Organizational Structure of Preventive Medicine Teams: The IDF’s preventive
       medicine structure is organized with three preventive medicine teams assigned
       to each Division and three teams, including five Reserve teams (one of which is
       an epidemiologic team comprised of a preventive medicine physician, two
       nurses, and a few medics), at the theater level. Each preventive medicine team
       typically consists of a veterinarian (team commander), a pest control specialist, a
       public health Warrant Officer specializing in food and water safety, and a medic
       capable of administering vaccinations. While teams can be scaled down to two or
       three personnel, a veterinarian or Warrant Officer and a pest control specialist
       are always included.

   •   Core Functions and Responsibilities: The core functions of the preventive
       medicine teams encompass ensuring food and water quality and safety,

                                                                                          43
    minimizing animal disease and damage, implementing pest control, and advising
    on facility hygiene. Division-level preventive medicine expert teams, composed of
    a water engineer, a pest control expert, and a public health physician, address
    complex issues and special missions and conduct weekly field rotations to
    maintain situational awareness.

•   Team Equipment and Mobility: These highly mobile teams are equipped with
    minimal but essential resources, including a 4x4 pickup truck with a closed bed
    that carries pesticides and animal cages, food and water testing equipment
    (thermometers, food sample bags, chlorine strips, DPD 1 tablets, digital turbidity
    sensors with analog backups, water sample bottles (for chemical and bacterial
    testing), and a cooler), pest control equipment (mobile manual pesticide
    applicator, rodent traps, mobile animal traps, cages, rabies poles), and personal
    protective equipment. The pest control specialist and veterinarian are licensed to
    apply pesticides.

•   Education and Messaging Strategy: These preventive medicine teams prioritize
    Soldier education and training, shifting their approach from restrictive “don’t”
    messaging to empowering “do” guidance- advising Soldiers on safe practices
    and mitigation measures to reduce incidence of disease.

•   Key Principles of DNBI Reduction: Several key principles underpinned the IDF’s
    preventive medicine efforts: Maintaining a “boots on the ground” presence within
    enemy lines, proactively targeting identified vulnerabilities, emphasizing
    education and personal responsibility, and ensuring a continuous supply of
    hygiene equipment, including “Zero Morbidity Kits” containing essential sanitation
    and preventative supplies for each platoon.

       ➢ “Zero Morbidity Kits” contain soap, gloves, toilet paper, alcohol hand
         sanitizer, chlorine tablets (20 tablets per Soldier equating to 3.5 days of
         water), fly paper, mosquito and tick repellant (>13% DEET), baby wipes,
         manure bags, sunscreen and informational pamphlets.

•   Data Collection and Tracking Limitations: Despite these successes, a notable
    gap existed in real-time DNBI tracking. While the IDF effectively implemented
    electronic medical record keeping for combat casualties, a similar system was
    not in place for DNBI documentation in the field.

•   Rapid Threat Identification and Response: However, the IDF demonstrated an
    ability to rapidly identify emerging medical threats and respond with robust

       preventive measures, such as accelerated vaccinations programs (expanded to
       include meningococcal, tetanus, diphtheria, pertussis, and seasonal influenza for

                                   Dental Challenges

Dental health remains a significant readiness challenge, particularly within the U.S.
Reserve and National Guard components. Studies have shown a marked deterioration
of dental health among U.S. Reservists and Guardsmen following repeated
deployments, reaching levels where up to 40% are deemed medically non-deployable.
This loss of personnel during subsequent mobilizations is likely exacerbated by
deployment-related stress contributing to issues like teeth grinding, and the difficulties
maintaining adequate dental hygiene in field conditions. This challenge is not unique to
the U.S. military, as similar issues have been observed with the IDF.

Observations:

   •   Proactive Dental Intervention Program: Recognizing the impact of dental issues
       on deployment readiness, the IDF Medical Corps implemented a proactive dental
       intervention program for all activated combat-designated Reservists. The
       program aimed to identify and address potential dental emergencies before
       deployment, thereby maintaining combat effectiveness.

   •   Risk Stratification via Comprehensive Assessment: Upon activation, each
       Reservist undergoes a comprehensive clinical and radiographic dental exam

         the dental pulp, teeth in need of root canal treatment, hopeless teeth to be
         extracted, third molars to be extracted.

Overview
IDF Military Working Dogs (MWDs) were invaluable during the recent conflict, despite

                    Whole Blood Supply, Distribution and Storage

The Use of Whole Blood
The IDF demonstrated markedly improved casualty survival rates following the
implementation of whole blood administration in the field. Previously, IDF protocols for
volume resuscitation of bleeding casualties relied primarily on Freeze Dried Plasma
(FDP); however, outcomes have significantly improved with the shift to whole blood.
Before the Swords of Iron War, whole blood was limited to transport on 669th Squadron
aircraft and military ambulances in specific sectors. It is now available at the battalion
evacuation team level, and in some instances, even lower in the tactical echelon. The
IDF sources its blood supply from the Magen David Adom (MDA) National Blood
Service, a government-operated system serving both civilian, law enforcement, and
military needs.

Observations on the Magen David Adom (MDA) National Blood Service

   •   Facility Hardening and Capacity: The National Blood Service facility is hardened
       against missile and UAV attacks, featuring underground processing floors, a
       refrigerated vault with 30,000-unit storage capacity, and a four-day supply of
       water, food, and power.

   •   Collection and Distribution Volume: Average daily blood collection is 1,100 units,
       surging to 4,000 units per day for the weeks immediately following October 7 th.
       Regarding LTOWB, from October 2023 to October 2024, the MDA supplied 4,700
       units of LTOWB to the IDF, 3,100 units to other organizations, and 4,300 units to
       hospital trauma units.

   •   Collection Methods and Processing Time: 93% of blood is collected via mobile
       vans returning to MDA for processing, with the remaining 7% collected at MDA
       ambulance station donor rooms. Average processing time is 12 hours, including
       3 hours dedicated to West Nile Virus (WNV) testing for blood collected June 1st
       through December 1st. Following October 7th, the Ministry of Health authorized
       suspension of WNV testing due to the state of emergency and the low risk in
       October. During the peak times, units were processed and ready for shipment
       within 6 hours.

   •   Workforce and Operations: Rapid processing of blood was enabled by 24/7
       operations via a robust workforce of 1,500 employees and 25,000 volunteers
       during peak conflict intensity.

                          Subterranean (SubT) Environment

Overview
The urban environment consistently presents a significant subterranean (SutT) layer, a
reality demonstrated historically in cities like Warsaw and Vilnius during World War Two,
and currently in areas such as Mariupol and the Kursk region. Both defenders and
attackers are expected to utilize this complex environment in future conflicts. Recent
operations, particularly those conducted by Israel in Gaza, Lebanon, and Syria have
revealed the sophistication of purpose-built subterranean constructs.

In Gaza, Hamas has developed an extensive network, a 25-year project exceeding 460
miles (750 km) in length beneath a relatively small land area, encompassing not only
shelter, but also training sites, infiltration routes, command and control centers,
manufacturing facilities, and high-value intelligence locations.

The sheer scale of this network has required the IDF to employ both conventional
combat engineers and specialized units like YAHALOM, the IDF’s Special Operations
Engineering Unit. The complexities of the environment are such that several of the IDF’s
largest MASCAL incidents have taken place during operations to clear or neutralize
subterranean constructs.

Consequently, the U.S. Army should anticipate encountering significant adversary use
of either naturally occurring subterranean spaces within urban areas or deliberately
constructed subterranean systems in future operations. This reality introduces unique
medical challenges and substantially complicates casualty evacuation procedures,
demanding specific consideration in planning and preparation.

Observations:

   •   Medical and Psychological Challenges: The SubT environment presents unique
       and significant medical and psychological stressors. These spaces are typically
       characterized by narrow confines, high temperatures (reaching 40°C/104°F),
       elevated humidity, compete darkness, and poor ventilation. This combination
       creates hazardous atmosphere with potential for low oxygen levels, and
       dangerous concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO2), carbon monoxide (CO),
       hydrogen sulfide (H2S), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and other toxic gases. Resulting
       injuries commonly include heat stroke/exhaustion, electric shock, and trauma
       from collapse. Furthermore, Hamas and Hezbollah actively employ detection and


                                                                                        66
    surveillance devices, alongside along with IEDs, which restrict the types of
    equipment that can safely be deployed within the SubT environment.

•   Casualty Prevention: The IDF prioritizes casualty prevention when operating in
    SubT environments, employing a tiered approach to minimize Soldier exposure.
    SOPs, detailed in CALL’s, Initial Impressions Report: Israel-Hamas War
    Observations, emphasize avoiding direct tunnel entry whenever possible. Initial
    reconnaissance utilizes drones, robots, and then specially trained SubT working
    dogs equipped with low-light and infrared video capabilities. When entry is
    unavoidable, it is conducted at night using thermal vision and at a deliberately
    slow pace. YAHALOM routinely employs gas detectors for oxygen, carbon
    monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and explosive gases prior to entering SubT
    constructs, and will often refrain from entry if atmospheric conditions are unsafe.
    While these procedures are vital, operational demands for speed and surprise
    can occasionally necessitate their violation. The IDF is actively developing
    technological aids such as personal carbon monoxide (CO) level calculator app
    to monitor Soldier exposure. The IDF implements measures to mitigate heat
    stress, including authorizing the removal of non-essential equipment, even
    personal protective equipment, once a tunnel is secured. Fresh air and oxygen
    are supplied using compressors and air/oxygen balloons.


•   Casualty Care Protocols: IDF medical protocols within the SubT environment
    prioritize rapid casualty extraction, treating the space as a “care under fire”
    situation and focusing on rapid, life-saving interventions. In the event of a tunnel
    collapse, the initial response centers on establishing a small access tunnel to
    provide air and water to trapped Soldiers. Hypoxia is addressed with
    supplemental oxygen from portable sources, while carbon monoxide poisoning is
    treated with subsequent high-flow oxygen and hyperbaric chamber therapy. Heat
    stroke is currently managed with cooling towels dipped in ice water, though
    alternative techniques are under investigation. Cardiac arrest, particularly
    ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia resulting from
    electrocution, is treated with a specialized mini-AED, which only produces three
    shocks (defibrillations).

•   Specialized Equipment: To support operations and casualty care within the SubT
    environment, the IDF utilizes specialized equipment designed for portability and
    effectiveness. For example, YAHALOM units deploy sleds for horizontal casualty
    evacuation and winching systems for vertical extraction.



                                                                                      67
Recommendations:

  •   Medical Force Preparation: Given the potential scale and complexity of SubT
      operations, it is critical to proactively prepare medical assets for this unique
      environment. All medical personnel likely to support SubT operations should
      receive comprehensive familiarization training covering the specific challenges
      and requirements of this environment, including atmospheric hazards, limited
      maneuverability, and communication difficulties.

  •   Specialized Medical Training and Equipment: Medical assets likely to support
      SubT operations require specialized training and equipment focused on providing
      immediate life-saving treatment with a “care under fire” paradigm. Emphasis
      should be placed on rapid casualty assessment, stabilization, and evacuation
      from the tunnel environment. This necessitates proficiency in managing injuries
      specific to SubT operations – including heat stroke, hypoxia, carbon monoxide
      poisoning, and electrocution – as well as familiarity with specialized equipment
      like mini-AEDs and portable oxygen delivery systems.

  •   Collaboration with YAHALOM: To maximize preparedness and minimize risk, a
      robust collaboration should be established between U.S. Forces and YAHALOM.
      This partnership should focus on learning and implementing YAHALOM’s best
      practices for mitigating medical threats in SubT environments, encompassing
      preventative measures, casualty are protocols, and equipment utilization. This
      knowledge exchange will be invaluable in developing effective strategies for
      protecting U.S. Forces operating in similar conditions.

                        Victim and Killed in Action Identification

Overview
In the immediate aftermath of the October 7th attacks, victim identification presented
significant challenges, requiring the establishment of two victim identification facilities:
one operated by the police and another by the IDF. Soldiers identifiable in the field were
directed at the military facility. The majority of casualties were not immediately

identification facility, which also spearheaded the subsequent war crimes
documentation. While the scope of this study does not encompass the detailed
operations of the Police Victim Identification Facility, further research should include its
critical role. Nevertheless, from October 7th and throughout the Swords of Iron War, the
IDF heavily utilized its own Victim Identification Facility, operated by the IDF Rabbinic
Corps (Chaplaincy), alongside police efforts.

Observations:

   •   Core Responsibilities: The IDF Victim Identification Facility fulfilled several critical
       functions beyond simply identifying fallen personnel. Its key responsibilities
       included supporting the determination of the combat fatality ratio, assessing the
       effectiveness of combat field medical care, informing training goals for combat
       medical teams, and evaluating the performance of body armor against enemy
       ammunition.

   •   Forensic Examination Process: The IDF’s forensic examination process is a
       three-step procedure designed for efficiency and comprehensive data collection.
       This begins with completion of a standardized external examination form that
       meticulously records wounds, field medical interventions, and body armor
       performance. The next step is detailed documentation of wounds through
       photography and written descriptions. Lastly, a post-mortem CT scan is utilized
       for rapid assessment of injury mechanisms, largely replacing traditional autopsies
       except in limited circumstances. The results of this comprehensive examination
       process are utilized to drive adaptation and improvement in medical care.

   •   Personnel and Psychological Wellbeing: The forensic team was compromised of
       physician volunteers with prior trauma experience and a self-reported absence of
       PTSD. Recognizing the inherent psychological toll of this work, both the Police
       and IDF Victim Identification Facilities prioritized personnel wellbeing. Measures
       implemented included stringent odor control and cleaning protocols, workload

      optimization, shortened shifts, personnel rotation, proactive psychological
      support, and increased flexibility in work schedules.

Recommendations:

  •   Streamlined Transport and Facility Establishment: Given the anticipated high
      casualty rates in LSCO against a near-peer adversary, proactive planning and
      preparation of Mortuary Affairs capabilities are critical. Processes for rapid
      transport of human remains to appropriate Mortuary Affairs facilities must be
      streamlined. Planning should include the pre-identification of locations for
      establishing temporary Mortuary Affairs facilities and, if necessary, contingency
      plans for temporary burial sites.

  •   Personnel Augmentation and Storage Capacity: Mortuary Affairs capabilities
      should be augmented by a readily deployable, trained team capable of surging

  •   Forensic Capabilities and Infrastructure: Military morgues must be equipped with

      filter masks, enhanced ventilation, and rigorous cleaning protocols, is paramount
      at all facilities.

  •   Personnel Preparation and Support: Comprehensive planning for forensic
      processes across all operational scenarios is required. If medical personnel are
      anticipated to participate in forensic activities, psychological pre-screening
      (beyond self-reporting), adequate training, and ongoing psychological support
      must be provided.

  •   Prioritization and Workload Management: Clear priorities of work must be
      established for Mortuary Affairs teams, including contingencies for when

  •   Psychological Support for Forensic: Forensic teams must receive consistent and
      readily accessible psychological support throughout their involvement in casualty
      identification and processing.

                             Integration of Civilian Hospitals

Overview
Following the October 7th attacks, Israeli civilian hospitals became central to both
casualty management and medical logistics. In a potential near-peer LSCO involving
U.S. Forces, it is anticipated that military medical resources will be insufficient to fully
stabilize, treat, recover, and convalesce all injured personnel, necessitating the
utilization of civilian medical facilities. During the Swords of Iron War, 95% of casualties
were evacuated to civilian hospitals for advanced treatment within an average of 47
minutes. Notably, the IDF did not deploy any field hospitals and was therefore entirely
reliant on civilian medical infrastructure for casualty care. This reliance was only viable
due to the exceptionally rapid transport times from point of injury to Israeli civilian
hospitals.

Preparing Civilian Hospitals for Mass Casualty Events:

   •   Pre-Existing Infrastructure and Capacity: Major Israeli hospitals can relocate
       many of their operations, including surgery, into underground, hardened facilities.
       This process was routinely rehearsed prior to the October 7th attacks. This
       practice allows continued functionality even during periods of bombardment.
       Furthermore, Israel maintains a robust network of non-hospital emergency care
       centers (Terem Centers) designed to alleviate pressure on hospital emergency
       rooms during crisis.

   •   Established Relationships and Interoperability: Divisions and corps enhance
       interoperability of medical assets through established relationships and
       development of common operational plans. Major hospitals had pre-existing
       relationships with both 669th Squadron and Homefront Command medical units,
       which facilitate efficient patient transfer. In the months leading up to the war
       against Hezbollah in the north, comprehensive round tables and war gaming
       exercises involving all medical assets, including civilian organizations and
       hospitals, fostered trust, established a common operational language, and
       refined plans.

   •   Integrated Resupply Chain: The IDF leveraged this period to establish a robust
       medical resupply plan. Equipment and medical supply packages were provided
       to responding medical assets (e.g. medical companies or light evacuation teams)
       directly by the platform receiving casualty (typically rotary wing aircraft). Critically,


                                                                                             72
       civilian hospitals served as the origin point for this resupply chain due to their role
       as the ultimate recipient of casualties.

   •   Liaison and Coordination: While a reserve military team was already assigned to
       each hospital for liaison duties, these teams were scaled up and enhanced as
       the conflict progressed, strengthening coordination.


Civilian Hospitals in Practice: Initial Response and Refinement

   •   Initial Chaos and Rapid Adaptation: The initial transfer of casualties to civilian
       hospitals on October 7th to 8th was chaotic due to the sheer volume and lack of
       immediate organization.

   •   Specialized Care and Prioritization: The practice of distributing casualties across
       multiple hospitals enabled prioritization based on specialized medical
       capabilities, ensuring patients receive the most appropriate level of care.

   •   Communication Challenges and Solutions: Initial communication proved
       challenging as the IDF medical tablet system could only interface with IDF
       liaisons within the civilian hospital, not the hospital staff directly. Recognizing this
       limitation, a dedicated MEDEVAC liaison team was established to augment the
       existing IDF hospital liaison teams. The combined team, comprised of an Air
       Force operator, a military medical provider, and a Homefront Command liaison,
       established direct communication with civilian hospital staff, significantly
       streamlining casualty evacuation from the warzone to the civilian hospital.

Recommendations:

   •   Underground Facility Training Enhancement: Role II and III medical assets
       should incorporate the utilization of underground structures (e.g. parking lots) for
       medical care into training exercises. Collaboration with IDF medical corps
       personnel with experience setting up and conducting healthcare in underground
       hospitals, can facilitate refinement of these procedures.

   •   Civilian Hospital Liaisons: Establishing US liaisons with key host nation entities
       (Ministry of Health) and facilities (Rearward Medevac Hub [RM Hub]) are critical.
       While not necessarily a medical professional, this individual must be capable of
       tracking patients, coordinating with operational units, and Tricare/Internation SOS
       (iSOS). Identification and integration of these liaison activities into pre-war

                                                                                             73
    planning and exercises is essential. Consider expansion beyond current Joint
    Support Element Control (JSEC) Liaison Officer (LNO) planning at Rearward
    Movement (RM) hubs to include key non-hub civilian hospitals and surrounding
    areas.

•   European Theater Wartime Hospital Engagement: Establish agreements for U.S.
    casualty treatment in European civilian hospitals now to enable more accurate
    planning factors and convergence that results in deterrence.

•   Collaborative Training Program Implementation: Once wartime agreements are
    signed, implement a robust and ongoing training program to foster trust, establish
    a common operational language, and ensure understanding of procedures. This
    program should be regularly re-assessed, as hospital capabilities and personnel
    may change over time.

•   Joint MEDEVAC and CASEVAC Exercise Facilitation: Facilitate training
    opportunities for MEDEVAC assets (and supporting CASEVAC teams) to
    rehearse casualty transfer procedures at these civilian facilities.

•   Capacity Creation Model Exploration: Where feasible, collaborate with partners
    to identify and implement models for increasing civilian hospital capacity for
    military casualties, drawing inspiration from systems like Israel’s Terem Centers.

                            Lessons from October 7, 2023

Overview
On October 7, 2023, Hamas and its allies launched a surprise attack on Israel,
successfully employing a defeat mechanism referred to in U.S. doctrine as
“disintegration”. This involved a combined arms approach utilizing Special Operations
(SOF) units, electronic warfare (EW), drones, precision-guided munitions (PGMs), and
saturating fires. This offensive created a critical window of opportunity, effectively
eliminating Israel’s ability to employ its C4ISR capabilities and cross-domain
advantages, allowing them to overrun an area of more than 600 km2.
Prior to the attack, Israel, like the U.S. Army, was a maneuver-centric military reliant on
advanced communications and ISR infrastructure to maintain situational awareness and
regain the initiative after enemy action. Both Israel and the US have leveraged a cross-
domain, technology-heavy approach, trading weight for speed and mass for decisive
force. This reliance rendered Israel particularly vulnerable to disintegration tactics.
The immediate aftermath of the Hamas attacks offers valuable lessons for the U.S.
Army. An adversary engaging the U.S. Army understands the necessity of first
disrupting the U.S. C4ISR and cross-domain capabilities. This section will outline
lessons learned to help U.S. Army medical forces mitigate the danger of an enemy
attempting to employ disintegration, and to effectively react should such an attempt
occur.

Observations:

   •   Impact of Disintegration and Initial Chaos

       ➢ Hamas successfully disintegrated IDF C4ISR at all echelons, significantly
         delaying the IDF’s ability to organize a coherent response.

       ➢ The loss of communication infrastructure forced Israeli units to rely on ad hoc
         methods, such as cell phones, though many cell towers were disabled.

       ➢ Due to Hamas’ disruption of C4ISR, units operating in the area lacked
         situational awareness regarding the scale and scope of the enemy operation,
         complicating medical response efforts.

       ➢ Hamas established ambushes and roadblocks at critical road junctions,
         hindering overall response and specifically medical response.


                                                                                        75
    ➢ The overwhelming volume of emergency calls from civilians and military
      sources overloaded dispatch headquarters, exacerbating confusion.

•   Mission Complexity and Resource Challenges

    ➢ Hamas’ targeting of civilians necessitated a contested Non-Combatant
      Evacuation operation (NEO) which Israel had to conduct during ongoing
      combat. The simultaneous demands of re-establishing the border, defeating
      Hamas, rescuing trapped forces and civilians, and conducting a contested
      NEO created significant challenges in prioritizing medical resources.

    ➢ Soft-skinned ambulances faced a dilemma: entering the combat zone risked
      medical personnel becoming casualties due to a lack of protection, while
      remaining outside the contested area resulted in ambulances congregating at
      vulnerable locations under indirect fire. A tiered approach emerged where
      medical personnel entered the contested zone to evacuate casualties to
      these pooled ambulance locations for onward transport.

•   Interoperability and Coordination Issues

    ➢ The National Police Force, IDF, and other medical services lacked effective
      communication with each other.

    ➢ The initial medical response by IDF and the National Police Force was largely
      ad hoc, with significant organization of effort and forces not occurring until the
      subsequent days.

•   Adaptation and Innovation

    ➢ Numerous medical personnel from diverse organizations (IDF, Police Force,
      Unit 669, civilian organizations) established ad hoc roadside triage and
      casualty collection points, often utilizing local civilian medical supplies brought
      by the medical personnel themselves. Despite significant differences in
      protocols, equipment, and terminology, these teams quickly became effective
      in triaging, treating, and evacuating casualties to civilian medical facilities.

    ➢ As areas were secured, forward triage, treatment, and evacuation locations
      were established at central junctions.




                                                                                      76
      ➢ With limited air assets available, Unit 669 personnel proactively formed ad
        hoc “ground extraction teams” and self-deployed to the frontline where they
        established landing zones and directly coordinated with pilots. This initiative
        proved highly successful, leading to the full-scale integration of these teams
        at the Brigade level during the Swords of Iron War – a clear example of
        transformation in contact. Known as the “Black Cats,” these teams identify
        and assess Landing Zones (LZs) using video feeds on a secured network,
        transmitting this information directly to pilots, although on October 7th, this
        was via WhatsApp.

      ➢ Due to the significantly limited ground coordination, 669th Squadron frequently
        landed in areas with unknown casualty situations and sometimes faced
        combat while extracting casualties from “hot” landing zones. Their ability to
        operate in these conditions was credited with saving lives, highlighting the
        value of combining ground combat and evacuation capabilities and the ability
        to undertake complex and evolving missions during periods of chaos.

  •   Leadership and Transition Challenges

      ➢ Medical commanders were frequently engaged in casualty treatment rather
        than establishing command and control. While this was initially required as
        more forces entered the combat zone, not all commanders were able to
        refocus.

      ➢ On October 7th-8th, the transition of casualties to civilian hospitals was chaotic
        due to the lack of organization. A MEDEVAC team – comprised of an Air
        Force operator, a medical provider, and a Homefront Command liaison – was
        subsequently established to streamline communication with and evacuation to
        civilian hospitals.

Recommendations:

  •   Enhance C4ISR Resilience Training: Incorporate scenarios into training
      exercises that simulate C4ISR degradation and require medical forces to operate
      using alternate communication methods and procedures.

  •   Prioritize Ad-Hoc Team Training: Conduct regular training exercises focused on
      establishing and operating ad-hoc medical teams, emphasizing rapid decision-
      making, designation of a single on-the-ground medical operations lead with
      decision-making authority, and establishment of clear roles and responsibilities

                                                                                         77
    for all personnel. Training should also focus on empowering team members to
    perform their roles without unnecessary questioning of medical decisions.

•   Improve Interoperability: U.S. Army medical personnel must be trained on Joint,
    multinational, and civilian communication and interoperability procedures.
    Incorporate these elements into larger exercises and foster Medical Security
    Cooperation at the lowest levels. During this training, focus on establishment and
    understanding of common operating procedures and terminology.

•   Strengthen Civilian Liaison: Establish pre-planned mechanisms for liaison with
    civilian medical facilities, including dedicated personnel to track patients and
    facilitate communication with units and families.

•   Prioritize Basic Skills: Reinforce fundamental medical skills, particularly
    hemorrhage control and triage, to ensure proficiency under stress and resource
    constraints.

•   Formalize and Communicate Medical Rules of Engagement (MEDRoE): Medical
    assets must understand MEDRoE – both pre-conflict and during conflict – to
    operate effectively even with communication failures. Disseminate MEDRoE to
    the lowest levels and ensure thorough training.

•   Develop Black Cat Team Concepts: Explore the adaptation of the Israeli "Black
    Cat Team" model, focusing on rapid deployment of medical teams to establish
    landing zones and coordinate evacuations.
