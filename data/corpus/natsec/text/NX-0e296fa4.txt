Introduction
The U.S. Army currently faces a profound structural and operational shift under Army Structure
(ARSTRUC) 2028-2032. By centralizing critical combat enablers at the division level, this
reorganization strips brigade combat teams (BCTs) of their organic intelligence, maneuver, and
protection assets addressed in this article. While designed to optimize large-scale capabilities,
this realignment creates a dangerous support vacuum at the tactical edge. Recent observations
from the Joint Multinational Readiness Center (JMRC) clearly demonstrate the fallout: brigades
now suffer from degraded situational awareness, routinely stumble into un-shaped contact with
the enemy, and struggle to secure their own vulnerable rear areas.

This article equips Army leaders and staff officers across the battalion, brigade, and division
echelons with the immediate insights required to navigate this new operational reality. It
diagnoses the specific friction points crippling units during large-scale combat operations
(LSCO) training and, more importantly, delivers actionable best practices and new tactics,
techniques, and procedures (TTPs) to solve them.

By analyzing these JMRC observations, this article provides commanders and planners with a
blueprint to transition their formations from a state of vulnerable codependence to lethal
interdependence. Readers will learn exactly how divisions must proactively manage these new
dependencies—by integrating multi-discipline analysis teams (MDATs), taking absolute
ownership of the deep fight, and synchronizing rear-area security. Ultimately, this piece provides
the tactical force with the concrete solutions necessary to close the support gap and ensure our
brigades survive and win at the point of contact.

Military Intelligence
The ARSTRUC 2028-2032 realignment fundamentally alters the intelligence landscape by
removing the brigade's (BDE) organic fusion capabilities and creating a critical dependency on
the division (DIV).
Challenge: Loss of Organic Fusion

The reallocation of the brigade intelligence support element (BISE) to the division's intelligence
and electromagnetic warfare battalion (IEW BN) effectively removes the BDE's analytical
engine. The BDE no longer owns the assets required to analyze, synthesize, and exploit
information into timely, actionable intelligence. This structural change introduces an additional
echelon of separation between analysis and action, forcing the BDE into a state of heightened
interdependency where it must rely almost entirely on DIV-level analysis.

Impact: A Reactive and Blind Force

This support gap forces subordinate echelons into a reactive posture. JMRC observations
confirm that lack of synchronized DIV guidance blinds BDEs and battalions (BNs) to enemy
courses of action (ENY COAs). Furthermore, the lack of a standardized reporting format

between the DIV and BDE forces subordinate units to waste critical time and personnel
reformatting data, which introduces a high risk of misinterpretation and slows the entire
decision-making cycle—a fatal vulnerability in LSCO.

Solution: Proactive Division-Led Integration

Divisions must proactively bridge this intelligence gap through deliberate and persistent action.

   •   Integrate the MDAT: DIV G2 and IEW BN leadership must embed MDATs deeply within
       BDE training and planning cycles. This habitual relationship ensures MDATs understand
       BDE mission profiles and can tailor intelligence products accordingly. MDATs must
       integrate early in the BDE's planning cycle to effectively drive the information collection
       (IC) plan.
   •   Establish a clear processing, exploitation, and dissemination (PED) architecture:
       Commanders at all echelons must understand the intelligence capabilities at their
       disposal. Intelligence personnel must conduct comprehensive capability briefs to
       maneuver commanders, ensuring leaders know how to task and direct sensors. A clear,
       synchronized PED plan prevents the collection of vast amounts of unusable data and
       ensures that the right asset answers the right priority intelligence requirement (PIR).
   •   Aggressively overcommunicate: Unity of effort depends on a shared understanding of the
       battlefield. The DIV G2 must drive consistent communication among all intelligence
       nodes, reinforcing the common intelligence picture (CIP) and preventing the
       misalignment of collection assets. This aggressive overcommunication ensures
       intelligence flows rapidly from the DIV to the BDE and down to the forward-most
       sensor.

MDATs Trained to Support BDE Mission
BDEs must integrate MDATs into training cycles to establish multi-echelon SOPs and generate
actionable intelligence. This habitual relationship enables MDAT leadership within the IEW BN
to fundamentally understand maneuver BDE mission profiles. The rapid introduction of
technological transitions1 created planning friction. The accelerated distribution of next-
generation equipment to unit task organizations resulted in a lack of clarity regarding the task,
purpose, and end-state of intel sensors at echelon.

For instance, JMRC observed specialized intel teams equipped with next-generation platforms
like GHOST-X, multi-function reconnaissance company (MFRC), MDAT, and electronic warfare
(EW) teams deploying without a synchronized PED plan. Rotational training units (RTU)
collected vast amounts of data in the BDE deep area, forwarding it to the DIV G2 to drive
targeting. The newly formed, understaffed G2 omitted an exploitation cell during planning,
leaving a critical gap in translating EW data into targetable intelligence. Examples like this

Synchronizing Intelligence at Echelon
Properly aligning intelligence nodes to support the BDE mission directly increases the
commander's situational understanding of enemy COAs, driving lethality at echelon. Unity of
effort", a foundational concept within the joint community, relies on a shared understanding and
the synchronization of efforts to achieve a common objective, even outside traditional command
and control hierarchies. In a constantly evolving battlespace, the key to unity of effort is the
aggressive overcommunication of information. Consistent communication among intelligence
nodes (primarily driven by the DIV staff) reinforces shared understanding and prevents the
misalignment of collection assets across the division's subordinate commands. As demonstrated,
the lines of communication flow from the DIV G2 down to the BDEs, and subsequently to
forward intelligence nodes on the forward line of own troops (FLOT), such as MFRC and
MDATs. In 2026, situations identified during two major exercises at JMRC where the MFRC and
MDATs could not verify if bottom-up refinement occurred. This timely information was key for
DIV situational understanding of ENY COAs but remained with the FLOT and was not

Maneuver: Restoring the Disruption Zone
The modern battlefield reveals a critical breakdown in synchronization between DIV and BDE
maneuver elements, characterized by the collapse of the BDE's disruption zone.

Challenge: Collapse of the BDE Disruption Zone

The removal of the cavalry squadron from the BDE without a corresponding replacement of
reconnaissance and security capabilities creates a dangerous operational gap. BDEs no longer
have the organic assets to shape their own deep area, placing the entire burden of disrupting the
enemy onto the division. This structural change fundamentally compresses the BDE
commander's decision-making space.
Impact: Un-Shaped Contact and Transferred Risk

A compressed decision space at the BDE level has cascading consequences. When a BDE
commander cannot effectively transition from the disruption zone to the battle zone, that risk
passes directly down to the battalions. This forces BN commanders to make critical decisions
while fighting an unshaped enemy in a high-risk support zone not of their own making. This
reality places the burden squarely on the DIV to mandate and maintain a robust disruption zone.

Solution: Division Ownership of Shaping the Brigade Fight

To prevent catastrophic tactical failure, the division must assume full ownership of shaping the
brigade fight.

   •   Mandate a security area: The DIV commander must use their assets, unmanned aircraft
       systems (UAS), reconnaissance surveillance target acquisition (RSTA) to establish and
       maintain a robust deep security area, typically in the 10-30 km band. This effort must
       attrit and disrupt the enemy, setting favorable conditions for the BDE's close fight.
   •   Define clear task, purpose, and end-state (TPE): The DIV must provide exceptionally
       clear TPE directives for all echeloned assets operating between the DIV and BDE. This
       ensures a seamless transition of responsibility and prevents the creation of dangerous
       seams in the security architecture.
   •   Embed DIV enablers (CEMA, EW, GEOINT, SIGINT, HUMINT) in the maneuver
       battalions near the FLOT mitigating the BDE support vacuum.

Risk Within DIV Battlefield Geometry
A direct consequence of increasing the BN frontage and depth is the critical risk of making un-
shaped contact with the enemy's main body. This reality places the responsibility on the DIV to
mandate and maintain a robust disruption zone or deep security area. Failing to attrit and disrupt
the deep-terrain enemy at the DIV level transfers tactical risk down to BDEs and BNs. Figure 2

Systemic Failure of CEMA and Intelligence Dissemination

The third major friction point is the systemic failure of the intel and CEMA architecture to
disseminate actionable, maneuver-focused intel down to the BN level. Bridging this gap requires
strict adherence to unit integration, which is paramount for mission success in a complex
electromagnetic environment.

Protection
The BCT transformation places an unavoidable burden on the DIV to provide comprehensive
protection, requiring an empowered leader to synchronize survivability operations.

Challenge: Loss of Organic Protection Assets

With the loss of the Brigade Engineer Battalion (BEB), BCTs now lack the organic headquarters
and enablers to manage and protect their own rear areas, command posts, and logistical nodes.
The burden of planning and requesting for forces (RFFs) for security now falls on a BDE staff
not designed for the task, while the DIV owns the very assets the BDE requires.

Impact: A Fragmented and Vulnerable Rear

This disjointed command and support relationship forces the DIV into a reactive protection
posture. Without a designated commander for the BDE's rear area, units experience a
fragmented, uncoordinated security effort, creating vulnerabilities for high-value targets. This
leadership gap slows tempo and spikes risk during complex operations.

Solution: Empowered and Centralized Synchronization

The DIV must take control of this co-dependent relationship to allow its subordinate BDEs to
focus their combat power forward.

   •   Define command and support relationships: The DIV must formally define the command
       and support relationships for all ADA and MP assets operating in the rear area. This
       ensures unity of effort and provides subordinate units with a shared understanding of the
       threat and the security plan.
   •   Maintain an open dialogue on risk: The BDE and DIV must maintain an open and honest
       dialogue about risk. If a BDE lacks the combat power to secure its rear, the DIV must be
       prepared to either allocate additional forces via RFF or assume responsibility for a larger
       portion of the battlespace by shifting boundaries.
   •   Have a Chief of Protection: The DIV must clearly define the role and responsibilities in
       lieu of BEB inactivation and grant that individual or entity the necessary authority to
       resource and synchronize complex, multi-faceted missions on behalf of the commander.

To support the ongoing transformation in Europe, the JMRC higher control (HICON) cell has
identified a critical need for units to manage the complex relationships between DIV and BDE
levels. Effectively controlling these inter-dependent and co-dependent relationships is essential
for supporting subordinate commands as they shape the close fight. In reference to the protection
WfF, training units have experienced three main challenges: protecting the rear area and
command posts, UAS management within the rear areas, and coordinating resources for wet gap
crossings (WGXs).

Mitigation Strategy
To ensure unity of effort, the division must formally define command and support relationships
for all ADA and MP assets in the rear area. Simultaneously, the DIV G2 must produce and
disseminate timely, rear-area threat analysis to establish a shared understanding across all
echelons. BDE then refines this intelligence through their own analysis and communicates
bottom-up refinements to the DIV G2. BDEs must assess their available combat power and
allocate forces for rear-area security. If requirements at the forward edge of the battle area
prevent a BDE from securing its own rear, both units must coordinate through the RFF process
and expand the DIV rear area to shorten the BDEs battlespace. This proactive coordination
provides an accurate operational picture, allowing the DIV to support subordinate BDE so they
can focus combat power forward to shape the close fight.

BCT Reconnaissance and Rear Area Challenges

The loss of the BEB has also resulted in the reorganization of the military intelligence (MI)
companies under the DIV IEW BN stripped BCTs of organic tactical unmanned aircraft systems
(TUAS). To compensate, proactive BCTs now field long and medium range reconnaissance
(LRR/MRR) platforms during rotations, integrating former TUAS platoons into new
                                                8
multifunctional reconnaissance companies (MFRCs). This transition creates "growing pains,"
specifically regarding equipment fielding and the BDE S2’s new responsibility to manage the IC
plan, a task previously handled by the BEB’s MICO. While BDE S2 sections successfully nest
their IC plans with DIV, they often prioritize threats near the FLOT and neglect the rear area.
Despite the conventional focus of LSCO, the S2 and S3 must remain proficient in producing a
rear-area common intelligence picture (CIP) to maintain shared situational awareness across the
entire battlespace.

DIV typically delegates rear-area security to a maneuver enhancement brigade or engineer BN,
but BCTs now lack a dedicated headquarters for this mission. Consequently, BCTs must submit
RFFs or negotiate boundary shifts to expand the DIV rear area and reduce their own battlespace.
Currently, many BCTs simply choose to assume risk in the rear. As a solution to ensure
responsive security, the force must assign a headquarters to rear security for the purpose of
transforming this reactive, codependent process into an interdependent system built on timely
threat analysis.

Coordinating Resources for WGXs

The third challenge is unique in the sense that it has only occurred in the constructive virtual
environment at JMRC, as WGXs are a DIV level and above mission set. BCTs are solely
dependent on DIV to plan and resource a WGX. Since the DIV does not clearly define or
empower the Chief of Protection, no single leader synchronizes the engineers, air defense
artillery (ADA), MPs, and fires necessary for complex WGXs. This institutional friction slows
the tempo of the operation and significantly increases risk to forces at the point of crossing. It
prevents the DIV from effectively setting conditions for subordinate BNs and BDEs.

The DIV must rectify this by clearly defining the role and responsibilities of a Chief of
Protection and granting that role—whether a designated individual or a delegated duty—the
necessary authority to resource, synchronize, and oversee complex, multi-faceted missions on
behalf of the commander. Mastering this interdependent relationship through a single,
empowered leader allows the DIV to effectively set conditions for its subordinate BDEs,
enabling them to successfully cross obstacles and continue to shape the close fight on the far
side.

Conclusion
The success of the Army's ongoing structural transformation in the European theater hinges
entirely on the DIVs ability to master its new, complex codependences with subordinate BDEs.
Under ARSTRUC 2028-2032, the transformation of the BCT fundamentally altered battlefield
geometry, deliberately transferring critical capabilities from the BDE to the DIV. This shift
created a rigid operational architecture where the BDE is now fundamentally dependent on the
DIV to shape the battlespace. When the DIV struggles to seamlessly fulfill these obligations, it

Addressing these structural and procedural deficiencies is no longer a mere matter of
organizational optimization; it is a fundamental operational imperative. The defined allocation of
task organization and TPE mandates from the DIV is the only mechanism that can bridge these
gaps. Ultimately, the ability of ground force commanders to make sound, rapid decisions
depends entirely on the DIVs capacity to synchronize these critical WfF functions. By actively
managing intelligence dissemination, shaping the deep maneuver fight, and assuming the burden
of comprehensive protection, the DIV effectively eliminates the support vacuum. These
adaptations are the essential prerequisites for setting the conditions for subordinate units to
survive, allowing maneuver BDEs to focus their full combat power on winning the close fight
and achieving mission success.
