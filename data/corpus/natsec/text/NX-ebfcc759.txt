                                      ACCELERATING MULTI-DOMAIN INTEROPERABILITY

The Department of War (DoW) and the U.S. Army are currently executing a paradigm-shifting
acquisition and operational strategy known as the Right to Integrate (R2I). Historically, the
defense enterprise has fielded disconnected, proprietary technology platforms that placed the
immense burden of system integration directly on the warfighter at the tactical edge. This
fragmentation has resulted in severe cognitive overload and fatal capability gaps, such as the
inability to rapidly synthesize data to counter drone swarms. To resolve this, the Army launched
Operation Jailbreak, a massive hackathon sprint with industry partners. The event successfully
resulted in defense vendors making their Application Programming Interfaces (API) available for
integration. 1

By mandating a Modular Open Systems Approach (MOSA) and leveraging AI-enabled
integration software like Anduril’s Lattice, the enterprise has successfully merged disparate
radars, sensors, and effectors into a unified common operating picture (COP). 2 Crucially, recent
divisional-scale exercises with the 4th Infantry Division (4ID) have proven that this next-
generation command and control (NGC2) architecture can be operated, managed, and expanded
directly by Soldiers in the field. Institutionalizing these open architectures and establishing a 30-
day software deployment pipeline is critical for achieving multi-domain decision dominance and
securing the joint force against modern autonomous threats.

The top five lessons learned insights from this document are:

    •   Tactical Integration Burden Shift: The R2I initiative eliminates proprietary data silos,
        freeing Soldiers from manually synthesizing data across multiple screens and
        significantly reducing cognitive overload during complex engagements.
    •   Establishing a DoW API Marketplace: The enterprise is replacing antiquated interface
        documents with modern APIs, proving that industry partners will willingly expose system
        interfaces to a collaborative marketplace when their intellectual property is protected.
    •   AI-Enabled COP: By using open architectures like Anduril’s Lattice to merge more than
        74 distinct systems, commanders can now leverage machine learning to rapidly filter
        targets and pair them with cost-effective kinetic and non-kinetic effectors.
    •   Soldier-Driven Network Ownership: Scaled exercises with 4ID proved that Soldiers
        can independently manage the NGC2 network, provisioning nodes and developing their
        own tactical applications directly at the edge.
    •   Rapid 30-Day Deployment and Cyber Realities: The Army has replaced multi-year
        fielding timelines with a rapid 30-day software deployment cycle, though sustaining this
        pace requires continuous Red Team penetration testing to prevent adversarial network
        exploitation.

THE INTEROPERABILITY CRISIS AT THE TACTICAL EDGE

For decades, Army modernization efforts and acquisition pipelines have inadvertently created a
persistent operational vulnerability: the extreme difficulty of integrating various technological
platforms into a cohesive network. Historically, acquisition programs required vendors to adhere
to highly detailed, antiquated, and military-specific interface control documents (ICDs). For
example, legacy requirements often mandated physical serial interfaces documented in
exhaustive, 700-plus-page manuals that relied on brittle bit-stream mapping. Furthermore,
systems were frequently classified at their inception, a practice that severely restricted the ability
of engineers to use modern, agile software development practices.

The operational consequences of this hardware-centric, siloed approach have been severe and, in
some cases, lethal. Soldiers and tactical formations were forced to act as their own system
integrators once they and their equipment reached the battlefield. Operators attempting to
counter complex, modern threats such as low-flying drone swarms or loitering munitions were
routinely overwhelmed by the need to manually synthesize data. Inside a standard tactical
operations center (TOC), a single Soldier might be forced to monitor eight or nine separate
screens tied to disparate command and control (C2) systems, cross-referencing isolated data
feeds in their head before making a life-or-death engagement decision.

This severe cognitive overload directly degrades combat effectiveness. The lack of native
interoperability between critical radars, sensors, and interceptors caused a fatal delay in March
2025 when a low-flying adversarial drone evaded detection and struck a tactical operations
center in Kuwait. 3 Resolving this life-threatening capability gap required immediate,
unconventional action by the enterprise to eliminate the lethal cost of proprietary vendor lock-in.

OPERATION JAILBREAK AND THE R2I HACKATHON SPRINT

Recognizing that traditional acquisition timelines were too slow to address these battlefield
realities, the service launched the R2I hackathon sprint. 4 Colloquially termed Operation
Jailbreak, this initiative challenged the defense industrial base to unlock and expose the
interfaces of their technology platforms, allowing the Army to create new digital pathways or
open existing ones that had been heavily restricted.

unmatched capacity if leveraged correctly. “We, the United States, can do it differently than

The sprint convened with 600 participants representing 50 companies at Fort Carson, Colorado.
Heavyweight traditional prime contractors—including Boeing, General Dynamics, L3Harris,
Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, and RTX—worked alongside modern, software-first tech
entrants like Anduril, Palantir, Perennial Autonomy, and Picogrid. 7

Crucially, this initiative was built on a foundation of intellectual property (IP) protection and
shared strategic investment. Participating companies signed a formal statement of support for the
Army’s integration efforts for current and future systems, executing the hackathon sprint at zero
additional cost to the Army. As Army Chief Technology Officer Dr. Alex Miller noted, the
government explicitly avoided appropriating proprietary vendor IP. “We don’t want your
intellectual property — that’s yours,” Miller assured vendors. 8 Instead, the focus was entirely on
establishing and documenting the API pathways through which systems communicate.

TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE: MANDATING MOSA AND THE DOW API
MARKETPLACE

The technical execution of the R2I sprint relied on a hard pivot away from legacy interfaces. By
asking vendors to build new remote procedure calls (RPCs) and document modern APIs, the
service effectively laid the groundwork for the War Department’s first-ever marketplace for
APIs.

Dr. Miller emphasized that in the commercial tech sector, open interfaces have driven the cost of
software integration to near zero, a standard the Army must now enforce. “MOSA has shifted
from a verb—how we do things—to a noun,” Dr. Miller stated. 9 He declared that exposing
interfaces and providing thorough documentation is now the “industry’s ticket to participate,”
particularly for autonomous systems and counter-unmanned aircraft system (C-UAS) platforms.

To facilitate this massive integration effort, the Fort Carson sprint used a centralized digital
“game board.” This provided a level playing field in which engineers from competing companies
could visualize the broader architecture, see where their systems fit, and collaborate dynamically.
This environment fostered a healthy sense of competition while being supported by key
enterprise assets. Engineers were flanked by the Army Software Factory, which assisted with
rapid code deployment, and had direct access to every echelon of Army leadership—from
program managers to the acquisition executive and the Secretary himself—to instantly clear
bureaucratic roadblocks.

SOFTWARE-DEFINED WARFARE: NGC2 AT SCALE

The most significant tactical victory of Operation Jailbreak was the successful ingestion of
disparate hardware feeds into a centralized, open architecture. During the sprint, industry
engineers successfully merged isolated data feeds from more than 74 distinct legacy and modern
systems into Anduril’s Lattice software platform. Lattice synthesizes disparate telemetry, radar
tracks, and optical feeds into a single, unified COP resembling the intuitive interface of Google
Maps. 11

At Ivy Mass, 4ID proved that an open-architecture data mesh can hold under the pressure of
divisional operations. The network fundamentally shifted from being vehicle-centric to Soldier-
centric. Previously, nodes on the mesh were restricted to computers mounted in vehicles and
command posts. During this scale-up, Lattice was fielded onto the tablets and end-user devices
that Soldiers physically carry, growing from roughly 10 connected devices to more than 2,500. 14
Every individual Soldier with a device became a live node on the same data layer as major
command posts and advanced weapon systems. Simultaneously, the number of tactical edge
computers (TECs)—deployable compute systems extending the network’s reach into the
formation—doubled to more than 130 nodes.

This expanded scale also integrated a vast array of new combat capabilities. At Ivy Mass, the
team successfully onboarded 17 new applications and 11 new sensors, bringing the network
totals to 40 distinct applications, 36 data feeds, and 37 effectors seamlessly communicating
across nine different hardware form factors. 15 The Lattice architecture created a resilient “data
lake for the future,” allowing commanders to evaluate multi-domain targeting options and task
autonomous effectors instantly, without the friction of manual data re-entry.

Most critically, Ivy Mass demonstrated a transition of ownership. The Army took over the
operation of the NGC2 mesh directly. Soldiers provisioned new nodes via Anduril’s one-click
peering function and managed their own software updates across all 2,500 end-user devices
using a centralized fleet manager tool. Furthermore, because Lattice is a modular, open
architecture, 4ID Soldiers began developing and deploying their own plug-ins. In a milestone
achievement, one Soldier used AI coding tools to independently build Circle-X, a vehicle
maintenance application that writes data directly into the Army’s enterprise logistics system
(GCSS-Army). This proves that NGC2 is no longer just an industry prototype; it is resilient
infrastructure that the division can operate, adapt, and expand on its own. 16

MULTI-DOMAIN AND ALLIED INTEGRATION

The implications of this scalable, software-defined integration are actively revolutionizing
autonomous warfare across all domains. In the maritime environment, the Royal Australian Navy
has begun operating the Ghost Shark, an extra-large autonomous underwater vehicle (XL-AUV),
powered by the Lattice open architecture, for deep-water survey and intelligence gathering. 17

In the expeditionary domain, U.S. Special Operations Command (SOCOM) uses these open
architectures for highly mobile C2 in austere, communications-denied environments. 18 By
relying on software that can dynamically integrate whatever sensors are locally available, special
operators maintain situational awareness without heavy logistical footprints.

Furthermore, the Army is testing these open-architecture concepts alongside the North Atlantic
Treaty Organization (NATO) allies in Eastern Europe through Project Flytrap. This joint exercise
is specifically focused on defeating hostile drone swarms, proving that software
interoperability—fueled by the R2I mandate—is the foundational linchpin for coalition warfare
and future joint-force operations. The broader homeland defense applications are already visible.
U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) currently employs Lattice to automate hundreds of
Sentry Towers along the southern border for persistent surveillance. 19

PERSISTENT OPERATIONAL GAPS AND CYBER HYGIENE

Although the initial integration initiatives and the scaling at Ivy Mass proved highly successful,
the rapid pace of “jailbreaking” exposes several enduring vulnerabilities in the military
modernization framework that must be carefully managed, including:

     •   The Challenge of Legacy Vendor Lock-In: Proprietary vendor lock-in remains a
         significant hurdle because legacy contracts do not inherently mandate open data
         architectures. This allows companies to revert to proprietary silos once immediate
         operational pressures subside. To counter this, future acquisition strategies must
         permanently institutionalize open API requirements.
     •   Network Security and Vulnerability Exposure: Rapidly bypassing software restrictions
         across multiple legacy systems creates unverified network connections that cyber
         adversaries could exploit. To counter this during the initial sprint, the Army Software
         Factory and Army Cyber Red Team were integrated directly onto the “game board”
         alongside vendor engineers to ensure immediate cyber hygiene and secure coding
         practices. 20 However, as the mesh scales to thousands of end-user nodes, continuous Red
         Team validation remains a critical gap that must be institutionalized across the force.
     •   Algorithmic Trust and Opaque Machine Learning: Trusting automated platforms to
         filter high-value targeting data creates a dangerous dependency on opaque machine
         learning algorithms. Operators must be trained to understand how these platforms
         prioritize threats, ensuring that human-on-the-loop safeguards are maintained to prevent
         catastrophic errors.

STRATEGIC PATHWAYS FORWARD: THE 30-DAY PIPELINE

To ensure the U.S. Army remains agile against rapidly evolving autonomous threats,
procurement regulations must immediately institutionalize open data standards. All future sensor,
radar, and interceptor contracts must include open APIs as a baseline deliverable to ensure
systems are integration-ready upon delivery, permanently ending the era of the 737-page serial
interface document.

Operationally, the enterprise is establishing a rapid deployment pipeline based on the successes
of the Fort Carson sprint and 4ID’s exercises. Following the conclusion of a hackathon, the
service now executes a 30-day continuous integration/continuous deployment (CI/CD) cycle.
This 30-day window is used to harden the code, conduct rigorous regression testing, and push the
newly integrated software updates directly to forward-deployed units.

“We have already been pushing updates from here to Central Command,” Dr. Miller reported,
noting that the first systems jailbroken were core C2 platforms tied to C-UAS radars and
cameras. 21 Moving forward, events like Project Convergence Capstone 6 (PCC6) will
demonstrate NGC2 to the wider force in joint and coalition scenarios.

By aggressively enforcing R2I, capitalizing on scalable platforms like Lattice, and maintaining a
strict 30-day operational deployment cycle, the DoW will cement multi-domain decision
dominance and ensure warfighters never again serve as their own system integrators on the
battlefield.
