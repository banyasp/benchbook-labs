Defending American Information; US Communications
Security Policy since 1945
Center For Cryptologic History

Introduction
Policy affecting the security of US government communication systems has, since 1945, been directed by
the President, though the work itself is accomplished through boards and committees at the immediate
cabinet or sub-cabinet level. These executive orders, directives and memoranda are generally non-
political and reflect the government’s efforts to respond to technological changes and address both
contemporary and foreseen threats to American national security.

Though the precise words have changed over time, the Secretary of Defense (now SECWAR) has, since
1952, held the responsibility to protect US information affecting national security. Since NSA’s creation,
the NSA Director has executed these responsibilities for SECDEF. Yet DIRNSA’s authorities to act in
defense of what are today known as National Security Systems (NSS) have only started to expand.

US policy on defense of government communications has evolved in accordance with the explosive
growth of computer-based communications and information technology, and the US government and
society’s reliance on it. Within the executive branch, new organizations have been added and there are
more seats at the table, yet many of the original departments still remain at the forefront of information
and communications security policy; the Department of War, the Intelligence Community, the National
Security Council and the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs (APNSA).



Getting organized: 1945-1968
July 3, 1945: Executive Order on Cryptographic Security
The first national – level directive on securing US communications was a classified executive order issued
by President Harry Truman, three months after being sworn in and just weeks before the end of World
War II. Truman’s executive order established a “Cryptographic Security Board” composed of the
Secretaries of State, War, and Navy. Their charge was straightforward; study the cryptographic (code)
systems in use across the government, establish standards and policies, and issue the implementing
directives.

Despite the executive order, it doesn’t seem that the cryptographic security board ever took hold as a
functioning body. However, cryptography, considered as one element of communications security
(COMSEC), carried on within the military services after the war. Army, Navy, and after 1947, Air Force
COMSEC were eventually consolidated under the Armed Forces Security Agency, or AFSA. AFSA was an
immediate predecessor to the National Security Agency, and had been established by Secretary of
Defense Louis Johnson in 1949 as an attempt to consolidate both COMSEC and communications
intelligence (COMINT) under a single, unified organization. While it had some success, AFSA left a
perception that the US COMINT effort remained inefficient and underachieving. This led, by 1952, to the
Brownell Committee, charged with investigating and proposing corrective measures to US
communications intelligence.



Truman Memorandum of October 24, 1952; NSC memorandum of October 28,
1952
Focused as it was on communications intelligence, the Brownell Committee report of 1952 had little to
say about cryptography, beyond their view that “true unification and centralization of the Government’s
cryptographic activities was achieved by the creation of the Armed Forces Security Agency (AFSA) in
1949.” Cryptography was working – but that was at the operational level, not a policymaking one.

Discussions among the NSC and the White House following the Brownell report resulted in a new
directive from President Truman, though it didn’t represent a real change in course. On October 24, 1952
– the same day he signed the memorandum to create the National Security Agency – Truman signed a
second memo on communications security (COMSEC). This stated that classified Federal
telecommunications were a national responsibility, policies and procedures had to be integrated, and
cooperation among all government agencies was essential.

To carry out this order, the President established a Special Committee of the NSC for COMSEC matters. It
was a committee of two (Sec State, SECDEF), who were to formulate policy for “high and uniform
standards of communications security throughout the government.” They were also directed to create a
US Communications Security Board. This was followed by an NSC memorandum on October 28, which
named SECDEF as the executive agent for COMSEC across the government. The NSA Director DIRNSA was
made the “executing agent,” with specified responsibilities and tasks to perform on SECDEF’s behalf.

October 20, 1953: National Security Council Directive 168 and 5711
The NSC followed up almost exactly one year later with Directive 168. This established the United States
Communications Security Board (USCSB), which acted for and under the Special Committee of SECSTATE
and SECDEF. USCSB membership included the Secretary of State, SECDEF, Secretary of the Treasury, FBI
Director, Secretaries of Army, Navy and Air Force, the Director of Central Intelligence/DCI, DIRNSA, and
the chair of the Atomic Energy Commission.

NSC 168 further defined the top-level roles and responsibilities for COMSEC. DOD was named the
executive agency of the government for all COMSEC matters, with “centralized, over-all cognizance” of
COMSEC used by the government.

NSC 168 was followed up by NSC 5711 in 1957. This contained nearly identical language, but with two
noteworthy exceptions:

    •   It included an exemption for CIA activities.

    •   It removed NSC 168’s stipulation that the DIRNSA would act under the direction of the Joint
        Chiefs of Staff. This was important, as it maintained DIRNSA’s autonomy as a direct subordinate
        to SECDEF; similar to his position in communications intelligence.
In August 1968, the NSC rescinded Directive 5711 and reissued a new Communications Security
Directive, but without any substantive changes.

Cold War influences: 1970s to the early 1980s
Throughout much of the 1970s, cryptographic and communications security of US communications was
not structurally altered or addressed at the executive level. Security was, for the most part, a matter of
survivability and resiliency during and after a nuclear attack by the USSR. However, there were several
very specific directives issued in response to Soviet espionage efforts against the US government and
defense industry, particularly in the Washington area. All of these concerned efforts to protect
communications pathways against Soviet eavesdropping, primarily through moving microwave
communications to landlines.

November 16, 1977: Presidential Directive/NSC-24:
Significant changes to US communications security policy arrived in the form of the then-classified and
restricted PD/NSC-24. This directive split responsibility for US government COMSEC for the first time.

The SECDEF retained his role for communications security for classified information and unclassified
national security information.

The Department of Commerce now took over communications protection for government derived
unclassified information, and was directed to engage with the commercial and private sector to enhance
communications protection and privacy.

Defense would provide non-cryptographic protection for unclassified government information, while
Commerce was given the responsibility to apply commercial cryptography to government information.
PD-24 acknowledged that there would likely be overlap between these two areas, and any issues would
be reviewed “case by case.”

PD-24 abolished the US Communications Security Board. It did not name a direct successor, but did
create a permanent interagency group, led by the Department of State, to scrutinize any future attempts
by the USSR and other Communist countries to acquire real estate in the United States. This group also
included the Executive Office of the President, the DCI, DOD, NSA and the FBI.

This directive also put telecommunications protection and policy in the hands of the NSC’s Special
Coordination Committee. That committee, in turn, created a subcommittee led by the White House
Office of Science and Technology Policy (OSTP), with members from State, Treasury, Justice, Commerce,
Defense, Transportation, Energy, CIA, GSA, NSA and the NSC staff. They were to define techniques to
protect government systems and offer maximum assistance to the private sector.

What remained constant was that all agencies were still held ultimately responsible for themselves, to
“conduct their communications and emanations security activities as they see fit subject to the
provisions of law.”

November 15, 1979: Presidential Directive/NSC-53:
This directive acknowledged the federal government’s reliance on commercial communications
networks. It asserted that the government’s primacy in recovering services after a crisis, and to help
improve network reliability and recovery time, it called for interconnection and interoperability among
interstate common carrier networks. It also directed the Federal Emergency Management Agency
(FEMA) to develop plans for the use of private line communications with significant capacity, such as
those connecting railroads, airlines and pipelines.

Adapting to Technology: 1980s into the 1990s
Partnerships with the private sector fully emerged in the 1980s, as the US government began to
recognize the true potential of disruptions to communications networks, as information and
communications technology began to converge. National Security Systems (NSS) were identified for the
first time, and the NSA Director (DIRNSA) was given a role as the National Manager for NSS.

December 4, 1981: Executive Order 12333
Executive Order 12333 is a foundational document for the US intelligence community. Although focused
on intelligence collection, it also affirmed the SECDEF’s role as executive agent for Communications
Security (COMSEC), as well as DIRNSA’s role in executing SECDEF’s COMSEC responsibilities.

September 13, 1982: Executive Order 12382
This executive order established the President’s National Security Telecommunications Advisory
Committee, or NSTAC, to advise on implementing the measures called for in NSC-53, and to propose
measures to improve telecommunications for national security. It is still active today as an element of
the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA).

June 13, 1983: National Security Decision Directive 97: National Security
Telecommunications Policy
NSDD-97 replaced PD-53, though it maintained a focus on resiliency and reconstitution with private
industry participation via the NSTAC. It set out a policy to enhance the national telecommunications
infrastructure during international tension, threat of war, wartime, and during the restoration of critical
national functions.

The directive called for a survivable and enduring national telecommunications capability composed of a
combination of government, commercial and private facilities, located outside of likely nuclear targets
where possible, and with mechanisms in place to reestablish itself.

It stated that the national telecommunications infrastructure must possess certain characteristics:
“connectivity, redundancy, interoperability, restorability and hardness.” Planning had to address
requirements for national security leadership – gathering intelligence, conducting diplomacy, ensuring
military command and control, and providing for continuity of government.

Finally, NSDD-97 created a Steering Group chaired by the APNSA to oversee and implement the directive;
it included the Director, OSTP, the Executive Agent of the National Communication System, and the
associate director of OMB for National Security and International Affairs.
September 17, 1984: National Security Decision Directive 145 (NSDD 145)
This directive recognized, for the first time, the foreign intelligence threat to US information systems, and
attempted to give the Department of Defense and the National Security Agency much broader authority
to defend all US government-wide communications and information technology systems.

NSDD 145 also noted – but did not define - an additional category of “unclassified but sensitive”
information. Previous directives and legislation up to this point had only recognized information as
classified or administrative.

The expanded role for NSA did not sit well with many in Congress, who took action to roll it back in the
1987 Computer Security Act. This legislation limited NSA’s information security role to national security
systems, and made it an advisor to the National Bureau of Standards on protecting sensitive but
unclassified information.

July 5, 1990: National Security Directive 42
NSD 42 was issued to clarify previous policy statements under a single document. It began by stating that
the growth of information technology and the convergence of communications and information systems
presented the United States with the opportunity for improved efficiency and a heightened risk of
technical exploitation. It reestablished a Policy Coordinating Committee to oversee its implementation,
and created the NSTISSC (National Security Telecommunications and Information Systems Security
Committee). This was a body to develop standards, operating policies and procedures, examine technical
issues, and to prepare and disseminate technical instructions at a national level. It was chaired by the
Assistant Secretary of Defense for Command, Control, Communications and Intelligence (ASD(C3I)), and
NSTISSC had voting representatives from the:

    •   Secretaries of State, Treasury, Defense, Commerce, Transportation, and Energy,
    •   the Attorney General,
    •   the Director, Office of Management and Budget,
    •   the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs,
    •   Director of Central Intelligence,
    •   Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff,
    •   the FBI Director,
    •   the Director, Federal Emergency Management Agency,
    •   the Administrator, General Services Administration,
    •   US Army Chief of Staff, Chief of Naval Operations (CNO), US Air Force Chief of Staff, and the
        Commandant, US Marine Corps,
    •   The Director, National Security Agency,
    •   the Manager, National Communications System,
    •   and the Director, Defense Intelligence Agency.

While NSDD 145 had identified protecting national security information as a priority, it did not define it.
NSD-42 introduced the phrase “national security systems” as a way to help define and separate it from
other government information.
The SECDEF’s title expanded to the Executive Agent for National Security Telecommunications and
Information Systems Security. DIRNSA was named the “National Manager for National Security
Telecommunications and Information Systems Security”, responsible for carrying out SECDEF’s
responsibilities – just as it had been since 1953. In both cases, the name changes reflected the expansion
of technology; their roles remained the same. For example, DIRNSA still had authority to liaise with
foreign governments for computer security and COMSEC, including agreements with governments,
international and private organizations.

Over 4 decades, some things remained unchanged. Agency heads were still responsible for securing
national security systems under their purview. And, just as before, NSD-42 did not authorize NSTISSC,
SECDEF or DIRNSA to examine the facilities of other agencies without permission.

Cyber as infrastructure: 1990s to early 2000s
The next steps shaping US information security took place at the end of the 1990s, through the Critical
Infrastructure Protection Plan (CIPP). The CIPP was the US government’s recognition that vulnerabilities
to information and communications technology affected every element of national life.
“Communications security” – now information security – wasn’t limited to the communications industry.
Although not a policy in and of itself, the CIPP was a government-wide effort to coordinate a national
response to these threats. Planning was organized around the concept of critical infrastructure;
cyberspace and computer networks were identified as elements of those physical systems. Public-private
partnerships were the dominant feature of all planning, and remained that way well into the next
decade.

May 22, 1998: Presidential Decision Directive/NSC-63: Critical Infrastructure
Protection
PDD 63 put the work behind the CIPP into policy. The stated goals were to protect the nation’s critical
infrastructure at the national, state and local levels, and to limit the impact of attacks to “brief,
infrequent, manageable, geographically isolated and minimally detrimental to the welfare of the United
States.”

The directive called for public-private partnership, outlining the framework to a National Infrastructure
Assurance Plan, or NIAP, organized around infrastructure sectors (telecommunications, energy, banking
and finance, water systems, emergency services, etc).

The federal government was to set the example for “infrastructure assurance” and to encourage the
market to develop the means and methods for protecting critical infrastructure, with government
regulation as a last resort. So while the US government recognized the threat and continued to ramp up
engagement with the private sector, the policy was still one of persuasion over compulsion.

October 16, 2001: Executive Order 13231: Critical Infrastructure Protection in the
Digital Age
Issued shortly after September 11th, 2001, this Executive Order created a senior executive board to
coordinate Federal efforts: The President’s Critical Infrastructure Protection Board. The board members
were principals or designees from multiple departments, including State, Treasury, Defense, Justice,
Commerce, Energy and the DCI.
The NSTISSC was redesignated under the auspices of this board as the Committee on National Security
Systems, or CNSS.

This EO created the National Infrastructure Advisory Council (NIAC) as an advisory body to the President
on the security of information systems for critical infrastructure like banking and finance, transportation,
energy, manufacturing and emergency government services. NIAC members were chosen by the
President from the private sector, academia, state and local governments.

Cybersecurity: 2008 to today
Before leaving office, however, President Bush advanced the US government position by defining
cybersecurity in its own right as a distinct area of national interest, and began to prescribe specific
actions to defend it. This was the era in which the US government began moving from observing and
responding to cyberattacks toward actively defending against them. In time, the US began proactively
engaging with known threats to US systems and networks.

Comprehensive National Cybersecurity Initiative
The Comprehensive National Cybersecurity Initiative, or CNCI, created a 12-point framework through
which to organize cybersecurity efforts and coordination across the US government. CNCI initiatives were
a mixture of short-term activities (deploy intrusion detection systems across networks; connect existing
cyber operation centers), and strategic endeavors (supply chain risk management, cyber education,
developing advanced technology).

Notably, the CNCI stated that the federal government must consider and define its role in extending
cybersecurity into critical infrastructure. This was a significant change from previous policies that relied
on encouraging industry to secure their own networks. It acknowledged that US national security
depends heavily on American industry across many sectors of the economy.

January 8, 2008: National Security Presidential Directive 54: Cybersecurity Policy
NSPD-54 formalized the work of the CNCI, which went on well before this document was issued in
January, 2008. In stark terms, it stated that “The electronic information infrastructure of the United
States is subject to constant intrusion by adversaries [. . .] The United States must maintain unrestricted
access to and use of cyberspace for a broad range of national purposes.’ It defined cybersecurity by tying
in the five pillars of information assurance; availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality, and non-
repudiation.

NSPD-54 also created the National Cyber Investigative Joint Task Force (NCIJTF), which was operated by
the FBI with representatives from the CIA, NSA, US Secret Service and others. (The NCIJTF still exists
today, and has participants from more than 30 agencies.)

The Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs and Assistant to the President for Homeland
Security and Counterterrorism1 were placed in charge of cybersecurity policy coordination. Notably, the
directive called on the Attorney General, the Director of National Intelligence, and the departments of

Defense, State, and Homeland Security to create a joint plan “for the coordination and application of
offensive capabilities to defend US information systems.” This was a first.

May 12, 2021: Executive Order 14028:
This EO laid out several specific, time-bound activities to improve cybersecurity in the federal
government. It echoed previous calls for government partnerships with the private sector, to include
sharing threat information with government contractors – and prescribed revisions to federal acquisition
regulations (the FAR) and government contractors to ensure that it took place.

The order called for cyber incident reporting to be shared across affected agencies. It also directed the
adoption of multifactor authentication (within 6 months for Executive Branch agencies) and encryption
for data, both while at rest and in motion.

New standards, procedures and evaluations were called for to improve software supply chain security.
(For example, providing a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM), and separating build environments between
products, to avoid sharing code between them.)

Section 7 of EO 14028 was significant in that it called for early and active detection of vulnerabilities and
incidents, including an EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) initiative. For decades, presidential
orders expressly forbid COMSEC monitoring among other agencies without advanced notice to and
approval from the agency. That changed in 2021 with Section 1705 of the National Defense
Authorization Act.2 This enabled the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) to
proactively hunt for cyber intrusions throughout federal networks.

January 19, 2022: National Security Memorandum 8
NSM 8 made some substantial changes to the NSA Director’s responsibilities as the National Manager for
NSS. The memorandum directed DIRNSA to provide recommendations to the Committee on National
Security Systems (CNSS) on how to log events and secure relevant network data. The CNSS was then to
set out policy for agencies to develop requirements on how each would ensure that their individual
security operations centers would see this data.

NSM-8 directed the National Manager to facilitate – not make – designations of NSS across the Federal
Government. As before, each agency was still responsible for designating, accrediting and protecting an
NSS under their control. But now DIRNSA was to help agencies identify and inventory information
systems that constituted an NSS.

Compromises and/or unauthorized accesses were to be reported to the National Manager, who, working
together with the CIA director and DNI, would establish reporting procedures for known or suspected
NSS compromises.

Finally, and in a significant boost to the NSA Director’s authority, the National Manager was given the
power to issue directives – a “National Manager Emergency Directive” to protect NSS from known or
suspected threats and vulnerabilities. DIRNSA could also issue a Binding Operational Directive to heads
of agencies, coordinated with SECDEF and DNI for their respective systems.

2026: National Policy for the Cybersecurity of National Security Systems
A National Security Presidential Memorandum (NSPM) issued in the summer of 2026 created a new
governing policy specific to National Security Systems, or NSS. It contains a new charter for the
Committee for National Security Systems (CNSS), rescinds previous policy (both NSD-42 and NSM-8), and
reestablishes the National Manager for National Security Systems (the NSA Director) with expanded
responsibilities. By sharpening authorities and clarifying responsibilities, the new policy creates the
structures and relationships necessary to address both current and anticipated cybersecurity challenges.



Conclusion
Since the end of World War II, the United States has contended with the growing vulnerabilities and
capabilities of an increasingly interconnected world. Maintaining the security and confidentiality of
government communications has been a joint effort since 1945, and the fact that it is addressed through
the President indicates an understanding of how critical information security is. In another sense, it also
stands as an example of the interplay between communications intelligence and security. The US came
out of World War II with a deep and lasting appreciation for the value of signals intelligence, and a
subsequent understanding of the need to secure our own. More recently, the US government has had to
grapple with the reality that communications and information security – captured together under the
term cybersecurity – is a distinct national security issue.
