INTRODUCTION

In the modern theater of operations, the electromagnetic spectrum (EMS) is no longer just a
supporting element of the battlefield—it constitutes critical terrain. To maintain overmatch in
large-scale combat operations (LSCO), the U.S. Army must own this invisible terrain. However,
the Army cannot achieve EMS readiness solely in classrooms or simulators. True readiness
requires the unforgiving friction of the field environment. Crucially, this readiness has immediate
geopolitical consequences.

Soldiers assigned to various units in U.S. Army Europe and Africa (USAREUR-AF) participated
in Spectrum Blitz 26, an annual exercise designed to enhance proficiency across the EMS and
strengthen readiness for future operations at the Joint Multinational Readiness Center’s (JMRC)
Hohenfels Training Area in Hohenfels, Germany, 20 through 25 June 2026. JMRC’s best
electromagnetic warfare (EW) platoon competition did more than validate unit-level skills; it
directly operationalized USAREUR-AF and the North Atlantic Treaty Organization’s (NATO)
deterrence posture along the eastern flank. By forcing EW platoons to hunt, survive, and
synchronize in a highly contested environment, the U.S. Army is hardening its multi-domain
lethality and sending an unambiguous message to near-peer adversaries: U.S. forces are prepared
to own the spectrum. This article provides critical insights for U.S. Army and NATO leaders,
defense planners, and EW professionals responsible for developing combat-ready formations.

The JMRC answered this call by planning, developing, and facilitating exercise Spectrum Blitz
26. The event served as a grueling tactical stress test for frontline EW operators. More
importantly, it provided a clear, unvarnished look at where current Army capabilities excel and
where the force must urgently modernize to win the next fight. Through force-on-force
competition, exercise Spectrum Blitz 26 sharpens the Army’s EW readiness, highlighting
essential field tactics and systemic gaps that are crucial for securing battlefield superiority.

THE CRUCIBLE OF COMPETITION IN THE EUROPEAN AREA OF OPERATIONS

Competition drives excellence. JMRC leveraged this principle to its maximum potential. The
JMRC team designed exercise Spectrum Blitz as a rigorous, force-on-force EW platoon
competition between units operating in the European area of operations (AO). To ensure a high
baseline of expertise, all participating platoons were required to hold a Tier 4 qualification based
on established Army training standards. Refer to the U.S. Army EW training strategy released 4
February 2026 for tier qualifications. Four of the theater’s premier formations met this standard,
pitting their platoons against each other: the 1st Armored Brigade Combat Team (ABCT), 1st
Armored Division (AD); the 173rd Mobile Brigade Combat Team (MBCT [Airborne]); the 2nd
Cavalry Regiment (2CR); and the 1st Battalion, 4th Infantry Regiment. The objective was clear:
identify the most lethal, adaptable, and technically proficient EW platoon currently operating in
the theater. Figure 1 illustrates the competitors and their organizations that participated in the
best EW platoon competition.

By thrusting participating units into a contested, peer-level electromagnetic environment, JMRC
stripped away theoretical doctrine. Observers and operators confronted the absolute reality of the
modern dismounted fight. The competitive environment compelled units to innovate under
intense pressure, producing a wealth of actionable data that will benefit the broader force.

GOUND-LEVEL INGENUITY: OBSERVED TTPS AND BEST PRACTICES

Ground-level operations during the exercise highlighted several highly effective techniques that
drastically improved EW capabilities. Elevating Beast+ antennas up to 20 feet using terrain,
trees, or vehicles proved critical, drastically improving signal detection and direction finding
(DF) for weaker signals approaching the -80 dB threshold. Furthermore, successful units
employed EW systems as mobile collection platforms rather than static assets, maneuvering
dynamically to secure multiple lines of bearing (LOBs) from separate locations to generate rapid
fixes and target cuts. A cut uses two approximate LOBs to calculate the general location of a
transmitter by determining where two LOBs intersect. 3 Figure 2 illustrates an example of a cut.

them to transition from simple detection to generating actionable intelligence through “cuts and

combat multiplier. Furthermore, cohesive internal team dynamics, characterized by proactive
leadership and the ability to operate with minimal supervision, was a common factor in mission

BRIDGING THE GAPS: CRITICAL LESSONS LEARNED

To maximize readiness, the EW platoons must resolve key gaps in planning, equipment, and
training.

   1. Manual Data Aggregation:
        • Observation: EW teams manually aggregated direction-finding data using
           unsecure, free-text messaging applications.
        • Discussion: This reliance on manual reporting introduced human error, created
           significant delays in building a common operational picture, and increased the
           platoon’s electromagnetic signature. The process was slow, inaccurate, and
           vulnerable to interception.
        • Recommendation: Company and platoon-level leaders must enforce the use of
           the networked capabilities inherent in the terrestrial layered system-brigade
           combat team manpack (TLS-MP) radios to automate data aggregation, ensuring
           near real-time, error-free reporting.

   2. Inefficient Spectrum Planning:
         • Observation: Operators wasted valuable collection time targeting known
              friendly, coalition, and civilian emitters.
         • Discussion: The root cause was a failure to integrate comprehensive spectrum
              management products (e.g., restricted frequency lists [RFL]) into the mission
              planning process. This led to collection inefficiencies and a high rate of false
              positives, bogging down the platoon’s ability to find and fix legitimate targets.

         •   Recommendation: Brigade and battalion staff must integrate spectrum
             management overlays during the military decision-making process (MDMP).
             Companies and below must integrate during step 4 of the Troop Leading
             Procedures (TLPs) to filter non-threat signals and prioritize collection efforts for
             EW platoons.

  3. Deficient Rehearsals and Planning:
        • Observation: Units conducted superficial tabletop rehearsals where junior leaders
            did not fully understand their specific roles, and pre-combat inspections were
            often cursory.
        • Discussion: This lack of rigor in preparation led to confusion during execution
            and equipment shortfalls discovered too late. A plan that is not physically
            rehearsed and inspected is a plan that is likely to fail under pressure.
        • Recommendation: Company and platoon-level leaders must mandate full-force,
            physical rehearsals over simple tabletop exercises and enforce detailed, hands-on
            pre-combat inspections (PCIs) to ensure every Soldier and system is prepared.

  4. Equipment and Load Plan:
        • Observation: Teams were physically tethered to their sensor systems during
          antenna emplacement, and outdated software limited equipment effectiveness.
        • Discussion: Two distinct friction points were noted: 1. Poor cross-loading of
          antenna extension cables prevented operators from moving to concealed positions
          after elevating an antenna. 2. Outdated Android tactical assault kit (ATAK)
          software (version 5.4) limited the TLS-MP’s mapping and operational potential.
        • Recommendation: Command and control (C2) elements must enforce robust,
          redundant primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency (PACE) plans that do
          not rely solely on cellular or Wi-Fi infrastructure, which is highly vulnerable in
          austere, restricted-terrain environments.

  5. Isolated Reporting Versus Collaborative Targeting:
         • Observation: EW teams focused on generating individual signal reports for their
            chain of command rather than actively sharing information with adjacent
            collection teams.
         • Discussion: This “stove piped” reporting culture prevents the platoon from
            achieving its primary purpose: generating actionable intelligence. By operating as
            isolated collectors, teams miss opportunities to cross-cue each other, validate
            signals, and rapidly develop a targetable location from multiple LOBs. This limits
            their overall effect from enabling the brigade fight to simply administrative
            reporting.
         • Recommendation: Platoon leaders must shift their training focus from report
            generation to collaborative target development. They must enforce procedures for
            cross-team communication and the active sharing of frequencies to synchronize
            sensor-to-shooter operations across the platoon.

       6. Mounted Platform Integration Challenges:
            • Observation: The crew operating the Stryker-mounted tactical electronic warfare
               system (TEWS), as depicted in Figure 6, encountered significant challenges with
               vehicle sustainment and signature management in the exercise’s restrictive terrain.
            • Discussion: The exercise revealed a critical friction point between the agility of
               dismounted teams and the logistical and tactical burden of heavy platforms. The
               effectiveness of a system like the TEWS is not solely dependent on its sensor
               capabilities but equally on the crew’s mastery of vehicle maintenance, self-
               recovery drills, and the discipline to manage the platform’s large physical and
               electromagnetic signature. Without this, the system’s power is negated by its
               vulnerability.
            • Recommendation: Company commanders must ensure that vehicle-mounted EW
               crews receive specialized training that goes beyond the sensor system. This
               training must include rigorous standards for vehicle preventive maintenance, self-
               recovery, and signature management techniques specifically tailored for operating
               in restrictive or contested environments.

   7. Over-Reliance on Fragile Communications:
        • Observation: C2 elements became overly reliant on cellular or Wi-Fi-based
            infrastructure for communication.
        • Discussion: This dependence on non-military, commercial networks created a
            critical vulnerability. These networks are unreliable in austere environments and
            would be among the first targets in a real conflict. A communications plan that
            defaults to this infrastructure is not a viable plan for LSCO.
        • Recommendation: The S6 (Signal Staff Officer) at the battalion and brigade
            levels must ensure units are equipped and trained to execute a robust PACE plan.
            Company commanders must then enforce this plan during training, forcing
            platoons to operate under the assumption that commercial networks will not be
            available.

STRATEGIC IMPLICATIONS: DOTMLPF-P RECOMMENDATIONS

Although tactical leaders must address immediate operational friction, the broader Army
enterprise must implement systemic changes across the doctrine, organization, training, materiel,
leadership and education, personnel, facilities-policy (DOTMLPF-P) framework to ensure
electromagnetic warfare dominance in LSCO.

Doctrine: The Cyber Center of Excellence (CCoE) must update EW doctrine to establish core
collective standards for automated data aggregation and collaborative targeting. This doctrinal
baseline provides BCTs the framework needed to standardize synchronized, multi-node target
development across disparate unit SOPs.

Training: Force providing Army Service Component Commands (ASCC) and the Combat
Training Centers (CTCs) must mandate Tier 4 electromagnetic warfare validation as a
prerequisite for all BCT combat training center rotations. Furthermore, training scenarios must
routinely force command posts to operate degraded, relying exclusively on their PACE plans
without commercial network infrastructure.

Materiel: Program Executive Office Intelligence, Electronic Warfare and Sensors (PEO
IEW&S) must address hardware and software integration hurdles. Specifically, antenna
extension cables must be fielded as mandatory basic issue items (BII) for dismounted systems to
ensure operator survivability. Additionally, the enterprise must establish a rapid, over-the-air
(OTA) software update mechanism to prevent units from deploying with outdated software (e.g.,
legacy ATAK versions).

Leadership and Education: Transformation and Training Command (T2COM) must integrate
rigorous EW signature management and heavy-platform sustainment (like the TEWS) into the
Professional Military Education (PME) pipelines for maneuver commanders and senior non-
commissioned officers, ensuring they understand the logistical and tactical burdens of modern
EW systems.

THE VOICE OF THE OPERATOR: PARTICIPANT SURVEY RESULTS

Following Spectrum Blitz 26, JMRC collected 35 operator surveys capturing the warfighter’s
perspective from inside the fight. These insights do not restate the observed TTPs noted above;
instead, they reveal how Soldiers adapted, refined, and improvised those techniques under
competitive pressure, and how their equipment shaped battlefield decision-making.

Soldier-Driven TTP Refinement

Operators described the creative, field expedient methods they used to enhance observed TTPs,
including:

   •   Raising Beast+, Kraken, and versatile modular adaptive transmit (VMAX) antennas into
       trees or improvised mast extensions to gain critical elevation and improve LOB accuracy.
   •   Refining lazy W, concave, and distributed sensor placement to tighten DF geometry in
       restrictive terrain.
   •   Cross-team LOB baseline checks that improved accuracy and accelerated fix
       development.
   •   Using drones as cueing assets to align sensors and drive rapid offensive targeting.

These refinements demonstrate how Soldiers internalized the observed TTPs and pushed them
further under real world pressure.

Lessons Operators Learned in the Fight

Operators repeatedly emphasized several truths learned through friction, including:

   •   Terrain is decisive. Even small changes in elevation or OP location dramatically
       improved DF confidence.
   •   Micro adjustments matter. Moving a mast a few feet or shifting an OP tens of meters
       frequently resolved line of sight (LOS) issues or multipath interference.
   •   Learning from peers accelerates proficiency. Short periods of cross-unit observation
       drove immediate TTP improvements.
   •   Discipline in sensing workflows is essential. Soldiers stressed the importance of signal
       validation, signal of interest (SOI) confirmation, and cross-team baseline comparison.

These insights reinforce that EW effectiveness is as much a product of operator judgment as it is
of system capability.

Equipment Feedback from the Field

Soldiers provided candid assessments of how their systems performed under stress, including:

   •   Beast+: Highly durable and fast to employ; benefited from longer tether cables, stronger
       masts, and better cooling.
   •   Kraken: Powerful sensing and effective for on the move operations; however,
       overheating and fragile mounts limited sustained use.
   •   VMAX: Simple and maneuverable but hampered by aging software, mapping
       discrepancies, and reduced sensitivity at low signal strengths.
   •   Common Across All Systems: Operators highlighted the need for modernized cables and
       adapters, standardized software versions, and well-maintained components to ensure
       equitable performance across formations.

This technical feedback provides direct, actionable guidance for modernization efforts.

Competition-Driven Innovation

The competitive format fundamentally shaped operator behavior:

   •   Teams moved faster to high ground vantage points and repositioned sensors more
       aggressively.
   •   Soldiers experimented with novel rigging methods, concealment strategies, and mobility
       driven sensing techniques.
   •   The pressure to beat peer formations sharpened problem solving, workflow discipline,
       and speed of analysis.
   •   Operators consistently reported that competition pushed them to achieve higher levels of
       tactical creativity and system mastery.

Competition did not simply test EW Soldiers—it accelerated their development.

Key Takeaways from the Operator Perspective

Lessons learned from operators included:

   •   Elevation and concealment remain the decisive combination for spectral dominance.
   •   Cross-team TTP sharing immediately boosts effectiveness across formations.
   •   Hardware modernization—particularly cooling, cable length, mounts, and software—
       directly improves battlefield performance.
   •   A competitive environment produces the most adaptive, lethal, and technically proficient
       EW operators.

Collectively, the operator feedback paints a clear picture: Spectrum Blitz 26 did more than test
equipment or validate doctrine—it forced every platoon to innovate, adapt, and mature under
pressure. Their candid insights illuminate the human dimension behind the observed TTPs,
revealing how Soldiers transformed friction into proficiency and turned competition into a
catalyst for rapid learning. With these ground truth perspectives in hand, the transition from
technical performance to final outcomes becomes unmistakable: the stage was set for a
culminating contest of skill, discipline, and adaptability that would ultimately identify the Best
EW Platoon in the European theater.

FORGING THE FUTURE OF THE ELECTROMAGNETIC WARFARE FORCE

Spectrum Blitz 26 proved that the Army’s EW formations are not only capable—they are
evolving. Across demanding terrain, complex missions, and a competitive environment designed
to expose weaknesses and reward excellence, EW Soldiers demonstrated adaptability, ingenuity,
and tactical discipline. Observed TTPs captured how units executed with precision in the field.
Operator feedback revealed how Soldiers refined those techniques through innovation,
improvisation, and sheer determination. Together, these insights offer a comprehensive
understanding of where the force excels and where modernization is essential—from equipment
improvements to more consistent training opportunities and increased cross-unit collaboration.

Most importantly, Spectrum Blitz reaffirmed that the Army’s competitive training model works.
By forcing platoons to sense, hunt, and survive against peer formations, JMRC accelerated
learning across every unit and elevated the performance of the entire cohort. Although one
platoon ultimately earned the title of Best EW Platoon, every competitor emerged stronger, more
lethal, and better prepared for LSCO in the EMS. The lessons captured here will inform training,
shape modernization priorities, and help forge an EW force capable of delivering decisive
advantage on tomorrow’s battlefield.
