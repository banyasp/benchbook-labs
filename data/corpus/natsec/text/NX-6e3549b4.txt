                                                 Introduction
Current U.S. Army recovery doctrine and standard recovery vehicles (such as the M88A2 and
family of medium tactical vehicles [FMTV] wrecker) are incapable of retrieving personnel or
assets from high-angle mountainous or severely restricted terrain. High-angle is the application
of technical rope rescue principles and recovery operations to retrieve personnel, vehicles, and
equipment from steep, vertical, or inaccessible terrain where conventional recovery assets cannot
safely or effectively accomplish the mission. To bridge this critical tactical gap, brigade combat
teams (BCTs) should establish an organic high-angle recovery team (HART). This publication
equips brigade-level commanders and sustainment leaders with a tested, validated, and
repeatable framework to organize, train, and equip a HART using existing unit personnel,
commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) rigging, and a disciplined "crawl-walk-run" training
methodology.
The Operational Gap
Modern combat operations increasingly take place in austere, mountainous, and urban
environments where vehicles and personnel routinely operate near steep dropoffs, cliffs, and
structural ruins.1 When an asset or soldier falls into restricted terrain, conventional recovery
assets cannot safely access or extract them. This capability gap forces commanders to choose
between high-risk ad hoc recovery attempts or abandoning vital equipment.
The Solution
The HART Framework
To solve this shortfall, a brigade maintenance company established a HART (a small, scalable,
and cross-trained team of recovery, maintenance, and medical personnel). Operating as an
extension of standard recovery doctrine, the HART relies on a three-phased implementation
model:
•     Equip: Procuring lightweight, standardized COTS high-angle rescue gear and leveraging
      tactical vehicles as anchoring systems
•     Train: Executing a progressive "crawl-walk-run" training pipeline that combines formal
      military mountaineering schooling with unit-led hands-on certifications
•     Validate: Verifying operational capability during a collective field training exercise (FTX),
      simulating realistic multi-domain recovery scenarios.
Commanders' Value and Lessons Learned
Establishing an organic HART demonstrates that units can build highly specialized life-saving
capabilities without modifying existing modified tables of organization and equipment (MTOE)
structures or requiring external civilian rescue assets. By implementing these tactics, techniques,
and procedures (TTPs), sustainment commanders immediately enhance unit survivability,
preserve combat power, and extend operational reach in the world's most challenging terrains.2

capability to recover casualties, disabled vehicles, and equipment in high-angle or restricted and

Current Recovery Capability and Operational Shortfalls
Brigade maintenance companies possess a robust capability to recover disabled vehicles and
equipment during conventional operations. Organic recovery assets (specifically the M984 heavy
expanded mobility tactical truck (HEMTT) and M1089 medium tactical vehicle wreckers)
recover disabled systems only when they can establish direct, vehicular access to the asset. These
systems provide the brigade with the ability to tow, winch, lift, and extract equipment under a
wide range of operational conditions. Severe terrain; however, inherently limits these
conventional recovery platforms. In Arctic, densely vegetated, and other restrictive
environments, recovery vehicles may be unable to safely maneuver to the disabled vehicle or
casualty. When terrain denies direct vehicle access, the brigade loses the capability to access,
assess, rig, prepare, and recover the disabled asset. Existing recovery doctrine provides limited
guidance for these scenarios, resulting in increased reliance on improvised techniques and unit-
developed procedures.4
This operational gap extends beyond vehicle recovery alone. Before conventional assets can
assist, recovery crews must descend steep slopes, traverse unstable terrain, build load-bearing
anchors, and control the movement of casualties or equipment. Lacking this organic capability
delays recovery operations, increases risk to Soldiers, and stalls the restoration of combat power.
Current brigade recovery capabilities do not provide an organic means to:
•     Safely access personnel or equipment located in steep or vertical terrain.
•     Establish standardized rope-based anchor systems for recovery operations.
•     Conduct controlled raising or lowering of personnel, casualties, or equipment.
•     Rig disabled equipment for recovery when recovery vehicles cannot establish direct access.
•     Integrate rope rescue techniques into sustainment recovery operations.
As a result, the primary operational limitation is not the recovery equipment itself, but the
brigades’ inability to safely reach and prepare the recovery site in restrictive terrain. This
capability gap directly impacts operational tempo, increases risk to personnel and delays the
restoration of combat power during expeditionary operations.
While standard recovery platforms like the M88A2 and M1089 are highly effective on trafficable
terrain, their severe limitations in mountainous, urban, or restricted environments create a critical
capability gap. When recovery crews cannot safely maneuver their vehicles to establish direct
access to a disabled asset, the operation fundamentally shifts from a standard vehicle recovery to
a complex access problem. Attempting to bridge this gap using standard-issue rigging or
improvised, ad hoc techniques rapidly introduce cascading risks across the formation,
threatening both personnel safety and mission success.

High angle recovery team (HART). The HART is a specialized highly scalable element
embedded directly within existing brigade maintenance companies. It requires no modifications
to the unit's MTOE and utilizes a lightweight, expeditionary equipment set.
1. Organization and Employment Concept
The HART leverages existing personnel to minimize organizational footprint while maximizing
operational reach. The team deploys specifically when terrain prevents conventional recovery
vehicle access or when standard recovery methods pose unacceptable safety risks.
    •   Convoy integration. Each convoy integrates a dedicated, six-person HART element
        consisting of:
           o 2x recovery personnel (from the wrecker team)
           o 2x maintenance personnel (from the contact truck team)
           o 2x medical personnel (for casualty coverage)
    •   Leadership. The existing recovery section non-commissioned officer in charge (NCOIC)
        leads the element, ensuring seamless integration with standard recovery operations.

    Vehicle density. The team must use whichever recovery asset the unit can support at the time
    of mission. Wrecker, field litter ambulance (FLA), contact truck or 4-door high mobility
    multipurpose wheeled vehicle (HMMWV). Typical convoys use a wrecker, a contact truck,
    an FLA, and all personnel associated with those vehicles augment the HART.

    •   Minimum outload of equipment:
               o 1x rescue tripod
               o 5x mountaineering harness
               o 1x static rope
               o 1x dynamic rope
               o 2x rock exotica omni-block 1.5 swivel pulley
               o 2x camp sphinx pulley
               o 6x non-lockers
               o 4x lockers
               o Multiple various lengths of tubular nylon and cordelette (cord tied in a loop)
From a tactical standpoint, the HART TTP should be executed after the parent unit has consolidated
its position and initiated reorganization and recovery efforts. This ensures the broader unit is
postured to provide a secure environment, allowing the specialized HART element to focus on the
technical aspects of the recovery.

2. Core Tactical Capabilities
Once deployed to a restricted recovery site, the HART provides commanders with the organic
capability to:
      •    Access. Safely navigate steep terrain to reach stranded personnel and disabled equipment.
      •    Anchor. Construct highly secure anchor systems utilizing tactical vehicles and natural
           terrain features.
      •    Rig. Prepare complex load-bearing rigging for heavy equipment recovery.
      •    Execute. Conduct the controlled raising, lowering, and extraction of heavy equipment.
      •    Evacuate. Conduct litter-based casualty recovery operations in high-angle environments.
3. The "Crawl-Walk-Run" Training Pipeline
To establish a HART, units execute a progressive training model that combines formal
institutional schooling with internal unit-led certifications. This model culminates in a scenario-
based FTX to validate the capability.
           •   Institutional schooling (the foundation). Select personnel attend formal military
               courses:
                        o   Basic/Advanced Military Mountaineering Courses; Fort Wainwright, Fort
                            Benning, National Guard-Vermont (2 weeks).
                        o    Cold Weather Medicine; Vermont (10 days).
                        o   Rappel Master; Lighting Academy Hawaii, Fort Drum, Fort Campbell,
                            Army National Guard, Fort Benning (1 week).
                        o Wheeled Recovery Specialist; Fort Lee, Pearl City Hawaii, Fort Stewart,
                          Fort Leonard Wood, Fort Hood (3 weeks).6
           •   Unit-Led certification (the application). School-trained personnel return to the unit
               to internally train and certify additional teams on:
               o Rope systems, load management, and mechanical advantage calculation
               o Anchor construction (vehicle-based and natural)
               o Personnel movement (rappelling and controlled descent)
               o Steep-terrain casualty handling and litter operations

This framework provides a repeatable blueprint for organizing, training, and equipping a HART.
However, to adopt these components as a credible TTP, the unit must rigorously test them under
realistic conditions. The following section details the results of an FTX designed specifically to
validate the HART's core capabilities and confirm its operational value.
                                    Capability Validation
                              Field Training Exercise Results
On 28 March 2026, a brigade maintenance company conducted a comprehensive high-angle
recovery FTX in Anchorage, AK to validate the HART concept under realistic field conditions.
The exercise confirmed that a small, organically trained team can safely and effectively execute
complex recovery tasks that are nearly impossible with conventional assets. The FTX
demonstrated that the HART capability is not theoretical but is a practical, repeatable, and
immediately applicable solution to a known operational shortfall. The following observations
detail the core tactical tasks validated during the exercise.
Validated Task 1: Establish Vehicle-based Anchor Systems
The HART successfully demonstrated the ability to use standard tactical vehicles as rock-solid
anchor points for complex, load-bearing rope systems. By integrating with the M1089 wrecker,
the team confirmed that units do not need specialized anchoring vehicles, proving the system's
adaptability and low logistical footprint. Figure 1 illustrates an M1089 Wrecker serving as the
primary anchor point for multiple high-angle rope systems during the FTX, demonstrating the
integration of existing unit equipment.

Validated Task 2: Execute Controlled Hoisting with Mechanical Advantage
Soldiers proved their proficiency in building and operating rope-based mechanical advantage
systems. Mechanical advantage is the capability of a rope and pulley system to multiply applied
force, enabling personnel to recover loads that exceed the force they could generate through
direct pulling alone. The lift limitations are 1,500 pounds, the recommended working load for
Army issued static rope. It has a breaking strength of 30 kN (kilonewton is a unit of force).
Issued locking-D carabiners have an approximate tensile strength of 29kN (6500 pounds-gate
closed). The Rock Exotica brand pulleys utilized have a rating of 36 kN. The team effectively
managed and moved heavy loads, successfully hoisting a simulated casualty up a steep, snow-
covered slope. This validated the core principle that a small team can generate significant force
to move heavy objects safely and with complete control. Figure 2 illustrates HART members use
a rope-based mechanical advantage system to hoist a simulated casualty, demonstrating precise
load control during the recovery.

Validated Task 3: Conduct High-angle Casualty Extraction
Using a unit-fabricated rescue tripod anchored to the M1089, the HART successfully executed a
high-angle casualty extraction. The rescue tripod that was fabricated in the brigade maintenance
company weld shop successfully passed a 6,000-pound proof load test, verifying the structural
integrity of the system under controlled conditions. The proof load should not be interpreted as
the operational working load limit. The limitations on what can be hauled with the tripod is with
the rope working load as opposed to what the tripod can handle. This critical task validated the
team's ability to perform vertical rescues in otherwise inaccessible locations, confirming that the
HART provides a life-saving capability for personnel who have fallen into ravines, crevasses, or
down steep embankments. Figure 3 illustrates a Soldier operating a high-angle rope system

equipment needed for high-angle movement, rigging, and rescue operations.
To further increase mechanical-advantage efficiency during hauling, lowering, and complex
rope-system work, the inclusion of two additional pulleys is recommended. The Rock Exotica
Omni-Block 1.5 Swivel Pulley provides high-efficiency operation with an integrated swivel and
a side plate design that opens without removing the carabiner, preventing rope twist during use.
It features a minimum breaking strength of 36 kN, with side plates rated at 18 kN, and is
compatible with rope diameters up to 13 mm. Complementing this is the Camp Sphinx Pulley
(Ref. 2152), a compact, lightweight pulley equipped with sealed ball bearings for smooth and
efficient operation. Its size and efficiency make it particularly well-suited for redirects,
mechanical-advantage systems, lightweight haul kits, and personal rescue applications.
Both pulleys were procured through Recreational Equipment Incorporated (REI) and serve as
critical enhancements to improve system efficiency and overall team capability during
high-angle rescue operations.
                                          Conclusion
BCTs operating in austere, mountainous, and Arctic environments can no longer rely solely on
conventional ground-based recovery assets. As adversaries push U.S. forces into increasingly
restrictive and complex terrain, the limitations of standard heavy wreckers create a critical
capability gap. Lacking a reliable, repeatable high-angle recovery method, commanders accept
unnecessary tactical risks and forfeit vital combat power.
The organic HART capability (developed and validated by a brigade maintenance company)
directly and effectively closes this gap. By integrating COTS rope systems, mechanical
advantage techniques, and vehicle-based anchor solutions, the HART extends recovery
operations into environments where traditional methods fail.
The successful validation of this concept during a high-angle recovery FTX proves that units do
not need to wait for multi-million-dollar MTOE changes or external civilian rescue assets. The
HART extends recovery operations into environments where traditional methods fail. While the
execution of these TTPs involves temporary exposure for the recovery team, the disciplined and
efficient nature of the procedure minimizes the overall time a unit remains static and vulnerable
in a combat zone, thus preserving momentum.

                                      Appendix A
                  Tactical Execution Procedures and Safety Standards
Prior to any tactical execution, the recovery site must be secured. The HART TTP must be
executed under the protection of a dedicated security element responsible for establishing local
area defense, including counter-unmanned aircraft system (C-UAS) measures. This security
element is essential to allow the recovery team to work without distraction in a potentially hostile
area. Executing high-angle recovery operations requires deliberate planning, disciplined
execution, and uncompromising safety standards. During deployment to a restricted recovery
site, the HART executes a precise, phased sequence to establish a safe and effective recovery
system.
Phase 1: Tripod Emplacement and Anchor System
Purpose. To establish a stable, load-bearing anchor system that serves as the foundation for both
personnel access and the casualty/equipment haul system.
   1. Emplace tripod:
       •     Position the tripod on stable, solid ground with an adequate load-bearing surface.
       •     Ensure each tripod foot is secure using the appropriate footwear for the terrain (e.g.,
             crampon-style points for ice, staked feet for soft ground).
         • Secure the base chains to prevent the legs from spreading under load.
         • Adjust the legs to meet the required height for the operation.
Figure 4 illustrates a fully emplaced tripod with hobbling chains secured. Figures 5 and 6
illustrate detailed views of the specialized tripod feet (crampon points for ice and staked plates
for soil) highlighting the system’s adaptability to different environmental conditions.

Phase 3: Rappeler Access System
Purpose: is to provide safe and controlled access for a recovery team member to reach the
casualty or equipment in the restricted area.
   1. Establish anchor and route rope:
        •   Select a primary anchor point that is strong, redundant, and properly aligned with the
            direction of pull.
        •   Use a dynamic rope for the rappeler’s safety line to absorb shock in the event of a
            fall.
        •   Protect the rope path from abrasion with edge protection before feeding the rappel
            rope through the designated pulley or anchor point.
   2. Rappeler setup and pre-combat checks (PCCs):
        •   The rappeler must utilize a rated rappel harness (a waist harness is the minimum).
        •   Critical: Prior to execution, buddies must thoroughly inspect each other’s harness
            integration, locked carabiners, rope routing, and rappel device setup.
   3. Execution:
        •   The rappeler conducts a controlled descent to access the casualty or equipment.
        •   The team must maintain constant verbal or visual communication throughout the
            entire movement.
Phase 4: Critical Safety Considerations
Failure to adhere to these safety standards will result in catastrophic failure, serious injury, or
death.
   1.   Vehicle Anchors:
        •   Do not use vehicles as anchors without the parking brake set, wheels chocked, and
            stabilization measures in place.
        •   Do not anchor to vehicles positioned on unstable or sloped ground without significant
            mitigation and stabilization.
        •   Do not anchor to cosmetic or standard bumpers; these unrated components will fail
            under load.
   2.   System Limits and Rigging:
        •   Do not exceed the rated capacity of any winch, rope, or recovery system component.
        •   Do not stand in the line of tension (snapback hazard zone) of any rope under load.
        •   Do not allow ropes to rub on unprotected sharp or abrasive edges.
