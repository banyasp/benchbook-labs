                                    FOREWORD
Intelligence is a joint function that serves to provide information, assessments, and
estimates to the commanders and their staff to support situational understanding and
enable decision-making.
Global Integrated Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (GIISR) is one of the
Air Force’s Core Functions. ISR is an activity that synchronizes and integrates the
planning and operation of sensors, assets, processing, exploitation, and dissemination
(PED) systems, and analytic systems or capabilities directly support current and future
intelligence operations.
The Air Force Deputy Chief of Staff for Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance
(AF/A2) is responsible for policy formulation, planning, evaluation, oversight, and
leadership of United States Air Force (USAF) global integrated ISR. As the USAF’s Senior
Intelligence Officer (SIO), the AF/A2 has the authority to collaborate and coordinate with
the Under Secretary of War for Intelligence and Security [USW(I&S)] and execute, as
delegated, the Secretary of the Air Force’s Secretary of the Military Department and
Department of War Component Head intelligence responsibilities. As head of the USAF
Intelligence Community (IC) element, the AF/A2 coordinates and advocates for USAF
ISR capabilities within the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI).

       CHAPTER 1: AIR FORCE INTELLIGENCE FUNDAMENTALS
United States Air Force (USAF) intelligence efforts are guided by a clear hierarchy,
flowing from national strategic policy and guidance down to daily operations. The
foundation of top-level direction from national security, defense, and intelligence
community (IC) standards shapes the organization and prioritization of intelligence forces.
From there, this strategic intent is translated into operational practice through specific
USAF core functions, such as Global Integrated Intelligence, Surveillance, and
Reconnaissance (GIISR).
Intelligence production follows a structured progression, moving from raw data and basic
information toward refined assessments and enduring knowledge frameworks.
Understanding this progression highlights the essential difference between intelligence
as a capability (the processes, people, and systems) and intelligence as a product (the
outputs that inform commanders’ decisions). Together, these elements form an integrated
architecture that aligns national priorities with the execution of the joint intelligence
process, ensuring coherence from strategic guidance to tactical application.

NATIONAL POLICY AND GUIDANCE
The President of the United States (US) and the Secretary of War (SecWar) provide
strategic guidance for the Department of War (DoW), which directs the prioritization of
homeland defense and deterring threats in key regions such as the Indo-Pacific. These
strategies create a cascading framework of objectives, priorities, and requirements that
flow from the highest levels of national leadership down to the individual analyst and
intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) platform. This alignment ensures a
unified effort of national and military intelligence for military operations, linking operational
intelligence collection and analysis directly to the nation's strategic objectives.
National Intelligence Strategy (NIS). The Director of National Intelligence’s (DNI)
strategy calls for a more agile, integrated IC that collaborates across the 18 member
agencies and with partners, reinforcing the need for USAF intelligence activities to share
data with other services, combatant commands (CCMD), and multinational intelligence
partners to inform national decision-making. This emphasizes common standards, data
interoperability, and fusion intelligence efforts. The NIS provides the broad IC-wide
analytic and workforce priorities and shapes the National Intelligence Priorities
Framework (NIPF), framing what capabilities and adversaries matter most, while the
defense intelligence guidance embedded in national defense planning translates those
into mission-specific, DoW, and USAF intelligence requirements that shape how
intelligence collection and analysis are conducted daily. This ensures that the USAF’s
analytic outputs are relevant, timely, and aligned with strategic goals from the President
and DNI down to the individual analyst.

                          Intelligence Community Directives

  policy and provide definitive direction to the IC (see dni.gov). While numerous

Joint Functions. Joint functions are a grouping of capabilities and activities that enable
joint force commanders (JFCs) to synchronize, integrate, and direct joint operations.
Intelligence is one of seven joint functions along with command and control (C2),
information, fires, movement and maneuver, protection, and sustainment. Intelligence
provides information, assessments, and estimates to enable decision-making.
Intelligence also enables the other joint functions by continuously providing commanders
and their staff with a holistic understanding of the operational environment (OE) to inform
operational design and planning, intelligence estimates to facilitate plans for future joint
military operations, and current intelligence to assess operations in relation to desired
effects and objectives.

FOUNDATIONS OF INTELLIGENCE PRODUCTION
The production of intelligence at the speed of modern conflict is no longer a matter of
simply collecting more data, but of mastering the data collected. A deliberate data
governance strategy is therefore a prerequisite for any effective intelligence operation.
Without it, the immense volume of information from the operating environment remains a
chaotic liability rather than a strategic asset. The ultimate goal is to achieve decision
advantage, which is the ability to make better, faster, and more effective decisions than
an adversary. It is gained by accessing superior information, analyzing it more effectively,
and acting upon it more decisively.

MASTERING THE DATA-RICH BATTLESPACE FOR DECISION ADVANTAGE
To achieve a decisive edge, military
operations aim for decision advantage.
This advantage is built on a foundation of
information advantage, the culmination of
a critical process that transforms raw data
into actionable intelligence. Initially, data
exists as discrete, unorganized facts such
as raw signal intercepts or satellite
images. When this data is processed,
organized, and given context, it becomes
information. Intelligence represents the
final, most valuable stage of this progression. It is the product of analyzing information to
understand its significance, predict future actions, and identify vulnerabilities. By
effectively converting data into information and information into intelligence, a force gains
a superior understanding of the OE, enabling the rapid and informed decision-making that
is the hallmark of decision advantage.
TAXONOMIES, ONTOLOGIES, KNOWLEDGE MODELS, OBJECTS
Mastering the data-rich environment begins with disciplined organization and
standardization at scale. The USAF relies on a structured framework built on taxonomies,
ontologies, and knowledge models, which refine raw data into usable intelligence. These
components create a common language that enables humans and machines to rapidly
interpret, relate, and analyze complex information, accelerating the transformation of data
into decision-quality insight.
Proper governance ensures data is discoverable, accessible, and trustworthy, enabling
rapid synthesis of intelligence and providing commanders with the sustained decision
advantage required to fight and win inside information-dense environments. These
distinctions highlight the importance of structured data governance and analytic
frameworks.
The USAF intelligence enterprise contributes to the IC by aligning with the Defense All
Source Analytic Enterprise (DIAAE) knowledge model for object-based intelligence (OBI)
and production (OBP). Successful implementation requires supporting data models and
standards, and the Defense Intelligence Core Ontology (DICO) provides a
mission-focused framework for organizing defense intelligence data, while the Defense
Intelligence Enterprise Knowledge Model (DIEKM), managed by the Defense Intelligence
Agency (DIA), serves as the IC model. The DIEKM establishes a uniform structure for
organizing information from different sources and domains. It serves as the master
blueprint that ensures all intelligence data, regardless of origin, can integrate into a
cohesive whole. By standardizing how information is structured, the DIEKM enhances
data interoperability, powers machine learning and AI, and enables analysts to access
and understand information more efficiently, leading to faster and more accurate
intelligence production.

Object-Based Intelligence. OBI is an approach to intelligence analysis that focuses on
defining and analyzing specific objects of interest to understand their relationships,
vulnerabilities, and potential impact. It is a shift away from traditional methods that often
prioritize geographic regions or specific threats. This approach relies on integrating data
from diverse sources to build a comprehensive understanding of each object. By
understanding object profiles and relationships, analysts can use this form of intelligence
to predict future events and potential threats. Changes in object attributes, relationships,
or activities can serve as early warnings. 1
Object-Based Production. OBP is the standardized IC process that organizes data around
objects of interest, enabling users to aggregate information across domains and tie it to
a specific object. The USAF intelligence enterprise translates this OBP methodology into
a mission line known as the disposition of forces (DOF) production process, which
supplements theater order of battle and is central to enabling the execution of theater
dynamic targets at speed and scale. While both OBI and OBP leverage the concept of
"objects," they operate differently and serve distinct purposes. The connection is that they
both use a consistent object-based data model. 2 Terms associated with OBP include:
Object. An object is a digital representation of a real-world thing, action, or abstraction.
An object is not always a target. Once an object is vetted and validated through the target
development process, it may then be designated as a target. In the dynamic targeting
process, objects become tracks, and tracks become targets.
Object Management System (OMS). OMS is the application, service, or
system-of-systems that enables the production and consumption of information and
intelligence within an object-based data construct, aligning all reporting to objects
agnostic to the producer or consumer.
Structured Observation Management (SOM). SOM is the method by which geospatial
intelligence (GEOINT) observations from satellites, sensors, and other sources are
captured, structured, stored, and shared in a standardized format compatible with the
OBP. This ensures that GEOINT information can be integrated into the IC’s OBP process.
Disposition of Forces (DOF). DOF is the identity, location, activity, and operational status
of an object or forces. DOF is informed by the relationships between objects, their
assigned garrison locations, and associations to other units, however, the execution of
DOF does not cease when objects enter or depart from garrison. DOF provides planners
the ability to perform quick queries to provide a dynamic picture of where the adversaries
were in the past, where they are going, and where they will be soon.

Intelligence is defined as a product resulting from the collection, processing, integration,
evaluation, analysis, and interpretation of available information concerning foreign
nations, hostile or potentially hostile forces or elements, or areas of actual or potential
operations. It is also the activities that result in the product as well as the organizations
that conduct them.
Intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) are integrated capabilities that
collect, process, exploit, and disseminate accurate, timely information to provide the
situational awareness necessary to plan and conduct operations. The goal of ISR is to
provide precise, timely intelligence to the warfighters and to help the USAF achieve
information superiority. ISR activities are how raw data and information are gathered from
the OE to answer a specific intelligence requirement.
Intelligence and ISR form a connected but distinct value chain. ISR provides the
foundational input: platforms, sensors, and personnel dedicated to collecting raw data
from the OE. In its unprocessed state, this data has limited utility for commanders. The
intelligence enterprise performs the essential value-added function, transforming that
data through exploitation, analysis, and contextualization. The result is intelligence, a
refined product that fosters understanding, reduces uncertainty, and enables effective
decision-making. This distinction is operationally significant. Resourcing collection
without a proportional investment in analytic capacity produces diminishing
returns and fails to generate the decision superiority required to compete and win.

AIR COMPONENT INTELLIGENCE SUPPORT
Air component intelligence support enables decision advantage for joint and multinational
forces through five integrated capabilities: battlespace characterization, collection
operations, targeting, production of intel mission data, and intel support to weapon system
design and acquisition. These capabilities are delivered to the CCMD or joint task force
(JTF) through divisions of an air operations center (AOC).
BATTLESPACE CHARACTERIZATION
The purpose of battlespace characterization is to understand and predict an adversary by
examining their capabilities, tactics, dispositions, centers of gravity, and courses of action
(COA) within the context of the OE. It is critical for providing indications and warnings,
identifying potential vulnerabilities to forces, and identifying opportunities to achieve
objectives. The aim of battlespace characterization is to identify what is and is not known
by continuously updating and validating assessments. Information gained through

COLLECTION OPERATIONS
Collection operations acquire raw data and information about relevant aspects of the OE
and provide that information to intelligence processing and exploitation elements.
Collection involves tasking and synchronizing ISR sensors, platforms, and exploitation
resources to characterize the OE, adversary activities, and infrastructure, and to find, fix,
track entities of interest. The aim is to test beliefs, confirm knowledge, and discover
intelligence gaps to enhance decision advantage. Collection operations are typically
driven by battlespace characterization or targeting requirements.

INTELLIGENCE MISSION DATA PRODUCTION
Intelligence mission data (IMD) production is the ability to derive, produce, and rapidly
update the intelligence used for programming platform mission systems in development,
testing, operations and sustainment to include the following functional areas: signatures,
electromagnetic warfare integrated reprogramming, order of battle, characteristics and
performance, and GEOINT. The proliferation of information-based weapon systems that
require detailed intelligence to operate as designed has significantly increased the need
for production and rapid integration of IMD into USAF operations.
INTELLIGENCE SUPPORT TO ACQUISITION
Intelligence support to acquisition and its associated analytical processes enable the
acquisition community to impact weapon system design through future threat projections
and to capture the intelligence sensitivity of a particular development program. Threat
assessments, extending from the current risk out beyond 20 years, include production of
System Threat Assessments and System Threat Assessment Reports. Programs also

INTELLIGENCE ROLES IN OPERATIONS
Intelligence is integral to every military operation, but its application is never
one-size-fits-all. The character and objectives of a specific mission dictate the focus,
tempo, and type of intelligence support required. From the foundational analysis needed
for nuclear deterrence to the real-time, tactical insights essential for special operations
forces, intelligence personnel and capabilities are expertly tailored and integrated to
provide commanders with the specific decision-advantage needed to succeed.
OPERATIONAL UNIT-LEVEL INTELLIGENCE
In support of combat operations, individual unit-level intelligence teams are collectively
organized to form the contingency intelligence network (CIN). 4 Wing intelligence
personnel form the main teams of the CIN; the combat intelligence cell (CIC), mission
planning cell (MPC), and squadron intelligence. The CIN funnels intelligence into and out
of a particular wing or organization to support three main functions: mission planning,
mission execution, and mission debriefing and reporting.
At the operational level, the CIC interacts with the unit support function inside the AOC’s
ISR division (ISRD) to gather current intelligence, threat data, and request for information
(RFI) responses. They disseminate this information throughout the wing and down to
operational squadrons. The MPC provides analysis of possible enemy COA, support to
targeting, and timely updates to operational mission planners. Squadron intelligence
personnel support specific airframes and communicate relevant intelligence directly to
aircrew. The CIN, through the CIC, MPC, and squadron intelligence, keeps the
commander and operations crews informed of intelligence that can affect mission
execution. Support may include intelligence databases, current threat briefings and
training, and assistance with mission planning.
USAF SPECIAL OPERATIONS
USAF special operations are intelligence-intensive and, in some cases, require detailed
and tailored intelligence support. Special operations forces (SOF) support tends to rely
on organic ISR assets, be less centralized, and focus on tactical intelligence. Intelligence
personnel produce detailed, specialized products tailored to the mission, customer, and
pace of operation. The sensitivity of SOF missions may constrain the release of
post-mission reports. Commanders should report sensitive mission data through special
access or routine intelligence channels accordingly.

HOMELAND OPERATIONS
When lawful and authorized to do so, USAF intelligence forces may be tasked to conduct
ISR or incident awareness and assessment (IAA) within the homeland. Though similar to
ISR, IAA operations are conducted during defense support of civil authorities (DSCA)
activities, providing timely and usable information to commanders and local, tribal, state,
and federal leaders to save lives, reduce human suffering, and protect property. Though
ISR activities may be conducted within the US for homeland defense, IAA is only
conducted within the US for DSCA.
Importantly, the laws governing DoW operations in the homeland are different than those
that govern overseas operations. These laws place specific restrictions and limitations on
the employment or use of military intelligence in the homeland. 5
NUCLEAR OPERATIONS
Nuclear operations require focused and detailed intelligence that can survive in pre- and
post-strike environments. Intelligence provides commanders with information needed to
make timely decisions and enables civilian leaders to send clear messages and signals
to deter adversaries and assure allies. 6
CYBERSPACE OPERATIONS
Cyberspace operations that support air operations are usually provided to the air
component through reachback and are broken down into three subsets: offensive (OCO),
defensive (DCO), and Department of Defense Information Network (DoDIN) operations.
Intelligence support to each of these subsets is technologically focused and requires
significant technical understanding of the domain. Cyberspace intelligence analysts
integrate as closely as possible with cyberspace personnel.
Intelligence for OCO identifies access vectors to gain entry into adversarial target
networks, detail target network characteristics, and identify network vulnerabilities for
further attack vectors. Intelligence analysts supporting DCO and DoDIN operations
consider the perspective of potential adversaries that may attack air component networks
to understand threats to those systems, whether from state or non-state actors. 7
FORCE PROTECTION
Intelligence provides vital support and capabilities to force protection and point defense
functions and activities to defeat threats to airfields and air bases and prevent mission
degradation. If tasked to defend assets and personnel at air component installations, the
air component commander should consider designating organic ISR assets to support
the Air Force Office of Special Investigations (AFOSI) counterintelligence (CI) activities.
Force protection forces rely on the unique capabilities of various sensors to develop a
comprehensive understanding of the OE and enhance their ability to identify and track

                    CONTRIBUTIONS TO LONG-RANGE KILL CHAINS
       Long-range kill chains are becoming essential for future conflicts because
       adversaries are investing in anti-access and area-denial systems, long-
       range fires, and distributed operations that demand we sense, decide, and
       act from far beyond traditional engagement distances. As threats grow more
       mobile, more concealed, and more geographically dispersed, the ability to
       execute a seamless chain of sensing, sense-making, and engagement
       across domains becomes the only way to maintain decision advantage.
       Future fights will unfold at machine speed, over vast distances, and under
       constant threat of disruption, which means forces should be able to detect
       targets early, validate them with confidence, and pair them to the right
       shooter without delay. As the force moves toward more contested, multi
       domain environments, the integration of advanced ISR, resilient C2, and
       precision fires makes long-range kill chains not just useful, but foundational
       to deterrence and combat credibility.
       Long-range kill chains function when the strategic, operational, and tactical
       levels of intelligence operate as a single, synchronized enterprise. Long-
       range engagements demand rapid cycles of sensing, sense-making, and
       shooting, and none of those phases can succeed in isolation. The DCGS
       provides the fused intelligence picture and manages the theater’s DOF, the
       AOC ISRD transforms that picture into operational direction, tasking, and
       timing, the IC supplies the global context, strategic warning, and specialized
       expertise that sharpen every decision. Together, they create a seamless
       architecture where sensors are positioned deliberately, targets are validated
       with confidence, and shooters receive precise, time-dominant intelligence.
       Without this triad working in concert, long-range kill chains would be slow,
       fragmented, and vulnerable to adversary disruption.

  CHAPTER 2: COMMAND AND CONTROL, FORCE PRESENTATION,
                   AND ORGANIZATION
Air component intelligence forces provide the foundation for a commander’s ability to
understand, anticipate, and shape the OE. They generate decision-quality intelligence,
integrate seamlessly with joint and IC partners, and execute a spectrum of activities that
include indications and warning, targeting, characterizing the threat environment,
performing collection, and assessment. These forces operate through a mix of assigned
and attached elements, with authorities and support responsibilities shifting based on
command relationships. Their effectiveness is amplified through robust support networks,
federated production centers, national level agencies, and other reachback organizations.
These extend analytic capacity and ensure the air component maintains a persistent,
comprehensive understanding of the adversary.

ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES
Command relationships delineate the degree of authority commanders have over forces.
Understanding these authorities and how they fit into the planning and direction portion
of the intelligence process is critical for intelligence operations.
DEPARTMENT OF WAR
The SecWar apportions national, strategic, and theater ISR assets to best meet all CCDR
requirements. Approved apportionment decisions are delivered to the joint force in a
monthly allocated directive (MAD), which identifies assets, units, and sortie rates for that
theater. These allocation tables are then used to identify what ISR assets are available
for tasking, which ultimately supports the development of the ISR Operations Strategy.
COMBATANT COMMANDER
A combatant commander (CCDR) employs assigned and attached intelligence forces to
achieve national and theater objectives. When additional forces are required, CCDRs
submit a request for forces (RFF) through the global force management (GFM) process.
When authorized, CCMDs may also coordinate to enable the cooperative use of assets
not regularly assigned to them to improve coverage. Based on guidance and direction
from the CCDR, intelligence (J2) and operations (J3) staffs develop an overall theater
collection strategy and posture to execute intelligence operations.
The CCDR establishes priorities for intelligence operations that align with national and
theater objectives and ensures theater planning efforts support the fulfillment of crisis
intelligence requirements. High-priority, time-sensitive requirements are identified and
pre-validated by the JFC for the air component commander to consider for dynamic
retasking during the execution of intelligence operations. When theater requirements
exceed the capacity of assigned and attached forces, the JFC or air component
commander may submit requests for additional ISR capabilities (personnel, platforms)
through the CCMD. Taskings involving ISR assets supporting more than one JFC are
coordinated and deconflicted by a common superior commander.

The CCDR may delegate operational control (OPCON) or tactical control (TACON) over
theater ISR assets to appropriate subordinate commanders. However, the CCDR retains
the authority to validate and prioritize requirements for collection by theater ISR assets.
At the theater level, CCDRs exercise collection management authority (CMA) for
collection operations within their area of responsibility (AOR). 9
Collection Management Authority (CMA). CMA is the authority to establish, prioritize,
and validate theater collection requirements, establish sensor tasking guidance, and
develop theater-wide collection policies. Commanders exercising OPCON over
intelligence forces may assume CMA of tasked ISR assets as part of the delegation of
authority. The theater J2 usually retains CMA to validate, modify, or non-concur theater
intelligence requirements through the CCDR’s joint intelligence operations center (JIOC).
CMA usually includes the authority to task geospatial-intelligence sensors and
lower-echelon signals intelligence (SIGINT) collection systems with more localized
collection capabilities. The National Security Agency (NSA) retains CMA over
strategic-capable SIGINT ISR systems. In addition, CCDRs may request, and
subsequently receive, temporary SIGINT operational tasking authority (SOTA) over
theater-wide capable platforms and sensors. The delegation of SOTA to a CCDR and
subsequent delegation of this authority to the JFC ensures the theater can prioritize
requirements and focus SIGINT collection where needed to carry out assigned missions.
Collection management includes two complementary functions: collection requirements
management (CRM) and collection operations management (COM).
 Collection Requirements Management (CRM). CRM is the authoritative
  development and control of collection, processing, exploitation, and information
  reporting requirements. CRM defines what intelligence systems should collect.
 Collection Operations Management (COM). COM is the authoritative direction,
  scheduling, and control of specific collection operations and associated processing,
  exploitation, and information reporting resources. The COM process specifies how to
  satisfy the requirement and provides details on how the collection occurs.
The CCDR’s staff is responsible for developing a collection strategy and execution
posture for ISR missions and coordinating with national agencies. The theater J2 reviews,
validates, and prioritizes all outstanding intelligence requirements, whether originating
with the J2 staff or subordinate components. In conjunction with component staffs, the
JFC's staff is responsible for developing a shared production architecture that leverages
reachback, distributed, and federated partners for intelligence exploitation and analysis.
This shared production architecture also leverages support from organic capabilities at
the component and JTF levels, the Services, other CCDRs, foreign partners, and national
agencies. This helps maximize coverage of intelligence requirements.

THE INTELLIGENCE PROCESS
The joint intelligence process provides the basis for common intelligence terminology and
procedures. It consists of six interrelated categories of intelligence operations and serves
as a comprehensive framework to guide intelligence activities of the joint services and
broader IC. This joint process feeds analytical efforts via the joint intelligence preparation
of the operational environment (JIPOE), a four-step analytical process used by joint
intelligence organizations to produce estimates and other intelligence products in support
of the JFC’s decision-making process. In contrast to JIPOE, Services use the intelligence
preparation of the battlespace (IPB). IPB mirrors the four-step process of JIPOE, but
differs in the purpose, focus, and level of detail. Service component intelligence personnel
provide force specific, detailed analysis via IPB that supports component operations and
complements the broader JIPOE. The USAF intelligence enterprise contributes to every
step of the JIPOE, IPB, and the joint intelligence process. The USAF is the only service
to refer to the joint intelligence process as an acronym (PCPADE). 16
Planning and Direction. Planning and direction set the foundation for the entire
intelligence process. Leaders identify what they need to know, establish priorities, and
issue guidance that shapes all subsequent intelligence activities. This phase ensures that
limited resources are focused on the most important questions, aligning intelligence
efforts with operational goals and strategic objectives.
Collection. Collection involves gathering raw information from a wide range of sources:
human, signals, imagery, open source, and more. The goal is to acquire relevant data
that can answer the requirements established during planning. Effective collection
balances speed, accuracy, and risk while ensuring coverage across all required domains.
Processing and Exploitation. Once information is collected, it must be converted into
usable form. Processing and exploitation include translating, decrypting, organizing, and
filtering raw data so analysts can work with it. This step transforms large volumes of
unrefined material into structured information, enabling the identification of patterns,
anomalies, or actionable details.
Analysis and Production. Analysts evaluate processed information, integrate data from
multiple sources, and produce assessments that explain what is happening and why it
matters. They apply critical thinking, contextual knowledge, and analytic methodologies
to turn information into insight. The final products: reports, briefings, and estimates,
providing decision-makers with clear, relevant, and timely intelligence.
Dissemination and Integration. This phase ensures intelligence reaches the right
people at the right time and is incorporated into planning and operations. This includes
distributing reports, coordinating with partners, and maintaining feedback loops to refine

PLANNING AND DIRECTION
Air component intelligence plays a pivotal role in meeting the CCDR and JFACC’s most
pressing information needs, as formally expressed in Commander Critical Information
Requirements (CCIRs). The subset of these requirements related to the adversary and
the OE are known as priority intelligence requirements (PIRs), and they serve as the
primary drivers for all intelligence activities. To effectively answer these high-level PIRs,
the intelligence enterprise breaks them down into more specific, manageable questions
called essential elements of information (EEIs). These EEIs guide the detailed planning
and execution of intelligence collection and analysis. When an intelligence question
cannot be answered with organic assets or available information, the formal RFI process
is initiated, tasking the broader IC to leverage its vast resources. This process ensures
that the entire intelligence apparatus, from tactical collection assets to national level
analytical centers, is synchronized and focused on providing the specific intelligence the
commander needs to make critical decisions.
Commander Critical Information Requirements (CCIR). Owned by the commander,
CCIRs are composed of PIRs and Friendly Forces Information Requirements (FFIR). To
focus the staff and prioritize resources on information that directly impacts the
commander’s decision.
Intelligence Requirement (IR). Any subject, general or specific, upon which there is a
need for the collection of information or the production of intelligence. Results in RFI, a
collection requirement (CR), or a production requirement.
Priority Intelligence Requirement (PIR). Owned by the commander but developed by
the staff. The intelligence component of commander’s CCIRs focused the employment of
limited intelligence assets and resources against competing demands for intelligence
support. These are used to drive intelligence collection and analysis against the most
critical adversary related knowledge gaps. These are the foundation of the ISR strategy.
Essential Elements of Information (EEI). Produced by intelligence staff, these are the
most critical information requirements regarding the enemy or adversary, and the
environment needed by the commander at a particular time, to be used in conjunction
with other relevant information and intelligence to assist in reaching a logical decision.
These translate the commanders’ broad questions into specific questions for tasking
assets and analysts.
Request for Information (RFI). Produced by any staff element, these are any specific,
time-sensitive, ad hoc requirements for intelligence information or products to support an
ongoing crisis or operation not necessarily related to standing requirements or scheduled

intelligence production. They are normally the action oriented mechanism for seeking
answers to EEIs and other information needs from the intelligence enterprise.
Collection Requirement (CR). A valid need to close a specific gap in intelligence
holdings in direct response to a RFI.
Production Requirement (PR). An intelligence requirement that cannot be met by
current analytical products, resulting in tasking to produce a new product that can meet
this intelligence requirement.
SUPPORT TO THE AIR PLAN
Joint Planning Process for Air (JPPA). JPPA is driven by clearly defined information
requirements, and intelligence is the element that gives those requirements meaning.
JPPA translates the JFC’s strategic aims into executable operational airpower, and
intelligence underpins every decision within that process. Intelligence professionals are
embedded throughout, ensuring commanders and planners understand the adversary,
the environment, and the opportunities and risks inherent in each COA. 17
Intelligence analysts enter planning with a detailed understanding of the adversary’s
capabilities, vulnerabilities, order of battle, decision-making processes, and likely/most
dangerous COA. This understanding shapes the commander’s operational approach and
informs the development of feasible, acceptable, and effective airpower options. All
source analysts develop the adversary picture, assess intent, identify critical
vulnerabilities, and anticipate adversary reactions across the range of potential friendly
actions. Collection strategists integrate sensor capabilities into planning by
recommending collection allocations, conducting feasibility assessments, estimating ISR
gain-loss, and ensuring alignment with component requirements and available
capabilities. Targeting strategists guide the development of the operational targeting
strategy, ensuring target systems are understood, desired effects are achievable, and
analyst teams can match the right targets to the commanders’ objectives. Together, these
intelligence roles ensure that JPPA is grounded in a realistic understanding of battlespace
and that airpower options are aligned with both strategic aims and operational realities.

COLLECTION
Collection operations acquire raw data about the OE and provide it to intelligence
processing elements. This involves the detailed tasking and synchronization of ISR
sensor platforms and exploitation resources to characterize the OE, adversary activities,
and infrastructure, and to support the targeting cycle. The overarching purpose of
collection operations is typically driven by the need for battlespace characterization,
targeting, test assumptions, confirm knowledge, identify intelligence gaps, and enhance
the commander’s decision advantage. This is done through collection management,
which is the process of converting intelligence requirements into collection requirements
(CRs), establishing priorities, tasking or coordinating with appropriate collection sources
or agencies, monitoring results, and retasking, as required.
Collection operations orchestrate three distinct but related functions: collection
management authority (CMA), collection requirements management (CRM), and
collections operations management (COM). At the operational level, the ISRD is central
to this process and is considered the most suitable entity for conducting COM for theater

requests for limited ISR capabilities and develops the draft joint integrated prioritized
collection list (JIPCL) to task these assets effectively.

critical intelligence gaps. The data gathered through RSTA missions feeds PED activities,

coordinates with the theater J2 staff to determine asset availability, evaluates sensors
using the AFAT methodology (access, fidelity, accuracy, timeliness) for imagery or signals
requirements, and supports targeting and BDA priorities.

information across a wide area. To mitigate threats or political sensitivities, these
platforms may operate in a standoff capacity, which can limit the range and depth of
sensor coverage. A critical consideration for all airborne ISR is the significant PED
support required to turn collected data into usable intelligence, which can be

When dedicated ISR assets are insufficient, planners should integrate non-traditional ISR
(NTISR) platforms, such as strike aircraft, to supplement collection. This tactic,
sometimes called operations reconnaissance (ops recce), leverages the advanced
sensors on multi-role aircraft to increase battlespace awareness. The successful use of
NTISR requires clear, pre-defined mission authorities that distinguish between aircraft
control, which remains with the pilot-in-command, and the separate authorities for sensor
control and sensor tasking. This ensures that even when an aircraft is performing multiple
roles, the C2 of its intelligence collection function is unambiguous and effectively
integrated into the broader ISR plan.
Space-based Systems. Space-based intelligence systems are integral to modern
military operations, offering the primary advantage of persistent, wide-area coverage over
denied areas where airborne or ground assets cannot operate. Their longevity and
reduced vulnerability provide commanders with critical information to assess situations
and develop plans. However, these systems are a limited resource, as demand often
exceeds capacity, and their orbital mechanics can create coverage gaps. Furthermore,
they can be susceptible to adversary denial and deception techniques, necessitating a
clear understanding of their capabilities and limitations during planning.
The US employs a variety of military, national, and non-military space systems to support
its objectives. Military and national satellite systems, controlled by the DoW and IC
respectively, provide a broad range of capabilities from environmental monitoring to
detailed imagery intelligence. Access to these high-demand assets requires detailed
justification and must be coordinated well in advance through the appropriate command
channels. To augment these limited resources, commanders can also leverage
non-military systems, including allied or commercial assets, though access often requires
pre-negotiated legal arrangements that should be established prior to the start of
operations.
Ground-based Systems. Ground-based intelligence systems are critical components of
the collection architecture, tasked worldwide to gather information for disciplines such as
SIGINT and Measurement and Signature Intelligence (MASINT), fulfilling national,
theater, and local requirements. These systems include air surveillance and target
acquisition radars, which, while primarily for air control, also provide valuable threat
warning within designated areas. However, these radar systems are limited by
atmospheric conditions, electronic jamming, and line-of-sight constraints.
Beyond tactical air surveillance, significant ground-based ISR resources are dedicated to
missile warning and space surveillance. Ground-based missile warning sensors primarily
identify and characterize ballistic missile attacks, while also contributing to space
surveillance. The Space Surveillance Network (SSN) actively tracks and characterizes
artificial objects in space, providing crucial data on adversary space order of battle,
overflight warnings, and spacecraft status. This information offers theater commanders
early warning and supports denial and deception efforts against potential threats.

PROCESSING AND EXPLOITATION
Intelligence collection operations are executed through several core intelligence
disciplines, each employing unique methods and platforms to acquire raw data from a
specific domain. Each discipline has an IC and DoW functional manager. Air component
intelligence should coordinate upward with the JTF J2 through the CCMD J2 to IC
agencies to ensure collection elements use appropriate reporting methods with a
response time sufficient to meet air component commander requirements. 22
GEOSPATIAL INTELLIGENCE (GEOINT)
GEOINT is the exploitation and analysis of imagery and geospatial information to
describe, assess, and visually depict physical features and geographically referenced
activities on or about the Earth. GEOINT consists of imagery, IMINT, and geospatial
information. GEOINT data sources include commercial satellites, government satellites,
aircraft, maps, commercial databases, census information, global positioning system
waypoints, and utility schematics. GEOINT can synthesize intelligence and data into
conceptualized geographic spatial content that can provide commanders key operational
intelligence (e.g., best vantage point for shooters, most advantageous entry points, and
spatial trends and patterns). The National Geospatial Intelligence Agency (NGA) serves
as the IC and DoW functional manager for GEOINT.
IMINT is the technical, geographic, and intelligence information derived through
interpreting or analyzing imagery and collateral materials. IMINT includes the exploitation
of imagery data derived from electro-optical, radar, infrared, multi-spectral, and laser
sensors. IMINT results from the processing and exploitation of raw imagery (information)
to create an analyzed product (intelligence). IMINT is used for historical comparisons, to
locate adversary military forces and facilities, and to provide insight into the adversary’s
capabilities. IMINT is also valuable for understanding the physical terrain and human
aspects like significant cultural sites (e.g., government structures, historical sites, and
schools), agriculture and urban infrastructure, water, and electrical grids. The air
component should leverage the imagery support element (ISE) specialty team in the
ISRD for imagery production, tailored analysis, and/or exploitation.

HUMAN INTELLIGENCE (HUMINT)
HUMINT is an intelligence collection discipline that uses people in an area of interest to
identify or provide insight into adversary plans and intentions, research and development,
strategy, doctrine, and capabilities. Dedicated HUMINT collectors amplify, clarify, or verify
information collected by other airborne, ground-based, or space-based assets. In many
cases, HUMINT may be the best source regarding adversary intentions. The USAF has
an organic HUMINT capability and works with DoW and other national-level agencies to
collect on priority USAF operational and strategic requirements. The Director of the CIA
serves as the National HUMINT Manager, and the Director of the DIA serves as the DoW
functional manager.
SIGNALS INTELLIGENCE (SIGINT)
SIGINT is a category of intelligence comprised of COMINT, ELINT, and FISINT, that may
consist of an individual element, or a combination. SIGINT uses intercepted
electromagnetic emissions to provide information on adversary forces' capabilities,
intentions, formations, and locations. SIGINT also includes collecting, processing, and
exploiting data from information in cyberspace. The air component’s link to the SIGINT
enterprise is the National Tactical Integration (NTI) specialty team in the ISRD. The NSA
serves as the IC functional manager for SIGINT. 23
Communications Intelligence (COMINT). COMINT consists of information derived from
intercepting and monitoring the adversary's communications systems. COMINT exploits
an adversary’s communications, revealing capabilities, intentions, perceived
vulnerabilities, and, often, an adversary’s perception of the US and multinational partners.
Electronic Intelligence (ELINT). ELINT is intelligence derived from interception and
analysis of non-communications emitters, such as radars. ELINT includes both
operational electronic intelligence (OPELINT) and technical electronic intelligence
(TECHELINT). OPELINT focuses on operationally relevant information (location,
movement, employment, tactics, and activity of foreign non-communications emitters and
associated weapons systems). TECHELINT concentrates on the technical aspects
(signal characteristics, modes, functions, associations, capabilities, limitations,
vulnerabilities, and technology levels).
Foreign Instrumentation Signature Intelligence (FISINT). FISINT consists of technical
information derived by intercepting electromagnetic emissions associated with testing
and operational deployment of foreign air and space, surface, and subsurface systems.
These emissions are typically generated by telemetry, electronic interrogators, and video
data links. The technical details of foreign weapons system development derived from
FISINT can provide insight into foreign capabilities, identify avenues for exploitation,
and/or aid the employment of appropriate countermeasures. FISINT is accomplished by
specialized, national-level Service and DoW organizations.

MEASUREMENT AND SIGNATURE INTELLIGENCE (MASINT)
MASINT is technically derived intelligence to detect, locate, and describe the physical
measurements and signatures intrinsic to targets and events. MASINT collects and
produces precise threat characteristics and performance information essential to support
planning, development, and application of weapon systems, countermeasures, targeting,
and battle damage assessment. MASINT is especially useful to monitor adversary
technical developments and deployments, as well as emerging threats from weapons of
mass destruction. DIA leads the IC effort for MASINT via senior science and technology
officers (SSTOs) who integrate DIA efforts to meet operational intelligence requirements.
OPEN-SOURCE INTELLIGENCE (OSINT)
OSINT is intelligence derived from publicly available information (PAI) that is collected,
exploited, and disseminated in a timely manner to an appropriate audience for the
purpose of addressing a specific intelligence requirement. PAI is information that has
been published or broadcast for public consumption, available on request to the public,
accessible online, could be seen or heard by a casual observer, or obtained by visiting a
place or event open to the public. OSINT complements other intelligence disciplines and
may cue collection and analysis, fill intelligence gaps, and supplement the accuracy and
fidelity of classified information databases. Importantly, OSINT is susceptible to
manipulation and deception and requires research expertise and OPSEC for internet-
based activities. The Director of the CIA is the IC functional manager for OSINT, and the
DIA Director is the DoW’s functional manager.
TECHNICAL INTELLIGENCE (TECHINT)
TECHINT is intelligence produced by collecting, processing, analyzing, and exploiting
data and information regarding foreign equipment to prevent technological surprise,
assess foreign scientific and technical capabilities, and develop countermeasures to
neutralize an adversary’s technological advantages. TECHINT begins with the acquisition
or recovery of a foreign piece of equipment or foreign scientific or technological
information. This work is typically performed in a forward-deployed location by a joint
captured materiel exploitation center (JCMEC) managed by DIA and OPCON to the
CCMD. 24

General Military Intelligence (GMI). GMI is intelligence concerning foreign countries'
military capabilities or topics affecting potential US or multinational military operations.
Current intelligence and GMI form a symbiotic relationship. Information gained during the
development of current intelligence forms the basis for the GMI effort and other analytical
products. Conversely, GMI provides underlying threat information to produce accurate
and meaningful current intelligence.
GMI focuses on a wide range of factors, including order of battle, organization, DOF,
training, tactics, and overall strategy to determine a nation's military strength and
effectiveness. GMI also analyzes critical supporting elements such as transportation
networks, military materiel production, military and civilian communications systems, and
military economics, including any foreign military assistance. Furthermore, it considers
the potential use of asymmetric tactics or terror attacks and involves the detailed location,
identification, and description of military-related installations. This analysis results in key
products such as military-related infrastructure assessments, which detail an adversary’s
capabilities and warfighting sustainability, and military capabilities assessments, which
determine an adversary’s potential to employ force by evaluating their order of battle,

Scientific and Technical Intelligence (S&TI). S&TI examines foreign developments in
basic and applied sciences and technologies with potential for military use, particularly to
enhance weapon systems. S&TI is a product that results from collection, evaluation,
analysis, and interpretation of foreign scientific and technical information. It covers foreign
developments in basic applied research and applied engineering techniques. S&TI also
covers scientific and technical characteristics, capabilities, and limitations of foreign
military systems. This analysis includes weapons, weapon systems, and materiel, the
related research and development, and the methods used to manufacture the foreign
military system. Example S&TI products include weapon system characteristics,
capabilities, vulnerabilities, limitations, effectiveness, research and development, and
related manufacturing information. S&TI products play a vital role in the acquisition
process by allowing the procurement of systems or systems upgrades to meet current,
developing, and future threats.
Acquisition Intelligence. Acquisition intelligence addresses future threats, specializing
in S&TI to anticipate enemy capabilities in advance by one to five years. Acquisitions
intelligence brings a focus on supportability issues associated with new ISR capabilities
planned to be brought into theater. It is especially important for multinational operations.
Identity Intelligence. Identity intelligence results from the fusion of identity attributes
(biologic, biographic, behavioral, and reputational information related to individuals), and
other information and intelligence associated with those attributes, collected across
various intelligence disciplines. The goal of identity intelligence is to discover unknown
potential threat actors by connecting individuals to other persons, places, events, or
materials, analyzing patterns of life, and characterizing the threat posed to US interests.
Target Intelligence. Intelligence operations play a prominent role in the targeting cycle
by detecting, locating, and identifying targets and supporting mission planning and
assessment. Target intelligence portrays and locates the components of a target or target
complex and indicates its vulnerability and relative importance. Required products, such
as target imagery, should be immediately available to support the air tasking cycle. ISR
assets may be employed to detect and identify potential targets, changes to existing
targets, or to conduct battle damage assessment. Multiple missions may be required to
provide the level of detail necessary to support the precision engagement of specific
targets. Target intelligence products include annotated target graphics, electronic target
folders, target system analyses, and geospatial information to provide comprehensive
data needed to plan and generate lethal and nonlethal effects.
Targeting within the air tasking cycle operates inside a compressed 72-hr window. Here,
the focus shifts from foundational target development to the selection and prioritization of
already developed targets from the restricted target list (RTL) or joint target list (JTL) for
inclusion on the component target list (CTL). Intelligence analysts and targeteers attend
joint targeting working groups (JTWG) and joint targeting coordination boards (JTCB) to
approve targets for the JIPTL to meet commander’s objectives. Regardless of the cycle,
intelligence personnel constantly evaluate the adversary and update target information,
ensuring that targeting, whether deliberate or dynamic, is based on the most current and

accurate intelligence available. 26
Current Intelligence. Current intelligence is produced by fusing data and intelligence
products regarding the current situation in a particular area or the activities of specific
groups. This type of intelligence is similar to warning intelligence in that both require
continuous monitoring of world events and specific activities in an operational area.
Information required to produce current intelligence products includes continuous
assessments of adversary intentions, capabilities, and the will to use military force, as
well as the identification of potential centers of gravity, operational plans, and
vulnerabilities. It also encompasses geographic, environmental, and social analysis of the
operational area, monitoring significant military and political events, and the status of
strategic transportation nodes such as major airfields and seaports. A critical component
of current intelligence is maintaining the adversary's DOF, a product of the DCGS
enterprise that provides the precise geographic location and status of known units,
directly enabling the Find, Fix, Track (F2T) phase of the dynamic targeting process.
Estimative Intelligence. Estimative intelligence provides forecasts of current or potential
situations that may have bearing on the planning and execution of military operations,
otherwise known as estimates. By employing pattern analysis, inference, and statistical
probability techniques, intelligence personnel produce estimates describing relevant
actors’ capabilities and behavior.
Warning Intelligence. Warning intelligence products are derived from a worldwide
system that analyzes and integrates information to assess the probability of hostile
actions and provide sufficient warning to preempt or counter their effects. Warning
intelligence systems rely on information and indications from sources at all levels. The
focus of warning intelligence products varies at each echelon and is most specific at the
operational and tactical levels. Warning intelligence products are specifically designed to
alert decision-makers to emerging threats and potential crises, focusing on adversary
intentions, capabilities, past behaviors, and doctrine. This includes monitoring foreign
government responses to crises, analyzing significant political, economic, or social
situations in both friendly and adversary states that could trigger instability, and tracking
changes in adversary force dispositions, military activities, and mobilization status.
Furthermore, warning intelligence examines adversary disinformation capabilities and
observes civil or bureaucratic activities that might foreshadow subsequent military action,
alongside continuously assessing the status of other military forces within an operational
area.
Counterintelligence (CI). CI includes strategic analysis to identify and produce all-
source finished intelligence on foreign intelligence threats to friendly operations. CI
develops and implements strategies and action plans to counter the CI threat, tasks CI
collection capabilities, and leverages all other intelligence disciplines to fill CI collection
gaps.
CI is critical to USAF operations, particularly force protection efforts. CI threat estimates
and vulnerability assessments identify friendly weaknesses and vulnerabilities that may

DISSEMINATION AND INTEGRATION
The dissemination and integration step involves the delivery to and use of intelligence.
Dissemination ensures commanders, planners, and operational forces receive finished
intelligence in time to make effective decisions and execute operations. The USAF’s
distributed operations capability provides timely and tailored intelligence across the globe

to multiple end-users.
Dissemination is accomplished through various means, each requiring continuous
management (e.g., electronic transmission, hardcopy annotated imagery and maps,
direct threat warnings, oral and written reports, briefings, and various servers allowing
structured discovery and retrieval). When managed ineffectively, communications paths
can become saturated by information retransmitted by numerous intermediate collection
agencies, resulting in circular reporting. Advances in cyberspace capabilities and
technologies should be leveraged to improve dissemination and reduce the information-to
production timeline for delivery of intelligence products. Importantly, intelligence planning
should include procedures for rapidly coordinating with public affairs for the public release
of appropriately declassified intelligence.
Integration ensures disseminated intelligence is employed effectively. After intelligence
products have been disseminated, intelligence organizations are responsible for
continuing to support users as they integrate the intelligence into decision-making and
planning processes. Continuous integration of intelligence and operations allow
commanders and operational planners access to the most current information. This
access optimizes intelligence support to operations planning, preparation, execution, and
assessment functions.
Common Intelligence Feed/Picture (CIF/P): A flexible display of relevant, instructive,
and contextual intelligence information regarding adversary, and neutral force disposition,
and supporting infrastructures derived from all sources at any level of classification,
shared by more than one command, which facilitates collaborative planning and assists
all echelons to enhance situational awareness and decision-making.
The CIP/F represents the synthesized product of the entire intelligence enterprise. It is
the authoritative, shared source of ground truth about the adversary, flowing up from the
tactical edge and back down from the strategic level. This critical warfighting function is
executed at each echelon: within the AOC, the ISRD develops and maintains the
operational level picture, supported by the DGS. At the tactical level, the CIC is
responsible for maintaining the intelligence picture for its wing, ensuring immediate
relevance to aircrew and combat operations. 28

EVALUATION AND FEEDBACK
All operations in the intelligence process are interrelated and should be evaluated to
determine the degree to which they facilitate each other and succeed in meeting
intelligence requirements. Importantly, evaluation and feedback are not an independent
operation in the intelligence process. Rather, it should be conducted continuously to
assess the intelligence process. The goal is to identify issues as early as possible to
minimize information gaps, mitigate capability shortfalls, and ensure intelligence
operations meet established analysis tradecraft and objectivity standards.
Evaluation and feedback require a collaborative dialogue between intelligence planners,

Assessment Methods. An ISR strategy is not complete unless it includes a qualitative
and quantitative ISR assessment. Assessments provide leaders with information to
reprioritize intelligence requirements, shift collection emphasis, change analytic levels of
effort, and reallocate available ISR assets. To assess intelligence activities and
operations, intelligence planners develop and document intelligence measures of
performance (MOPs) and intelligence measures of effectiveness (MOEs).
 Measurement of Performance (MOP). An indicator used to measure a friendly
  action that is tied to measuring task accomplishment.
    MOPs are informed by quantitative indicators. They indicate whether a particular
    platform or sensor is performing as expected: e.g., the number of sorties conducted
    or number of images taken.
 Measurement of Effectiveness (MOE). An indicator used to measure a current
  system state, with change indicated by comparing multiple observations over time.
    MOEs gauge the impact of intelligence tasks performed within the intelligence
    process. MOE indicators tend to be qualitative and related to the value of the
    intelligence produced.
