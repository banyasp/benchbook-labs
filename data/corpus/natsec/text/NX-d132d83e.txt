                                               Introduction

In an era of modern warfare defined by a sensor-saturated battlefield and unprecedented lethality,
reconnaissance elements must see without being seen to survive and achieve mission success.
The proliferation of enemy drones and the reach of precision fires mean that any pause in
movement or gap in observation can expose a unit to rapid and decisive destruction. This
publication provides a critical methodology for today’s soldier, detailing how to fully integrate
Group 2 small, unmanned aircraft systems (sUAS) as a co-equal maneuver element to dominate
this lethal environment. Group 2 is a category of UAS for tactical operations and sits between
smaller Group 1 and larger Group 3-5 offering a balance of mobility, endurance, and sensor
capability for frontline missions. The publication directly confronts the common but dangerous
practice of using drones as episodic tools, which creates vulnerabilities and slows operational
tempo.

Instead, this article introduces the “ground-air screen bounding cycle,” a deliberate and
repeatable four-step process which is to clear, occupy, secure, and establish. It synchronizes
ground and air movements. The reader will learn how this cycle extends observation depth,
preserves momentum, and enhances survivability for scout sections enabling them to maintain
continuous layered observation while mitigating the ever-present threat of enemy counter-
reconnaissance in complex terrain.

The Ground-air Screen Bounding Cycle

Reconnaissance formations that integrate Group 2 sUAS1 as a co-equal maneuver element gain a
decisive advantage in depth, tempo, and survivability. Too often, UAS are employed as episodic
collection tools rather than synchronized partners in movement. When treated as an attachment
instead of a mutually supporting element, the platform creates pauses in reconnaissance, exposes
launch sites, and fractures observation continuity. To remain hidden while collecting detailed
information,2 scout sections must deliberately pair ground maneuver with aerial observation in a
repeatable pattern that preserves concealment, maintains standoff, and prevents exposure gaps
across successive terrain features. Figure 1 depicts a movement technique of a scout section.

Scout sections that employ a deliberate ground–air screen bounding cycle and pairing platform-
led clearance with ground occupation, security, and displacement, can achieve continuous
mutually supported reconnaissance that extends observation depth, preserves tempo, and reduces
risk across complex terrain. This publication outlines that cycle at both the macro and micro
level, explains the geometry required to sustain it, and demonstrates how layered sensing
between scouts and a Group 2 sUAS creates a resilient reconnaissance posture that endures
friction, weather, and enemy counter-reconnaissance.

The Four-step Methodology

The ground-air team advances through a deliberate, repeating pattern known as the ground-air
screen bounding cycle. In this cycle, the drone clears forward terrain while the scouts maintain a
screen. The scouts then occupy the newly cleared position as the drone continues to cover their
movement. Once at position, the scouts secure the site while the drone team recovers the
platform and displaces to a preselected launch point. From there, the drone team establishes the
new launch site under the protection of the scouts’ forward screen. This four-step rhythm of
clear, occupy, secure, and establish preserves tempo, maintains continuous observation, and
ensures both elements remain mutually supported as they progress through successive terrain
features.

1. Clear

The cycle begins with the drone clearing forward terrain while the scouts maintain a protected
screen from their current elevation feature. As the platform pushes into areas the scouts cannot
observe without risking exposure, the drone team identifies potential ground observation sites for
the scouts as well as concealed terrain-masked launch sites for its own follow-on displacement.
This early identification of positions preserves reconnaissance reach and sets the conditions for a
smooth continuous advance.

2. Occupy

Once the drone confirms the area is clear, the scouts advance to occupy the next position while
the drone maintains a forward screen. As they move, the scouts validate the previously identified
launch site to ensure concealment, masking, and communications reliability before occupying
their own observation point. This confirmation step ensures the ground–air team remains
synchronized and maintains continuous observation throughout the bound.

3. Secure

After occupation, the scouts secure the position by refining observation sectors, confirming blind
spots, and validating terrain assessments made from the air. During this consolidation period, the
drone crew recovers the platform, conducts rapid checks, and displaces to the launch site
confirmed by the scouts. This sequencing ensures the reconnaissance element retains tempo
while avoiding exposure gaps.

4. Establish

With the scouts maintaining the forward screen, the drone team establishes its new launch site on
the reverse slope or other terrain-masked location. Once emplaced, the team verifies
connectivity, prepares the platform, and sets conditions for the next forward clearance.
Establishing this site completes the cycle and resets the paired mutually supporting movement
that defines ground–air screen bounding. Figure 2 depicts team actions during ground-air
bounding cycle.

The Cycle in Action

Step 1: Platform-led Clearing and Site Identification

The sequence begins with the drone team launching the sUAS from a concealed, terrain-masked
position behind the intervisibility line (IV) while the ground scouts hold a forward screen. Once
airborne, the platform clears the scouts’ next intended terrain focusing on dead space, vegetation
corridors, and micro terrain that scouts cannot observe from covered positions. Simultaneously,
the UAS identifies viable scout observation sites and concealed follow-on launch locations,
evaluating each for cover, concealment, communications reliability, and access routes. This
forward clearance confirms the absence of enemy presence or obstacles, allowing the ground
element to maneuver with confidence and reducing the risk of unexpected contact during
displacement. Figure 3 depicts air operators clearing and ground scouts occupying the
observation post.

Step 2: Ground Scouts Occupy the Cleared Position and Validate the Launch Site

Once the platform verifies the route and confirms the next occupation site, the ground scout team
advances through the cleared terrain under the protection of the Group 2 UAS’s forward screen.
As they move, the scouts validate the previously identified launch site to ensure it provides
adequate concealment, masking, access, and communications reliability for the UAS team’s
upcoming displacement. After confirming the launch site, the scouts continue to their own
occupation point that is preferably an elevated feature or at minimum, positioned roughly 300
meters short of the reconnaissance objective to preserve standoff, observation depth, and
survivability. Establishing this position provides the section with a secure vantage point that
supports reconnaissance, enables continuous observation, and sets the conditions for the next
iteration of the ground–air screen bounding cycle. Figure 4 depicts ground scouts securing and
air operators occupying.

Step 3: Scouts Secure while the Drone Team Recovers the Platform

After the ground scouts reach their new observation site, they secure the position by refining
sectors, confirming blind spots, and establishing the forward screen. While the scouts
consolidate, the UAS team recovers the Group 2 platform at the previous location, conducts
rapid system checks, and prepares for displacement. This division of effort allows the scouts to
maintain uninterrupted situational awareness while the drone team resets the platform for the
next bound. By securing the position and recovering the UAS in parallel, the section preserves
tempo, avoids exposure gaps, and sets the conditions for a synchronized displacement to the
confirmed launch site.

Step 4: Drone Team Occupies the Launch Site as Scouts Maintain the Forward Screen

After recovering the platform and completing system checks, the UAS team displaces to the
launch site that the platform identified during Step 1 and the scouts validated during Step 2.
Moving under the protection of the scouts’ forward screen, the drone team occupies this terrain-
masked site and establishes the conditions necessary for the next launch such as verifying
concealment, communications reliability, and observation geometry. While the UAS team
emplaces at this new position, the scouts maintain vigilance over the reconnaissance objective
and adjacent avenues of approach. With both elements set, the section reinitiates the ground-air
screen bounding cycle, beginning again with a platform-led clearance of the next occupation
area.

Step 5: Platform Launch and Forward Clearance of the Next Scout Observation Site

With the UAS team established at its new launch site and the scouts maintaining their forward
screen, the drone team launches the Group 2 platform to initiate the next bound. The platform
immediately clears the scout’s intended named area of interest (NAI) focusing on micro-terrain,
vegetation corridors, and dead space hidden from their current position. As it conducts this
sweep, the platform also identifies a potential observation site that offers the scouts masked
access, reliable fields of view, and sufficient standoff from the objective. Throughout this
process, the scouts sustain their screen to protect the UAS team and ensure continuous
observation, setting the conditions for the next movement in the ground-air screen bounding
cycle. Figure 5 depicts platform screening forward as scouts occupy.

Step 6: Platform Screens Forward as Scouts Occupy and Confirm Their Observation Site

After the platform has cleared the immediate NAI, the UAS team pushes the Group 2 platform
forward to screen beyond the cleared terrain, orienting on likely threat avenues and concealed
movement corridors. While the platform maintains this forward screen, the scouts move to
occupy the newly identified observation site. Upon arrival, the scouts confirm the suitability of
the position by validating masking, fields of view, and observation geometry. This parallel action
preserves surveillance continuity while ensuring the ground element occupies a position that
supports both survivability and reconnaissance effectiveness.

With the scouts now covering the NAI, the UAS team recovers the Group 2 platform and
conducts essential reset tasks such as battery replacement, sensor alignment, and minor
adjustments to equipment. After completing the reset, the operators relaunch the platform and
push it deeper into the far edges of the NAI to clear terrain the scouts cannot feasibly observe
without exposing themselves. This sequencing maintains tempo, ensures sensor readiness, and
prevents exposure gaps across the depth of the NAI.

Step 8: Platform Provides Forward Screen and Early Warning

With the scouts secured in their observation site, the UAS team moves the Group 2 platform into
forward screening positions within the NAI. The platform orients on terrain the scouts cannot
observe due to vegetation, masking, or dead space, providing a sensor layer that extends the
section’s reach without exposing the ground element. In this phase, the platform’s immediate
purpose is to maintain a forward screen and cue the scouts to any detected movement or changes
in the environment. Once established, this posture transitions the section into its sustained-
observation phase, where the scouts retain their position and the platform continues to operate
forward of them. Figure 6 depicts both teams screening across a NAI.

Sustained Observation in the Area of Interest

Once the section occupies the NAI, the ground scout team orients on the most critical terrain.
This is likely enemy avenues of approach, key terrain features, and decision points linked to the
commander’s priority intelligence requirements. From their concealed observation site, the
scouts provide persistent high-fidelity surveillance and maintain continuity even when conditions
degrade air operations. Their role anchors the reconnaissance effort and ensures a constant
baseline of observation regardless of weather, signature concerns, or platform availability.

The UAS team complements this by employing the Group 2 platform to cover dead space and
masked terrain the scouts cannot observe without exposing their position. Operating forward of
the scouts, the platform monitors the near periphery and identifies early-warning indicators such
as movement, displacement, or enemy reconnaissance efforts approaching the section’s position.
Its advantage enables rapid cueing of the ground team and helps detect changes in the
environment before they become threats.

The scouts maintain uninterrupted observation until aerial sensing can safely resume if weather,
battery constraints, enemy counter-reconnaissance, or other factors force the platform to ground.
Together, the ground–air team creates a resilient layered surveillance posture that preserves
situational awareness, mitigates exposure risk, and supports continuous reconnaissance under
shifting tactical conditions. Figure 7 depicts sustained occupation of an observation post.

                                           Conclusion

Reconnaissance is not defined by how far a platform can see, but by how long a section can
observe without being decisively detected or disrupted. When a Group 2 sUAS is integrated as a
coequal maneuver element, the scout section gains more than extended optics because it gains
time, depth, and survivability. The ground-air screen bounding cycle provides a deliberate
rhythm that synchronizes clearance, occupation, security, and establishment into a continuous
mutually supporting movement. By pairing platform-led screening with disciplined ground
maneuver, the section eliminates observation gaps, preserves tempo, and advances across terrain
without surrendering concealment.

This approach is scalable, repeatable, and resilient under friction. If air operations are
constrained, scouts maintain observation. If terrain masks the ground element, the platform
extends reach. Together, the ground–air team creates a layered surveillance posture that sustains
reconnaissance across successive terrain features and throughout occupation of the NAI.
Integrating sUAS in this manner does not replace reconnaissance fundamentals, it reinforces
them. When geometry, timing, and mutual support are deliberately aligned, the scout section
regains the initiative in depth and preserves the commander’s ability to see first, understand first,
and act first.
