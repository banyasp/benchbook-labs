INTRODUCTION

The modern battlefield has fundamentally changed, becoming a transparent and hyper-lethal
environment where persistent sensors and long-range precision fires have blurred the traditional
lines of combat. Observations from the Joint Multinational Readiness Center (JMRC) show that
for brigade combat teams (BCTs) to succeed, they must proactively dominate their deep
operational areas before close-combat maneuver begins. This shift requires a new approach, as
waiting for higher echelons to shape the deep fight is no longer a viable strategy in large-scale
combat operations (LSCO).

Although the widespread availability of commercial unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) offers
commanders a revolutionary view of the battlespace, technology alone is not a panacea for
success. In contested environments characterized by aggressive electronic warfare (EW),
complex terrain, and sophisticated air defenses, technology often fails when it is needed most.
An over-reliance on these autonomous systems creates significant vulnerabilities, proving that
they cannot overcome the inherent friction of advanced threat environments on their own.

To achieve victory, BCTs must master the synthesis of human expertise with autonomous
technology. This is accomplished by turning staff procedures into a lethal advantage through
disciplined, friction-based rehearsals and maintaining lean, efficient battle rhythms. The most
critical element is the deliberate fusion of resilient staff processes with skilled human sensors,
such as joint fire support specialists, who can complement and validate autonomous platforms.
By integrating resilient human observation with intelligent technology, BCTs can win the crucial
battle of sensors and shooters and dominate the future fight.

THE BATTLE OF SENSORS AND SHOOTERS

In previous decades, the first exchange of direct fire between maneuver elements often defined
“contact.” In the fiscal year 2026 (FY26), LSCO environment at JMRC, contact begins
kilometers away and hours—if not days—before a crew chambers the first tank round. This
initial phase is the battle of sensors and shooters, a high-stakes competition between opposing
sensors and long-range shooters.

A simple brutal logic defines the duel: scouts target whatever they see, and fires destroy
whatever they target. The primary objective for the BCT during this phase is to degrade the
enemy’s sensor-to-shooter complex while protecting its own. The supported commander uses
indirect fires to create effects contributing to accomplishing their mission. The BCTs shaping of
the situation in depth with lethal and nonlethal means is important in assuring freedom of action
for their battalions and for sustaining the BCT’s offensive actions to greater depth. 1 Table 1
depicts the operational requirements with complimenting objectives.

The operational requirements and objectives detailed in the table above represent a foundational

dominance in counter-fire engagements, and saturating the battlespace with a diverse array of

the survivability and effectiveness of friendly forces but also imposes significant dilemmas on
the enemy, restricting their freedom of action and degrading their decision-making cycle.
Continuous refinement and adaptation of these operational requirements are essential to
maintaining a competitive edge in future conflicts.

The most critical observation from FY26 is that successful combined arms maneuver is now a
derivative of the deep fight. If a BCT fails to win the battle of sensors and shooters, its maneuver
elements will be attrited by indirect fires and loitering munitions before they ever reach the
close-combat area. Conversely, when the BCT aggressively defines and dominates its “deep
area,” it shapes the conditions for maneuver units to exploit enemy gaps with speed and violence
of action. Winning this duel requires a shift in mindset: the division’s deep fight is not a
secondary shaping operation, but the critical effort that determines the survival of the subordinate
brigade.

RECLAIMING THE BRIGADE DEEP AREA

Historically, BCTs have often operated under the assumption that the deep fight is exclusively
the domain of the division. In this outdated paradigm, the BCT focuses almost entirely on the
close fight, trusting higher echelons to shape the deep environment. However, FY26 JMRC
rotations demonstrated that waiting for the division to attrite enemy operational reserves,
command and control (C2) nodes, and sustainment areas is a fatal error in the modern LSCO
environment.

Successful BCTs are actively reclaiming the deep fight by defining and dominating a “brigade-
owned deep area.” At brigade echelons and below, differentiating between close, deep, and rear
may have less use during LSCO because of the high tempo, narrow focus, and short planning
horizons of close combat. At every echelon, however, commanders must understand the
relationships among these operations and their combined effect on mission accomplishment. 3
This requires commanders and staffs to aggressively use the decide, detect, deliver, and assess
(D3A) targeting methodology, not just to support the current maneuver phase, but to shape the
battlespace 48 to 72 hours in advance.

By pushing their influence beyond the coordinated fire line, brigades can systematically
dismantle the enemy’s ability to reinforce or sustain their forward elements. Table 2 depicts the
operational effects of the BCT targeting best practices during FY26 at JMRC.

Adhering to these best practices with the D3A framework ensures that BCTs maintain the
initiative, neutralize critical enemy capabilities, and conserve valuable resources. The integration
of organic sensors, fires, and robust assessment plans creates a lethal and responsive targeting
cycle capable of defeating peer adversaries in LSCO.

Successfully prosecuting the battle of sensors and shooters and dominating the brigade deep area
is not merely a matter of employing technologies or artillery; it is fundamentally a question
process. The ability to rapidly synchronize a complex web of sensors, intelligence feeds, and
joint fires against fleeting, high-value targets—as required by the D3A methodology—demands a
staff that can operate at a tempo exceeding traditional methods. This is where the strategic
imperative to ‘win in the deep fight’ meets the practical reality of execution. To achieve the
necessary speed, resilience, and lethality, a BCT cannot rely on outdated, bureaucratic staff
procedures. Instead, it must transform its internal operations into a finely tuned system, which is
accomplished by turning staff procedures into a lethal advantage.

TURNING STAFF PROCEDURES INTO A LETHAL ADVANTAGE

A brigade combat team can possess the most advanced technology and the most lethal artillery,
but the lack of a disciplined staff to synchronize them renders those assets ineffective. JMRC
FY26 rotations repeatedly showed that the operations process must focus on turning staff
procedures into a lethal advantage to move at the speed of modern combat. Staffs must transition
from administrative, garrison-style management to highly lethal, output-driven execution. Figure
1 depicts the modernization of the operations process, comparing traditional methods with those

The shift from scripted to friction-injected rehearsals represents a significant evolution in
military training philosophy. Although scripted rehearsals can be useful for initial
synchronization, they fail to adequately prepare staffs for the realities of the modern battlefield.
The FY26 best practice of injecting friction validates not just the plan, but the very processes and
products the staff will rely on in combat. This methodology builds mental resilience, enabling the
staff to synchronize and maintain initiative despite plan disruption. The ability to adapt and
overcome friction is the true measure of a well-rehearsed and combat-ready unit.

By ruthlessly enforcing a lean battle rhythm and demanding friction-injected rehearsals,
commanders ensure their staffs generate true shared understanding and maintain operational
momentum regardless of enemy actions.

THE HUMAN ELEMENT IN AN UNMANNED ERA

The proliferation of commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) and purpose-built UAS has undeniably
transformed tactical reconnaissance. However, FY26 JMRC rotations exposed a dangerous over-
reliance on these platforms. When BCTs treat video feeds as their sole source of situational
awareness, they become highly vulnerable to the friction inherent in LSCO.

In a highly contested environment, the technological edge is frequently blunted. Enemy EW
disrupts data links and Global Positioning System (GPS) navigation; integrated air defense
systems (IADS) and counter-UAS networks cause rapid attrition of aerial platforms; and simple
environmental factors—such as severe weather or the logistical burden of battery management—
can ground an entire drone fleet. When the screens go black, the lack of redundant, human-
driven intelligence networks blinds a BCT.

Although technical systems enhance situational awareness, they do not replace the requirement
for human-led reconnaissance, which provides the persistence and judgment necessary to operate
in a contested environment. 9 The solution observed among top-performing brigades is the
deliberate forward positioning of the human sensor. Rather than tethering joint fire support
specialists (forward observers) strictly to maneuver companies in the close fight, commanders
are dynamically task-organizing these highly trained personnel deep into the battlespace. By
infiltrating scouts and forward observers (FOs) forward, brigades establish a resilient intelligence
network that enemies cannot jam or hack. Table 5 depicts sensor type comparison through

The comparison between autonomous and human sensors makes it clear that one is not
inherently superior to the other; they are complementary assets that must be employed in concert.
The technical capabilities of UAS provide breadth and speed, while the cognitive depth and
resilience of human observers provide certainty and reliability. A successful intelligence and
targeting effort leverage the strengths of each to mitigate the vulnerabilities of the other. The
commander who understands how to orchestrate this combined-arms approach to intelligence
collection dominates the information environment and, ultimately, the battlefield.

This dynamic creates true manned-unmanned teaming (MUM-T). The most lethal formations at
JMRC used autonomous platforms to probe enemy defenses and draw out electronic signatures,
while concealed human observers confirmed the targets and called in the devastating precision
fires. Technology provides the reach, but the human provides the resilience and the judgment.

CONCLUSION

FY26 rotations at the JMRC dictate a clear mandate for the future of combined arms maneuver.
Although the character of war rapidly accelerates, combat still fundamentally relies on
navigating friction, uncertainty, and human endurance. The BCTs cannot simply purchase
tactical superiority through commercial drones. In a hyper-lethal environment saturated by
advanced EW and complex air defenses, autonomous technology provides a critical advantage,
but it does not offer a permanent solution to battlefield friction.

To secure victory in the battle of sensors and shooters, organizations must aggressively reclaim
and dominate their deep operational areas. This effort demands disciplined staffs that weaponize
the operations process using minimalist battle rhythms and friction-injected rehearsals.
Furthermore, leaders must drive a decisive cultural shift across the formation. Commanders must
value the cognitive edge, adaptability, and resilience of the forward-deployed joint fire support
specialist equally with the high-resolution data feeds provided by autonomous platforms. Human
sensors validate technical sensors, ensuring continuity when networks inevitably fail.

Technology continuously evolves, yet it remains merely a tool in the commander’s arsenal. The
discipline of the staff, the aggression of the commanders, and the courage of the Soldiers
operating at the battlespace’s extreme edge forge the true lethality of the BCT. To win the first
battle of the next war, BCTs must seamlessly integrate their autonomous systems, empower their
human experts, and decisively dominate the deep fight today.
