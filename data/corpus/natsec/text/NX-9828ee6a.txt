INTRODUCTION

The proliferation of small, unmanned aircraft systems (sUAS), persistent surveillance, and
networked fires has fundamentally altered survivability on the modern battlefield. Traditional
methods of protection—relying on distance, camouflage, or physical armor—are no longer
sufficient. Modern reconnaissance systems continuously search for the tell-tale signatures of
military operations: thermal emissions, electromagnetic activity, sustainment traffic, and vehicle
concentrations. As a result, survivability is no longer primarily a problem of protection after
enemy sensors detect a unit; it is the challenge of preventing efficient detection in the first place.

This new reality demands that commanders view survivability through the lens of enemy
reconnaissance economics. Every observable signature offers a data point to the enemy, while
every effort to reduce, conceal, or fragment those signatures imposes a cost on their
reconnaissance-strike system. This approach aligns directly with the protection warfighting
function and the core tenets of multi-domain operations (MDO) 1,2, transitioning passive security
into an active effort to deny enemy synchronization. The objective is to make the “cost” of
finding and identifying U.S. forces so prohibitively expensive in time, battery life, and operator
attention that the enemy’s intelligence apparatus goes bankrupt before it can complete its
targeting cycle. In the drone-observed era, units achieve survivability by managing signatures to
impose crippling costs on the enemy, forcing them into inefficient and low-payoff targeting
decisions.

SIGNATURE MANAGEMENT AS SECURITY

On the modern battlefield, a unit is a collection of signatures, not a single geographic location.
Although terrain or camouflage might visually hide a command post, that post still generates
detectable thermal, acoustic, and electromagnetic patterns that reveal its function. Enemy forces
identify these operational patterns—not necessarily maps or tents—to find their targets.
“Convenience compression,” in which units cluster together for ease of coordination, worsens
this problem by creating a dense, high-payoff target for enemy sensors.

Therefore, commanders must redefine security as a signature management problem building
upon the principles of survivability operations. Traditional security measures, like expanded
perimeters, can unintentionally increase vulnerability by generating more movement and thermal
signatures. The primary goal is to avoid detection. Concealment is less about total invisibility
and more about creating uncertainty. By using camouflage, dispersion, and disciplined control of
electronic emissions and vehicle movement, a unit can prevent an enemy from confidently
classifying it as a target. The longer an enemy remains uncertain, the longer a unit remains
survivable. Table 1 shows how specific survivability tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs)

TACTICAL DISPERSION FOR COST IMPOSITION AND THE C2 FRICTION

Tactical dispersion has evolved from a defensive spacing technique to a critical offensive method
that degrades enemy reconnaissance and imposes severe targeting costs on the adversary.
Friendly units often fail at dispersion by organizing for internal convenience rather than
minimizing their signature against enemy sensors. The critical metric is no longer doctrinal
spacing, but sensor exposure: if the enemy detects one vehicle, how many other friendly assets
does that placement reveal in the same field of view?

By denying the enemy high-density targets, dispersion forces adversary commanders to waste
munitions and time on isolated nodes. However, this creates significant command and control
(C2) friction for friendly forces. Physically scattering nodes and enforcing strict emission control
(EMCON) severely degrades internal communications. To operate effectively while digitally
dark, friendly commanders must accept this friction. 4 They must replace constant radio
synchronization with true mission command, relying entirely on disciplined initiative, clear
commander’s intent, and decentralized primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency (PACE)
plans.

TERRAIN EXPLOITATION AND OBSERVATION GEOMETRY

Persistent enemy aerial surveillance has inverted the friendly rules for terrain selection. The
terrain that appears tactically dominant from the ground is often the most vulnerable from the air.
Leaders must update their application of intelligence preparation of the operational environment
(IPOE); 5 evaluating cover and concealment (the “C” in obstacles; avenues of approach; key
terrain; observation and fields of fire; and cover and concealment [OAKOC]) must now heavily
prioritize overhead canopy geometry and lateral drone observation angles over traditional
ground-level masking.

In restrictive terrain, survivability depends on exploiting terrain masking and canopy density.
Many elevated features have sparse canopy along the crest but denser concealment on the
military crest. Positioning directly on the highest terrain exposes units to lateral drone
observation through canopy gaps. As the 2-37 Armor’s main command post (MCP) demonstrated
during Combined Resolve 26-07, remaining undetected is highly achievable. By selecting
positions that maximized canopy masking and maintaining absolute signature discipline, the
MCP operated undetected for more than 96 hours near the forward line of troops (FLOT),
completely exhausting opposition force (OPFOR) drone assets.

survive, aligning with survivability tenets in sustainment operations. 6 Furthermore, the most
advanced application of signature management is tactical deception. 7 By projecting false
signatures—deploying decoy vehicles or dummy radio signals—a unit actively disrupts the

Employing tactical deception by way of signature management in practice can produce a very
low opportunity cost against a very high survivability payoff. The threat reconnaissance efforts
must commit to confirm either a false positive or false negative (ambiguity enhancing versus
reducing). 9 Therefore creating a “reconnaissance dilemma,” forcing the adversary into a lose-
lose scenario: either they over-invest resources to verify every signature, slowing their
operational tempo, or they hesitate to strike real targets for fear of wasting munitions on decoys.

CONCLUSION

Ultimately, economics govern survivability in the drone era. Success belongs to the commander
who forces the enemy to exhaust finite assets—operator attention, battery life, and precision
munitions—against fragmented signatures and low-payoff targets. By dispersing to dilute target
density, using canopy and terrain to block aerial sensors, and executing deception across all
warfighting functions, friendly forces can exhaust the enemy’s targeting cycle. The modern
battlefield no longer rewards units that merely absorb detection and weather strikes; it rewards
those that make detection itself too costly, uncertain, and operationally inefficient for the enemy
to sustain.
