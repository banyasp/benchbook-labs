EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This paper examines Ukraine’s low-cost acoustic monitoring networks (commonly reported as
“Sky Fortress,” Zvook, FENEK, and related projects) that detect and cue defenses against low-
altitude, unmanned aerial systems (UAS) and first-person view (FPV) attack drones. It traces the
technological lineage to World War I and interwar acoustic mirrors used by the United Kingdom;
documents how acoustic nodes are used operationally in Ukraine to cue mobile fire groups and
air-defense networks; evaluates limitations; and provides concrete recommendations for U.S.
adoption on the North Atlantic Treaty Organization’s (NATO’s) Eastern Flank Deterrence Line
(EFDL) and in Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM), including Guam, South Korea, and
Australia. This paper argues that low-cost, distributed acoustic sensors—integrated via open
messaging into theater command and control (C2)—can provide an affordable layer of close-in
early warning, conserve high-value interceptors, and increase resilience in distributed basing. It
recommends U.S. leadership drive the development of industrial-scale, civilian-manufacturing
partnerships to produce hardened acoustic node kits, open standards for reporting, and rapid pilot
programs in both theaters.

OBSERVATION

Small, unmanned aerial systems (sUAS), especially FPV and loitering munitions, pose a unique
threat set: low-radar cross section (RCS), low-altitude flight profiles, and slow speeds that
complicate legacy radar detection. Since 2022, Ukraine has demonstrated a scalable approach:
thousands of passive acoustic sensors detect engine and propeller signatures, locally classify
events with edge machine learning (ML), and share compact detection messages to triangulate
tracks and cue local shooters and point defenses. The renewed interest in acoustic sensing revives
older techniques—listening to engines—with modern computers, networking, and ML.

Acoustic detection of aircraft predates radar. During World War I and the interwar years, British
experiments yielded a range of devices (acoustic visors, horn collectors, and large concrete
‘sound mirrors’) used as coastal early-warning installations. Notable British examples include
the concrete mirrors at Denge (Dungeness) and Hythe, and smaller mirrors at Kilnsea, Redcar,
and Boulby. These devices focused engine noise into a microphone and allowed operators to
estimate bearing; where multiple stations could triangulate an approach and cue defenses.
Though radar’s arrival in the late 1930s rendered such mirrors obsolete, the fundamental
practice—distributed listening, operator classification, and triangulation—remains relevant to
contemporary acoustic networks. 3

Ukraine Today: Systems, Names, and How They Operate

Since full-scale war began in 2022, multiple Ukrainian projects have built passive acoustic
sensor networks. Leading names in open-source reporting and project sites include Sky Fortress
(a large national concept reported to number in the thousands to tens of thousands of nodes),
Zvook (a project with an active website describing parabolic collectors, edge ML, and networked
cues), and FENEK (a manufacturer and sensor suite now in testing). These systems share a
common architecture: low-cost directional microphones or parabolic collectors, local computers
for classification, time-synchronized timestamps (often via Network Time Protocol
[NTP]/Global Positioning System [GPS]), and compact detection messages (bearing, confidence,
node ID, timestamp). Multiple coincident detections are correlated to triangulate location and
produce an alert packet that is either acted upon locally (mobile fire groups and shooters) or
forwarded to higher echelons for fusion with radar, electro-optical/infrared (EO/IR), and other
sensors. 5, 6, 7

Operationally, reports indicate these acoustic networks are used primarily to do the following:

    •   Cue nearest local shooters and small-unit air-defense teams to engage low-cost FPV and
        loitering munitions.
    •   Provide early warning to civil authorities.
    •   Feed higher-level C2 for situational awareness.

Ukrainian implementations emphasize cheap, replaceable nodes, passive sensing (difficult to
detect and hard to jam in the radio frequency [RF] domain), and human-in-the-loop confirmation
for high-consequence actions. 8

Technical Typology: Sensor Types, Signal Processing, and Networking

Sensor hardware ranges from simple parabolic dishes fitted with directional microphones to
compact Micro-Electro-Mechanical-System (MEMS)-microphone arrays. Parabolic collectors
provide high passive gain and simple mechanical directionality; microphone arrays permit digital
beamforming and more precise direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation. Edge processing on
single-board computers runs ML classifiers to filter environmental noise (birds, vehicles, wind)
and recognize signatures of specific UAS types or engine classes. Nodes typically send compact
detection records rather than raw audio, conserving bandwidth and preserving operational
security. When several nodes observe the same event, the system correlates DOA bearings and
timestamps to triangulate a track and estimate heading and speed. Triangulated tracks are then
queued for human confirmation or automatic cueing of local defenders. 9

Ukraine has developed a remarkably effective drone defense system called “Sky Fortress,” using
a network of approximately 9,500 acoustic sensors strategically placed across the country.
Originally conceived by two engineers who prototyped the system with a microphone and cell
phone, these sensors detect and pinpoint the location of incoming one-way unmanned aerial
vehicles (UAVs). This data is then relayed to mobile fire teams via iPads, providing flight path
information and enabling minimally trained operators to engage and destroy the drones with anti-
aircraft weaponry.

The system’s effectiveness is significant; recently, Ukraine successfully intercepted 80 of 84
UAVs launched in a single Russian attack. The low cost of each sensor—roughly 400 to 500
dollars—makes the entire network substantially cheaper than a single Patriot missile system.
Following a demonstration at Ramstein Air Base, other nations, including Romania, are now
evaluating the potential of this innovative acoustic sensor technology for their own defense
needs. 11

Ukraine is bolstering its defenses against Russian air attacks with innovative technologies, most
notably the “Zvook” AI project—Ukrainian for “sound.” This system uses ML to identify and
track enemy cruise missiles, helicopters, drones, and fighter jets operating at low to medium
altitudes, filling critical gaps in existing surveillance capabilities. Developed by a team of
Ukrainian IT and telecom experts, Zvook detected its first cruise missile just four hours after
initial installation and has since been deployed across 40 locations nationwide, increasing missile
interception accuracy from 50 percent to 100 percent.

Zvook operates on the principle of acoustic detection, distinguishing the sounds of enemy
aircraft from other ambient noises through advanced neural networks. This approach offers
significant advantages over traditional radar systems, which are vulnerable to targeted attacks.
Zvook stations are passive (they do not emit signals), making them virtually undetectable to
Russian forces and economically impractical to destroy. Experts estimate that approximately 600
stations are needed to fully protect Ukraine, strategically positioned along borders and
throughout the country to provide a comprehensive acoustic “frontier,” complementing existing
air defense systems. 13

FENEK

FENEK is an acoustic detection system using a network of microphones and advanced sound-
filtering algorithms to identify and locate drones. The system triangulates drone positions by
analyzing acoustic signatures, accurately determining distance and altitude. Even a single
microphone setup can determine a target’s azimuth with an accuracy of up to 2 degrees, a notable
achievement for sound-based detection.

For comprehensive facility protection, FENEK requires the installation of three or more units to
pinpoint precise target coordinates and supports dynamic tracking of moving sound sources and
impulse-based capture of single acoustic events like explosions. Current development efforts
focus on aligning the system with NATO standards to streamline data exchange and integration
with other defense solutions. 14

Operational Employment in Ukraine: Cueing and C2 Integration

Ukraine’s networks deliver timely, low-altitude detections used to cue ‘mobile fire groups’—ad
hoc shooters, autocannon teams, or short-range point defenses—rather than to consume
expensive surface-to-air missile (SAM) interceptors on cheap kamikaze UAS. Reports and field
accounts indicate that acoustic cues are commonly delivered via secure messaging apps or
bespoke dashboards to local commanders and to regional air-defense C2, where they are fused
with radar and EO/IR. Because acoustic nodes are inexpensive and passive, they are often
deployed in large numbers; loss or capture of nodes is operationally acceptable given
redundancy. 15

Limitations, Environmental Factors, and Countermeasures

Acoustic detection is limited by ambient noise (urban, industrial, sea surf), atmospheric
absorption at higher frequencies, and wind and temperature gradients that refract or attenuate
sound. Quiet propulsion (electric motors with low acoustic signatures) or masking tactics reduce
detection range. Acoustic sensors also have limited stand-off compared with long-range radar;
typical effective ranges for small quadcopters or FPV systems are on the order of hundreds to a
few thousand meters, depending on sensor quality and noise conditions. As such, acoustic
networks are most effective as dense, local early-warning layers and should be fused with other
sensors for a full air picture. 16

Adapting and Employing Acoustic Networks for the U.S.: Eastern Flank Deterrence Line

On NATO’s Eastern Flank (commonly referenced here as the Eastern Flank Deterrence Line
[EFDL]), acoustic networks offer several advantages: low-cost sensor kits permit dense coverage
of critical nodes (airbases, logistics hubs, bridges, power infrastructure) where low-altitude UAS
present asymmetric risks; passive sensors are less likely to be targeted by electronic warfare
(EW); and local edge processing minimizes bandwidth needs. Implementation considerations
include integration with NATO message standards (e.g., Link 16 translators, or other Data Link
Translator and Communications Control System for tactical use), provisioning of resilient
comms (tactical radios, satellite communication [SATCOM] fallback), and pre-deployment of
hardened solar-powered kits for allied forces. Pilot deployments with host-nation partners allow
calibration of ML models to local acoustics (e.g., engine noise signatures common to the
region). 17

INDOPACOM Applications: Guam, South Korea, and Australia

INDOPACOM poses different but complementary opportunities for acoustic networks. Guam
and other island bases are vulnerable to low-flying cruise missiles and small UAS operating over
water where radar returns can be degraded by sea-clutter and low-altitude radar-horizon limits.
Dense, solar-powered acoustic nodes positioned on shores and elevated terrain can provide close-
in detection to cue local point defenses and shipboard systems. Likewise, South Korea—facing
persistent low-altitude intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) and potential kinetic
drone threats—can use acoustic arrays to protect airfields and logistic nodes, especially in
complex terrain where radar coverage is interrupted. Australia’s vast approaches and dispersed
facilities benefit from low-cost, distributed nodes to monitor littoral approaches and remote
infrastructure. Maritime adaptations (vibration-isolated mounts, wind-noise suppression, and
array processing for sea-noise environments) are necessary for effective coastal and ship-board
installations. 18

Integration with U.S. Force Posture and C2

For both theaters, the U.S. should treat acoustic nodes as a tactical sensor layer that feeds
existing C2 and air battle management. Key technical elements are standardized detection
message formats with cryptographic authentication; time synchronization (GPS/NTP); and
secure, low-latency translation services into theater battle management systems. Acoustic alerts
should be labeled with confidence scores and provenance metadata to assist human operators in
weighted decision making. Where appropriate, acoustic detections can directly cue local,
authorized ‘shoot-on-sight’ defenses under predefined rules of engagement (ROE), while
detections elevated to higher echelons require additional sensor corroboration. 19

Industrial Approach: U.S.-Led Civilian Manufacturing Partnerships

A central recommendation is to pursue U.S.-led, civilian manufacturing partnerships to
industrialize and scale acoustic node production. Reasons include:

     •   Speed and cost—civilian manufacturers (audio electronics, telecoms, solar power firms)
         can rapidly scale production lines.
     •   Industrial base resilience—diverse suppliers reduce single-point dependency.
     •   Exportability—civilian-led production facilitates allied procurement and burden-sharing.

The proposed model: U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) funds initial design and certification
(ruggedization, electromagnetic interference [EMI] hardening, security modules), then issues
long-term production contracts to civilian firms under defense production authorities. Open
hardware and software specifications (for detection message formats, ML model interfaces, and
security modules) should be published to encourage an ecosystem of suppliers and third-party
auditors. Public–private partnerships could pair firms in the U.S. (audio hardware manufacturers,
embedded computer firms) with allied assemblers (e.g., in NATO countries, Australia) to
produce locally tailored kits for EFDL and INDOPACOM. 20

Risk Assessment and Countermeasures

Risks include: adversary changes in acoustics (electric propulsion), environmental degradation,
and deliberate deception.

Mitigations include: continuous ML retraining, multi-sensor fusion, and redundancy in node
placement. Operationally, avoid over-reliance on a single sensor class; instead, formalize
acoustic sensing as a high-value cueing tool within a layered detection architecture.

Prioritized recommendations include:

     •   Initiate dual-theater pilots: one on NATO’s Eastern Flank (EFDL) co-located with a
         brigade or key airbase, and one on Guam or a comparable INDOPACOM island facility.
     •   Publish open detection message standards and require cryptographic signing for node
         reports.
     •   Establish U.S.-led civilian manufacturing contracts with modular design requirements
         and allied co-production options.
     •   Fund research and development for maritime acoustic adaptations and sensor-fusion
         algorithms.
     •   Develop doctrine and training packages for local commanders and integrate acoustic cues
         into theater C2 with human-in-the-loop safeguards.

Implications for U.S. Army DOTMLPF-P

The adaptation of any counter-unmanned aerial system (C-UAS) (acoustic), such as those listed
in this paper will include all framework of doctrine, organization, training, materiel, leadership
and education, personnel, facilities, and policy (DOTMLPF-P). This is because of a capability
gap observed within the U.S. Army and the DoD regarding C-UAS and monitoring for UAS.

The integration of acoustic monitoring systems into U.S. Army capabilities can be effectively
framed using the DOTMLPF-P lens. Each element provides a pathway for institutionalizing
lessons from Ukraine while ensuring applicability to the European theater (EFDL) and
INDOPACOM.

Doctrine: Acoustic monitoring should be codified as a passive detection layer in Army air and
missile defense doctrine. It complements radar and EO/IR systems, offering an all-weather, low-
cost, and survivable method for early warning and cueing.

Organization: Specialized acoustic monitoring detachments should be attached to air defense
artillery brigades, multi-domain task forces, and regional commands in EUCOM and
INDOPACOM. These detachments can be structured similarly to signals intelligence or C-UAS
teams.

Training: Operators must be trained to deploy, calibrate, and interpret acoustic data. Training
scenarios at the Joint Multinational Readiness Center (JMRC) and Joint Pacific Multinational
Readiness Center (JPMRC) should integrate acoustic monitoring alongside radar and EW tools.

Materiel: U.S.-led procurement of modular, scalable acoustic sensors (leveraging civilian
manufacturing partnerships) will enable fixed and mobile deployment. These systems should be
compatible with existing Army C2 networks and able to feed data directly into Integrated Air and
Missile Defense Battle Command System (IBCS).

Leadership: Leaders at all levels must understand the operational value of acoustic monitoring
as part of layered defense. Professional military education (PME) courses should emphasize
integrating acoustic systems into mission command.

Personnel: The Army may designate an additional skill identifier (ASI) for acoustic monitoring
operators within the Air Defense Artillery and EW career fields. Cross-training with intelligence
analysts will improve fusion of acoustic data into the broader operational picture.

Facilities: Acoustic systems should be permanently emplaced at key facilities along the EFDL,
on Guam, and near allied bases in South Korea and Australia. Integration into regional command
centers will ensure real-time warning and coordination with allies.

Policy: At the policy level, the DoD should establish acquisition pathways for acoustic systems,
enable foreign military sales (FMS) for NATO and Indo-Pacific allies, and create multinational
data-sharing frameworks.

This policy foundation will ensure interoperability and shared situational awareness across the
alliance.

Recommendation

Ukraine’s acoustic monitoring efforts demonstrate that low-cost, distributed listening networks
can meaningfully contribute to C-UAS operations by providing dense, passive, and affordable
close-in detection. For the U.S., formalizing an EFDL/INDOPACOM acoustic program—backed
by U.S.-led civilian manufacturing, interoperable standards, and rapid pilots—offers a pragmatic
hedge against proliferating UAS threats. Acoustic nodes will not replace radar or EW, but they
can conserve scarce interceptors, increase the density of early warning, and harden distributed
bases against low-altitude threats.

In summary, adopting acoustic monitoring within DOTMLPF-P ensures the U.S. Army does not
treat this as an ad hoc solution but as a systematically integrated capability that enhances layered
air defense against UAS and FPV drones.
