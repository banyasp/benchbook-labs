APPENDIX A: Cryptologists of World War I
From the Ground Up: American Cryptology during World War I

By Betsy Rohaly Smoot                                               2023



       The names of the individual soldiers at the bottom of the G2 structure who
       furnished the proof that the German divisions were still in their trenches are lost
       to history. Probably none of them understood, or ever will know, what part he
       played in the victory.
                                                    - William E. Moore, “The Crisis of St. Mihiel”1


       Time will not dim the glory of their deeds.
       - General John J. Pershing


The human factor—the elusive “right stuff” in abilities, background, and personality—played a

role in the selection of personnel for American cryptologic work during World War I. These

people have been mostly anonymous to us, and we know very little about many of them except

for some basic details of their birth and death. Few of the hundreds wrote of their experience

and few were remembered in print by others. Approximately 650 men would serve with the

American Expeditionary Forces (AEF) in cryptologic positions; about 450 men (and women)

would serve in such positions in the United States. These workers came from across the United

States—academics, lawyers, businessmen, clerks, engineers, telegraphers, and others. They

were old and young, coming from well-established careers and just starting out in life. Some of

the men recruited for the technical work of signal collection were already serving in the army or

were in the Signal Corps reserve, but far more were working in the commercial telephone and

telegraph industry.


                                                1
Radio was the “cyber” of 1917. The technology was new, and amateur radio buffs were the

smart young (and not so young) geeks of that time. Skills in radio and all other modes of

telecommunications—the older telegraphy and the middle-aged telephony—were needed, as

were engineers and mechanically inclined men of all sorts (and sometimes women). So great

was the need for skilled radio operators that in some cases the fact that a man was slightly

under the required weight was overlooked. The net was cast far and wide. Based on very rough

calculations from 1910 census data, fewer than 4 percent of the United States’ 30 million

working men were in occupations that were the logical pool for cryptologic talent—engineers,

telegraphers, telephone workers, lawyers, teachers, stenographers, and typists. These numbers

were likely slightly higher by 1917. Interestingly, in 1910 the word “radio” does not appear in

any occupational category.2 These skills would be needed throughout the US military—the US

Army Signal Corps and the US Navy took whole groups of “Marconi men” and Bell Telephone

workers to fill signal battalions and companies. Those working intelligence collection would find

themselves in fully equipped but very primitive facilities that could be moved from site to site.



Cryptology was almost entirely a white male world during the war, as was the pool of

individuals in the academic and technical fields from which these organizations found their

recruits. The need for language skills, particularly for those who could understand and write

German, meant that some skill fields contained many children and grandchildren of European

immigrants who learned the language at home, in addition to those who had studied European

languages academically. While 20 percent of the soldiers in the AEF were born overseas, the


                                                2
number of foreign-born cryptologists in the AEF seems to have been substantially lower,

probably because of security concerns. Sixty of the 947 individuals (6.3 percent) listed in this

appendix are known to have been born overseas; a few of those were born to American citizens

living abroad. In the Radio Section, where German speakers were actively recruited, at least 38

of the 483 men (7.8 percent) are known to have been born outside the United States, less than

10 percent of the total.



A small group of Native Americans played a part in increasing communications security toward

the end of the war, presaging the use of Native American codetalking in the Second World War.

These men were not trained in cryptology and were not part of the broader national system.

See chapter 16 for an in-depth discussion of their work. My research has uncovered the

previously unknown fact that there were three individuals of Native American descent (3.8

percent of the 79 assigned personnel) working in the AEF G2A6 breaking codes and ciphers.

Gilbert W. Daney, a registered member of the Choctaw Tribe, whose draft card indicated his

race as white, served as an army field clerk from March to December 1918. Corporal Joseph

Thunder, who served in the G2A6 from July through December 1918, was probably one of the

three Oglala Sioux of that name who served in the AEF; however, there is not enough

information to positively identify him. And Nazard M. Coursolle, who served in the office in May

and June 1918 before transferring to the AEF Adjutant General’s office, was part Mdawakanton

(Dakota) Sioux.3




                                                 3
An equally small group of women was engaged in cryptologic work on the home front. While

the British allowed women to enlist and play a cryptologic role in France, the United States did

not. These women, however, had a disproportionately successful role in some areas at home.

See chapter 17 for details about their work. At least 12 women worked as cryptanalysts in MI-8,

and 5 women at Riverbank did analysis on material provided by the government.



No African Americans have, to date, been identified in any official government cryptologic

pursuit during World War I. The only Black signal unit during the war was the 325th Field Signal

Battalion of the 92nd Division, which arrived in France in June 1918. This unit was not tasked

with radio intercept but, like other signal units at the front, may have inadvertently intercepted

German communications. Any intercept by this unit would have gone to the division’s

intelligence officer; if such intercept exists it has not been found and did not make its way to

the AEF G2A6 in Chaumont.4 Similarly, no Asian Americans have been identified in the

workforce of 1917-1918. World War I draft cards provide broad racial identification and

birthplace but do not allow easy categorization of other demographic data. It was beyond the

scope of this book to examine all extant military personnel records for all individuals listed in

this appendix.



Conclusion

After the war, there was little need to collect radio communications; codebreaking flourished

against diplomatic cable traffic that was intercepted by the major telegraph companies with no

technical collection effort on the part of the cryptologists. Some of the men and women who


                                                 4
worked in MI-8 or in the AEF went to work for Herbert O. Yardley’s Black Chamber. The tools

and techniques needed to intercept radio signals were not entirely forgotten, but the skill set

needed to exploit these communications was generally not sought out until the mid-1930s.

Only four veterans of AEF cryptology would go on to serve in the army cryptologic service of

WWII, for even the very youngest cryptologists of WWI would have been well into a second

civilian career by 1941. The few that were still involved in cryptology in the Second World War

had maintained some sort of connection with the work in the interwar years. Most of the World

War I cryptologists went back to their lives and built careers—some in telecommunications, but

others were writers, artists, lawyers, businessmen, and farmers. A few stayed in the military or

the reserves, and some were recalled to duty in World War II in noncryptologic work.



Those who worked in cryptology during World War II also came from a wide range of

occupations, including mathematicians with advanced degrees, missionaries with language

skills, professors and other scholars. Unlike World War I, there were many, many, more women

working in cryptology on the home front and overseas. Overseas, the Signal Corps continued to

rely on engineers and those who had worked for telephone and telegraph companies. 5 After

World War II, there would be an increased emphasis on hiring those with academic and

scientific credentials.



There is debate as to whether cryptologists are born with the innate ability to do the work, or

whether people can be trained to accomplish the task. What is clear is that many of the World

War I American cryptologists shared some characteristics. Many were more educated than the


                                                5
average American soldier. Quite a few of those selected for cryptologic work had capability in

one or more languages other than English. Those involved with signal collection, if not

completely familiar with all the technical details of the communications technologies of the

day, understood the basics. Most of these men had a degree of maturity that led to their being

trusted to operate somewhat independently. They were the cyber warriors of their day, and

they had the right stuff to get the job done.



This Appendix provides a list of all the individuals who have been identified (as of December

2021) as working in American cryptology during the war. I developed this list from a variety of

sources. Rosters of most (but not all) of the cryptologic organizations can be found in archival

sources; at their best (e.g., a postwar list for the AEF Signal Corps Radio Section) they contain

name, rank, date of arrival in France, and hometown. The historical records of several

organizations (e.g., MI-8 and the US Navy Code and Signal Section) contain no lists at all. I used

the extant rosters and other archival materials found in the National Archives to develop a base

list of names, and then I conducted hundreds of hours of research into these individuals using

publicly available genealogical and newspaper databases. Some individuals were easy to

identify, but others’ records were elusive. Every effort has been made to supply accurate

information; birth and death information has not been listed unless I was able to make a

connection between this information and evidence of cryptologic service. Where it was not

possible to precisely identify an individual (most commonly because there were many of the

same name, and organizational records do not provide enough information), only the name and

rank (and hometown if given in an official roster) have been provided; these are indicated by an


                                                 6
asterisk. The rank information should be treated with care, as many men remained in the

reserves or active service and were promoted to higher rank after the war, or they returned to

service in World War II. While this has been noted within the sections for each organization, I

must emphasize that this list is incomplete. Any errors in identification are my own. I hope to

be able to share the wealth of fascinating stories and career-related information I have

gathered on many of these individuals in another venue in the future.

primary organization).

For the purpose of this book, everyone who worked in a cryptologic organization or trained in

cryptology is counted as a cryptologist. The lists in this appendix are incomplete for many

organizations due to lack of information in the organization’s records; 80.8 percent (947) of the

estimated 1,171 cryptologists are named here.



Of the 947 names, 850 can be positively identified with biographical detail, or nearly 90

percent. This means we have at least some information on 72.75 percent of all World War I

cryptologists. The search continues.



The tables use the following conventions:

      Full names are provided when known, though the text of the book may only give a

       middle initial. The names given reflect the name the individual is listed under in official

       military records. Where this name differs from the birth name, the other name(s) are

       given in parentheses.

                                                 8
      The FINAL RANK column reflects the last known rank of the individual during his or her

       World War I service. For those who stayed in the service or rejoined a service during

       World War II, that information appears in the COMMENT field. In most cases, these

       tables are service specific. The exception is the MI-8 Full-Time staff table, where the

       service is noted when known. All ranks are spelled out where known.

      If an individual is known to hold a PhD, the title Dr. appears with his or her name. Social

       titles (Mrs., Miss, Mr.) are only provided where they appear in official records.

      Place name spellings have been corrected from those that appear in official records; if

       the location is now in a different country than when the individual was born, that is

       noted. If a foreign place name could not be resolved, I have defaulted to the country

       alone.

      Individuals who have only a name and rank (and very occasionally a birthplace without a

       birthdate) are considered to be incomplete records and are marked with an asterisk.

Riverbank Trainees

William and Elizabeth Friedman taught two cipher classes at Riverbank Laboratories for the US
Army. The first, consisting of four students selected by the Military Intelligence Division in
Washington, took place in late November 1917; these students were immediately sent to the
American Expeditionary Forces’ G2A6 and became the heart of that organization. The second,
larger, class began in February 1918 and continued through March, though not all students were
present for the entire period. These students were supplied by individual army divisions; only a few
of these men exhibited aptitude for the work and thee majority did not work in cryptology during
the war.

MI-10E

MI-10E, the Military Intelligence Division’s Radio Intelligence Section, was established to collect
and analyze radio signals from sites in the United States both along the US-Mexico border and in
Maine. It had an authorized strength of 140, and 100 men were on duty at the time of the Armistice.
This list is incomplete. The organization continued to operate and recruit new personnel until the
early 1920s. While every effort has been made to limit the list to those on duty before the November
1918 Armistice, it is possible that some of these men arrived after that time.

Additional Signal Corps Units
The AEF Signal Corps Radio Section did not achieve full personnel strength until late 1918.
Other AEF Signal Corps units provided personnel to the Radio Section to conduct necessary
signal intercept tasks in late 1917 and early 1918. The units listed below are those known and
documented to have provided personnel to augment early Radio Section efforts.
406th Telegraph Battalion
The 406th Telegraph Battalion was one of the twelve battalions organized in the Spring of 1917 by
Bell Telephone. Company D comprised employees of the Philadelphia and Harrisburg divisions of
the Bell Company and Company E was made up of employees from the Pittsburgh division. The
battalion was part of the AEF Signal Corps and after August 1918 subordinate to the First Army’s I
Corps.

APPENDIX A7: Native American Codetalkers

The employment of Native Americans to serve as “codetalkers” was an unplanned, impromptu
effort to provide better communication security for combat troops using telephones. Recent
scholarship has expanded our knowledge of this effort but it seems likely that we do not know
all the names of those who participated in codetalking during World War I.
