 Every issue of Trend Line is built on a simple, powerful foundation: you have a problem and
we have the solution. This quarterly publication bypasses high-level theory to deliver concise
actionable recommendations directly to tactical commanders and the Soldier at the tactical edge.

 This issue delivers immediate usable lessons on critical modern battlefield challenges ranging
from battlefield power management to decentralized airspace management. Additionally, we
have included links to podcasts and supplemental products to further assist you as you train to
fight and win.

 The first article in this issue, The Evolution from CALL to the TLLM Framework, reminds us that
modern warfare requires us to be able to adapt faster than our adversaries. The key to adapting
faster is sharing the knowledge of how to succeed and solving the issues that prevent success.
Trend Line is one effort to achieve this purpose by sharing best practices on a variety of subjects
with you so you can continuously improve. On our end, TLLM-Army will proactively seek out
those best practices and push them to you to adapt and use for your formations.
This is only the beginning. Expect the next issue in November 2026, with subsequent releases
arriving every three months.
  I encourage you to share this publication across your formations. The insights contained
within these pages are meant to be put to work immediately. TLLM-Army is proud to stand
alongside you as we drive continuous improvement and secure the success of our Soldiers on
the battlefields of today and tomorrow.

                    The Evolution from
                   CALL to the TLLM-Army                                  Steven Vass, IV
  The Army is transforming its lessons learned program to give units a more direct voice in shaping
doctrine and equipment. The new Transformation and Lessons Learned Manager (TLLM) system
provides a faster, more direct method for integrating operational observations. This article explains the
new TLLM framework and how units can use it to their advantage.

  To meet the demands of the Army Transformation Initiative, large-scale combat operations (LSCO),
and modernization in capturing lessons learned, the Army created TLLMs as part of the Transformation
Integration Directorate (TID). TLLMs consolidate change agents, decentralize the lessons learned
process, and leverage digital ecosystems to accelerate transformation. This new approach changes
how the Army processes and implements operational insights.1

  Embedded within centers of excellence (COEs), TLLMs serve as the “user representative” across
the doctrine, organization, training, materiel, leadership and education, personnel, facilities, and policy
(DOTMLPF-P) spectrum. The Center for Army Lessons Learned (CALL) transitioned into TLLM-Army
and evolved into a data-driven hub, leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) and the Quick-Fire ecosystem
to support these embedded managers.

    Next-Generation C2 Gap
    How a Transformation and Lessons Learned Manager is
    Operationalizing the Newest C2 System     Micheal Clowser

 The fielding of advanced, data-centric command and control (C2) platforms has accelerated, promising
to revolutionize situational awareness and decision-making on the modern battlefield. However,
assessments of next generation C2 exercises and integrated assessments across recent operational
cycles have illuminated a persistent trend: training units frequently struggle to effectively implement the
Newest C2 System into their tactical formations.

   Through observations across major training events, including recent Warfighter Exercises (WFXs)
and divisional exercises, the Transformation and Lessons Learned Manager-Command and Control
Integration Directorate (TLLM-C2ID) identified critical friction points. To address these, we have deployed
a comprehensive doctrine, organization, training, materiel, leadership and education, personnel, and
facilities (DOTMLPF-P) strategy designed to transition the force from legacy briefing processes to true,
live data-centric operations.                                       U.S. Army photo by Capt. Phillip M. Smith, MCCoE

The Unmanned Revolution on the Modern Battlefield
 The wars in Ukraine and Israel have permanently disrupted the classic paradigms of land warfare,
establishing the lowest tactical altitudes as a decisive battlespace. The proliferation of inexpensive,
commercial-off-the-shelf unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) has stripped away traditional tactical
sanctuary, creating an environment where physical, thermal, and electromagnetic signatures can be
instantly geolocated and targeted.

 To maintain overmatch, the U.S. Army is rapidly shifting from a legacy fixed-base posture to a highly
agile, maneuver-centric force optimized for the air-ground littoral, the critical airspace immediately above
the tactical formation. This transition requires a complete overhaul of how we train individual Soldiers,
develop tactical leaders, and codify collective doctrine. By aggressively institutionalizing real-time threat
data from current battlefields, the Army ensures its tactical formations are prepared to survive, fight, and
win in today’s drone-saturated environments. The following analysis details the specific actions the Army
has taken to institutionalize these critical lessons across the force.

 The Emergence of the Fifth Sustainment Commodity
 For over a century, military logistics has revolved around four essential commodities:

       • Fuel
       • Ammunition
       • Food
       • Water

  These fundamental resources have enabled armies to maneuver, fight, and sustain operations across
every major conflict of the modern era. Today; however, a fifth commodity is rapidly emerging as a decisive
factor on the modern battlefield: electrical power.

  The modern infantry formation bears little resemblance to its counterpart from two decades ago. During
operations in Iraq and Afghanistan, most Soldiers relied primarily on radios, night vision devices, and a
limited number of electronic systems. Today, virtually every warfighting function depends upon electrical
power. The proliferation of small, unmanned aircraft systems (sUAS), digital mission command platforms,
tactical smart devices, electronic warfare systems, sensors, and advanced communications equipment
has fundamentally changed the energy demands placed on tactical formations. Electrical power is no
longer a convenience, it has become a strict prerequisite for maintaining situational awareness, command
and control, reconnaissance, targeting, mobility, and survivability.

                        To Prevent Thermal Runaway,
            Units Must Strictly Enforce the Following Protocols:
    • Storage and transportation: Never transport batteries fully charged or loose in card-
   board containers. Store them at a 50 percent to 75 percent state of charge (SoC) in secured,

   with the rubber seals removed to prevent pressure buildup).

    • Terminal protection: All battery contact terminals must be taped or physically shielded
   during transport and storage to eliminate the risk of accidental arcing and external short
   circuits.

   extinguishers cannot stop active lithium-ion thermal runaway. Instead, they should only be
   used to suppress surrounding secondary fires, while the thermal runaway itself is mitigated
   by drenching the cells with copious amounts of water or sand to cool the core temperature

PROTECTING THE PROTECTORS
The Realities of Multi-domain Protection and High-value
Asset Displacement                                Brad Marvel



   For a U.S. Army division to successfully project combat power, maneuver offensively, and sustain
its forces, it must first establish and maintain a comprehensive multi-domain protection umbrella. This
umbrella is complex and resource-intensive network of interdependent systems, spanning from upper-
tier air defense systems to dedicated combat engineers to localized ground security forces.

  Adversaries enjoy a sophisticated understanding of this protection network. Threat doctrine explicitly
identifies the key nodes of the Army’s protection architecture as a primary center of gravity blocking
their own freedom of action. Consequently, dismantling this protection umbrella is, in most scenarios,
the enemy’s first operational imperative.

  This article leverages unclassified observations from Warfighter Exercise (WFX) 26-4 to explore best
practices executing multi-domain protection against a near-peer adversary. It establishes the baseline
operational environment by examining the inherent internal friction divisions face when layering
defenses and then transitions into the practical application of these concepts, detailing a well-executed
movement of a Patriot battery while under enemy surveillance and fire. It provides division staffs and the
institutional Army with actionable best practices for mitigating risk, eliminating single points of failure,
and improving survivability of high-value protection assets.

Introduction
 The division’s deep fight is more hotly contested and complex than ever before. To win this fight, a division
must possess the ability to see deep and strike with timeliness and precision. Recent Transformation and
Lessons Learned Manager – Army (TLLM - Army) observations, especially those at Warfighter exercises
(WFX), demonstrated that traditional methods of shaping the depth are slow-reacting, vulnerable to
disruption, and lack both mass and resiliency.

  During WFX 26-4, these challenges forced the 1st Cavalry Division to evolve how it manages tactical
airspace and integrates emerging technologies. Faced with severe numerical shortfalls in traditional
unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) and constrained by legacy airspace management rules, the division
had to adapt rapidly to survive. By exploring the decentralized employment of low-cost tactical UAS and
the integration of launched effects (LE), this publication highlights how a division bypassed traditional
training limitations to generate massive, expendable sensor volume. More importantly, it illustrates why
securing information advantage in the modern deep fight requires commanders to shift away from legacy
procedures to fully exploit a huge number of new technologies available to the division to shape its
battlespace.

  As Russian electronic warfare, cyber operations, and precision strikes relentlessly targeted Ukrainian
command and control (C2) infrastructure in the initial months of the 2022 invasion, Ukrainian forces
deliberately avoided reliance on any single communication method. By employing a diverse mix of
military radios, commercial cell networks, Starlink satellite links, physical couriers, and preplanned
mission orders, they established vital communications resilience. This layered approach prevented a
single point of failure, enabling decentralized command and sustained unit operations despite severe
jamming, network degradation, and kinetic attacks on command posts. This highlights why a robust
primary, alternate, contingency, emergency (PACE) communications plan is critical for modern combat
operations.

communication being ineffective, there was a need to go to another form of communication. While high
frequency (HF) and tactical satellite (TACSAT) was part of the troop’s PACE plan, no one in the troop
had possession of a TACSAT and even though the OP had brought HF radios, the Soldiers had very
little training on how to properly use them rendering them useless.

 In this episode of the Insights from the Fight podcast, Command Sergeant Major Christopher Prosser of First Army discussed
 the critical role of First Army as the premier training and validation command for the Army National Guard (Compo 2) and
 Army Reserve (Compo 3), which collectively represent 52 percent of the Total Army. CSM Prosser outlines First Army’s
 complex operational mandates, spanning the Global Force Management Allocation Plan (GFMAP), large-scale mobilization
 operations, and the vital “on-order” missions of constitution and forward-deployed reconstitution. First Army employs an
 elite cadre of Observer Controller/Trainers (OC/Ts) who are deeply grounded in doctrine and masters of unit training
 management (UTM). First Army is spearheading cutting-edge, data-driven modernization initiatives. Developing platforms
 which integrate multi-source readiness and maintenance data from systems of record to inform trainers on unit readiness
 and an AI-enabled “Novel Training Pipeline” designed to automate resource scheduling, range reservations, and logistics,
 enabling commanders to focus exclusively on execution and decision-making.
