Introduction
This article provides reconnaissance elements with a detailed methodology to survive and see on
the modern battlefield by deliberately integrating small unmanned aircraft systems (sUASs) as a
dispersed ground-air team. It moves beyond the simple employment of drones and teaches
readers how to employ a survivable four-site framework that separates launch, control, recovery,
and retransmission functions. Scout sections can achieve three key effects by implementing the
techniques outlined in this article. First, they protect operators from enemy detection. Second,
they create a resilient and layered observation posture. Finally, they provide continuous and
reliable reconnaissance, which reduces uncertainty for the commander.
Maximizing Observation and Survivability at the Observation Post
Terrain masking, electronic warfare, and enemy counterreconnaissance disrupt reconnaissance
formations and deny them the ability to observe and remain undetected. Although sUASs offer
reconnaissance elements unprecedented reach, commanders who employ them poorly can expose
operators, create predictable patterns, and fail to meaningfully extend a section’s observation
beyond terrain limitations. Simply attaching drones to a scout section does not automatically
improve reconnaissance effectiveness; without deliberate integration, aerial systems risk
becoming isolated sensors that neither protect the ground element nor sustain continuous
observation.
At an observation post, the scout section positions its ground team forward inside the named area
of interest (NAI), oriented on the most critical terrain, such as a key building, intersection, or
chokepoint, from the best position that balances line of sight with available concealment. The air
operator team occupies a concealed position on the far side of a terrain feature, typically 500–
1,000 meters from the NAI and behind an intervisibility (IV) line to preserve survivability and
reliable connectivity. From this posture, the group 2 platform pushes forward of the ground team
to cover sectors the scouts cannot observe without risking compromise. Its primary function is to
screen the ground scouts by detecting movement or threat indicators before being exposed;
detailed collection is a secondary task that supports overall reconnaissance continuity.
A scout section that deliberately integrates groups and group 2 sUASs as a dispersed ground-air
system gains depth and resilience in reconnaissance. Forward scouts anchor observation on key
terrain while the aerial platform screens forward space, detecting movement and environmental
changes before they threaten the ground element. Dispersion of launch, control, recovery, and
retransmission functions combined with nonlinear flight routing preserves the survivability of the
operator team and denies the enemy’s ability to correlate aircraft activity with friendly positions.
When employed in this manner, sUASs expand the scout section’s sensing reach while
preserving reconnaissance fundamentals of continuous observation, freedom of maneuver, and
survivability.1 Figure 1 depicts the disposition of sUAS integrated scout section at an observation
post.

Ground Team Retransmission Capability
The ground reconnaissance team’s organic retransmission capability enables the scout section to
adapt when missions shift or new observation opportunities emerge. With lightweight group 1
sUASs and ground-based retransmission assets, the scouts can quickly extend or reorient the
section’s surveillance posture across adjacent NAIs without forcing the air operator team to
displace or pause collection. This capacity to reposition signal support at the point of need allows
the section to seize fleeting observation windows, maintain reconnaissance tempo, and preserve
the continuous ground-air complement that underwrites effective sensing.
Sustained Observation in the Area of Interest
Once the section occupies the area of interest (AOI), the ground scout team orients on the most
critical terrain—likely enemy avenues of approach, key terrain features, and decision points
linked to the commander’s priority intelligence requirements (PIRs). From their concealed
observation site, the scouts provide persistent, high-quality surveillance and maintain continuity
even when conditions degrade air operations. Their role anchors the reconnaissance effort and
ensures a constant baseline of observation regardless of weather, signature concerns, or platform
availability.

situational awareness, mitigates exposure risk, and supports continuous reconnaissance under

Launch and Recovery
Launch and recovery operations are the most vulnerable moments in sUAS employment, and
poorly structured procedures expose operators to detection, patterning, and enemy
countermeasures. Effective reconnaissance requires the aircraft to contribute reach and fidelity
without revealing the ground element controlling it. To achieve this, operators must treat launch
and recovery as deliberate, low-signature actions built around dispersion, nonlinear routing, and
disciplined site selection. When executed correctly, these methods preserve the survivability of
the drone team, sustain continuous observation for the scout section, and reduce uncertainty for
the commander.

Four-Site Employment Framework
The drone team employs four dispersed sites that separate launch actions, control functions, and
provide communications support. Dispersion complicates enemy geolocation, reduces friendly
signature, and sustains the reconnaissance effort under observation or threat.
Launch Site (Short-Duration Occupation)
The launch site is a small, concealed area used only long enough to assemble and launch the
platform. The launch team immediately displaces after takeoff to prevent enemy sensors from
correlating UAS activity with its position. The site remains sterile and unoccupied except during
the moment of launch, minimizing attribution risk.
Recovery Site (Short-Duration Occupation)
The team deliberately separates the recovery site from the launch site to break enemy pattern
analysis and prevent straight-line geolocation. The recovery team occupies this area only when
recovery is imminent, retrieves the aircraft quickly, and displaces. Like the launch site, the team
never uses it as a sustained control location.
Retransmission Site (Sustained Position: Recovery Team)
When not actively recovering the aircraft, the recovery team occupies the retransmission site.
This node extends communications between the scout section, scout platoon, and adjacent
elements, enabling stable signal flow for voice and data. Terrain masking and concealment are
essential to reducing electromagnetic and visual signature while maintaining reliable
connectivity. The retransmission site provides redundancy in the reconnaissance network and
enables the ground-air team to maintain tempo, even when dispersed across restrictive terrain.
Control Site (Sustained Position: Launch Team)
After launching the aircraft, the launch team moves to the control site, which is the primary
location for operating the drone. This site must offer concealment, reduced signature, and a
favorable antenna geometry that does not compromise the team’s position. Operating the drone
from the control site rather than the launch point prevents enemy sensors from correlating flight
paths with operator presence and enhances survivability.
Alternating Team Roles
The two teams rotate between launch, recovery, control, and retransmission functions. This cycle
distributes workload, prevents operator fatigue, and ensures continuous sUAS availability.
Alternation also allows the section to maintain operations if one team displaces, comes under
threat, or conducts internal sustainment tasks. By preventing a single point of failure, the system
keeps air capability available to support the scouts’ movement and observation. Figure 3 depicts

Deceptive Routing and Flight Geometry
The aircraft must never depart directly toward the AOI. Instead, upon launch, it should execute a
retrograde movement, then fly laterally for at least 1,000 meters before turning generally toward
the AOI. This offset flight path denies the enemy a straight back-azimuth that could reveal
friendly control or launch sites. The deviation also increases the time and complexity required
for enemy sensors to correlate aircraft movement with operator locations.
Nonlinear Approach to the Recovery Site
Recovery follows the same logic, in reverse. The aircraft departs the AOI on a lateral offset,
moves retrograde away from the AOI and operator element, and only then approaches the
recovery site. This geometry prevents enemy observers from using the aircraft’s return path to
triangulate the operator’s position or infer friendly patterns of movement.
Purpose and Effects
Nonlinear routing provides built-in deception, disrupts enemy targeting algorithms, and
complicates enemy sensor mixing efforts. By obscuring the aircraft’s origin and destination, the
operator team preserves survivability and prevents the enemy from associating UAS activity with
ground reconnaissance elements.

Signature Management and Survivability Considerations
This four-site construct relies on strict discipline in visual, thermal, and electromagnetic
exposure. Launch and recovery sites must remain unoccupied except during the moment of
action, preventing enemy sensors from identifying a persistent signature. Control and
retransmission sites must exploit terrain, vegetation, and masking to reduce detection likelihood
while enabling reliable signal management. Dispersion between all four sites creates uncertainty
in enemy targeting and delays attempts at geolocation or counter-UAS action.
Link to Reconnaissance Fundamentals
This method supports reconnaissance fundamentals by preserving freedom of maneuver,
maintaining continuous observation, and ensuring survivability through dispersion and
deception. The distributed four-site layout enables the drone to enhance, rather than replace, the
scout section’s forward observation. The system keeps the air asset available without fixing the
operator team in predictable positions, sustaining reconnaissance tempo and reducing uncertainty
across the AOI.
Conclusion
Reconnaissance success depends on the ability to observe the enemy without becoming
decisively engaged. Scout sections that integrate sUASs significantly improve their overall
efficiency. They use the aerial platform to cue ground observation while using dispersion and
deception to protect the entire system. Forward scouts maintain persistent surveillance on key
terrain while the aerial platform searches forward past masking terrain, creating a layered sensing
system that reinforces each elements’ strengths and mitigates their limitations.
The distributed four-site employment construct, combined with nonlinear flight routing and
disciplined signature management, preserves drone team survivability while sustaining aerial
coverage in support of the scouts. By separating launch, control, recovery, and retransmission
functions and preventing direct flight paths to and from the AOI, the section denies the enemy
clear attribution of drone activity to friendly positions. This structure transforms sUASs from a
supplemental tool into a resilient ground-air reconnaissance system that preserves observation,
complicates enemy targeting, and reduces uncertainty for the commander.
