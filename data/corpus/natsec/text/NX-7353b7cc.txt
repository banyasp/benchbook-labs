Introduction
Although technology does not create competence, disciplined practice over many iterations does.
For the small unmanned aircraft system (sUAS)-integrated air operator team, technical
proficiency in rapid preparation, launch, recovery, and field troubleshooting forms the
nonnegotiable foundation. These skills are critical, yet they represent only the first layer of
tactical competency. The defining measure of an sUAS-integrated scout section is its ability to
break contact under any form of compromise—be it lethal or nonlethal—while simultaneously
preserving reconnaissance continuity and its freedom of maneuver.1
This article provides a framework of best practices observed at the Joint Multinational Readiness
Center (JMRC) for achieving this level of tactical mastery. Although the examples are based on
scout sections, all sUAS teams can adopt these principles to improve their tactical employment
and survivability. The article details how to best integrate technical sUAS skills into rehearsed
battle drills that turn the platform into a decisive advantage during contact. This article will
explore how to retain freedom of maneuver by using the sUAS to cue threats and inform egress
routes, enabling the section to disengage cleanly rather than becoming decisively engaged.
Furthermore, this article establishes that an sUAS team can achieve continuous reconnaissance
not by holding ground, but through deliberate displacement, pairing every tactical action with
immediate movement to manage signature and maintain the element of surprise. To achieve this,
this article outlines specific, synchronized air-ground break-contact drills, including immediate
sUAS suppression techniques and the use of claymore mines to create the critical window of
opportunity needed for the ground element to move, survive, and continue the reconnaissance
fight. Ultimately, this article demonstrates how to transform the sUAS from a simple observation
platform into a fully integrated enabler of reconnaissance fundamentals—the ability to disrupt,
displace, and continue observing.
Retaining Freedom of Maneuver
SUAS teams, like scouts, must retain the ability to break contact decisively, even as drones
assume a larger share of reconnaissance tasks. Reconnaissance forces maintain freedom of
maneuver, avoid decisive engagement, and preserve the ability to continue observing the enemy
after displacement.2 A sUAS-integrated section must therefore organize and train, so the
platform reinforces that freedom rather than constraining it. Ground scouts maneuver forward to
develop the situation while the air operator team offsets in a supporting position that protects the
launch site and maintains coverage of the scouts’ movement corridor and likely enemy avenues
of approach. This dispersion preserves mutual support while reducing concentrated signature.
From altitude, the sUAS provides information the ground scouts cannot see—obstacles masked
by terrain, key terrain influencing egress, enemy maneuver in depth, and flanking elements
attempting to fix the section. That information is not solely for higher headquarters; it drives
immediate section-level decisions. The scouts use drone-derived awareness to determine when
decisive engagement is imminent, where to egress under pressure, whether to bypass or

Perform Continuous Reconnaissance Through Displacement
One of the fundamental principles of reconnaissance in FM 3-98 is to perform continuous
reconnaissance. Continuity does not mean remaining stationary. It means sustaining observation
and information collection despite contact, effects employment, or enemy reaction. An sUAS-
integrated scout section maintains reconnaissance continuity by deliberately displacing, not by
remaining in one place.
Any employment of effects—lethal or nonlethal—creates signature. Calling indirect fire on
dismounted elements, jamming an enemy drone, destroying a vehicle with a drone-delivered
munition, or emitting sustained radio frequency (RF) traffic all reveal the section’s presence.
Even successful actions degrade survivability if followed by inertia. The moment the section
achieves an effect, it must assume detection and immediately displace. This displacement is not a
retreat; it is a continuation of reconnaissance under a new geometry of advantage.

By deliberately moving through a named area of interest (NAI),3 occupying successive vantage
points, and reestablishing observation from depth or offset, the section clears the area while
denying the enemy the ability to fix and decisively engage. Each displacement allows the scouts
to search from a new angle, confirm or deny priority intelligence requirement (PIR)-related
indicators, and maintain standoff. Fires, drones, and nonlethal systems do not interrupt
reconnaissance; they trigger the maneuver that sustains it. A section achieves continuous
reconnaissance by moving purposefully through an area, not by holding ground.
Reconnaissance Break-Contact Battle Drill
The break-contact competency is not a reaction; it is a rehearsed sequence of synchronized
technical actions. Once compromise occurs, the section executes a disciplined displacement
under overwatch. The ground element initiates movement by bounds, while the air operator team
immediately adjusts flight profile to support maneuver. Rapid control inputs, profile changes,
and concise reporting are not technical conveniences; they are the enabling conditions for
survivability. If the air team cannot reposition the aircraft quickly, it must adjust the sensor
orientation, or maintain link integrity under stress, the break-contact drill collapses.
As displacement begins, the drone cues threats beyond the scouts’ line of sight, identifies pursuit
elements, and confirms enemy reactions. The air crew refines fires if required, monitors flanking
movement, and provides real-time updates that shape the section leader’s egress decisions.
Simultaneously, the ground scouts execute rapid shifts in overwatch responsibility, maintain
dispersion, and move toward pre-identified or dynamically selected rally points. This requires
disciplined crew coordination—immediate cueing between air and ground, crisp fire commands,
rapid role transitions, and clear authority on who controls the movement at each phase of the
bound.
Technical proficiencies—rapid recovery, battery swaps under time pressure, link
troubleshooting, and displacement of the launch site—are inseparable from the drill itself. If the
section cannot recover and move the aircraft quickly, it concentrates signature and forfeits
freedom of maneuver. The purpose of the break-contact battle drill is not to win a firefight; it is
to disengage cleanly, regain standoff, and rapidly resume observation from a new vantage point.
When executed correctly, synchronized air-ground effects allow the section to fight only long
enough to preserve reconnaissance continuity and protect the commander’s decision cycle.
Immediate Small Unmanned Aircraft System Suppression Drill
At the moment of compromise or detection while the aircraft is in flight, the air operator team
immediately transitions the platform into a suppression or diversion profile to create a temporal
and spatial advantage for the ground element. Traditionally, this top-down pressure disrupts
enemy movement, complicates firing solutions, and buys critical seconds for scouts to initiate
displacement. As group 2 platforms increasingly integrate drop munitions or operate in
conjunction with one-way attack systems, the suppression effect can shift from purely
observational to lethal.

Technical proficiency becomes decisive at this moment. Rapid transition between surveillance
and suppression profiles, disciplined target confirmation, concise reporting, and the ability to
maintain aircraft control under stress determine whether the platform meaningfully supports
maneuver or becomes an additional liability. When properly integrated, aerial suppression is not
an escalation of the fight; it is a means of preserving freedom of maneuver and sustaining
reconnaissance continuity under pressure.
Claymore-Enabled Security and Initial Break
Pre-emplaced claymore mines and organic fires provide immediate shock during compromise
and layered security during long-duration observation. In extended operations, where the ground
scouts maneuver forward and the air operator team offsets in a supporting position, dispersion
creates survivability but also increases vulnerability. Claymore mines emplaced along likely
enemy avenues of approach provide early warning, channelization, and a deliberate trigger for
violence that protects the ground element and launch site.
During routine surveillance, these emplacements serve as a security measure that allows the
section to focus on sensing without collapsing inward for protection. If enemy elements probe

At the moment of compromise, claymore mines transition from passive security to active

continuity.
Training Recommendations for Integrated Break-Contact Competency
To build a tactically superior sUAS-integrated scout section, training must focus on the
synchronized execution of break-contact battle drills. First, the scout section must rehearse the
immediate transition of the sUAS from a reconnaissance to a suppression profile at the moment
of compromise, using its top-down perspective to disrupt the enemy and create the critical
seconds needed for the ground element to maneuver. Second, section leaders should train the
ground element to use this window of opportunity, created by either the drone or pre-emplaced
systems like claymore mines, to immediately initiate a disciplined bound away from the point of
contact. Finally, every drill must conclude with the section successfully displacing, managing its
signature, and rapidly re-establishing continuous reconnaissance from a new vantage point.
Success should be measured not by the destruction of an enemy force, but by the section’s
demonstrated ability to disengage cleanly, preserve its freedom of maneuver, and continue its
primary mission of observing and reporting.
Conclusion
Advanced technology, accumulated flight hours, or the number of munitions it can carry does not
define an sUAS-integrated team’s effectiveness. Instead, mastery of a single, critical capability—
the ability to break contact under any form of compromise while preserving reconnaissance
continuity and freedom of maneuver—measures its true value. Technical proficiency in
employing the sUAS is the foundation, but leaders and organizations must recognize it as merely
the starting point. A section achieves tactical mastery only when it seamlessly integrates these
technical skills into the core reconnaissance tasks of displacement, signature management, and
disciplined break-contact battle drills. These principles, though demonstrated through the scout
section, provide a blueprint for any team employing sUAS to master the break-contact fight.
In today’s operational environment, every action—from a radio transmission to employing
fires—creates a detectable signature. A unit that achieves a tactical effect but then remains
stationary has already forfeited its survivability. Therefore, leaders must drive a cultural shift so
the force understands continuous reconnaissance as a function of continuous and deliberate
maneuver, not of holding ground. Command teams must train their formations to achieve
continuity through displacement, using every contact as a trigger to move, re-orient, and resume
observation from a new position of advantage.
Ultimately, the sUAS is not a replacement for the fundamental principles of reconnaissance; it is
a powerful amplifier of them. When integrated with discipline, the platform provides
