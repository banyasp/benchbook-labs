     Abstract



  Special operations and the units that carry them out are captivating
to many. In recent years, several scientists have developed theories
to better understand this military–strategic phenomenon. Notably,
American scientist Tom Searle developed a theory where he describes
a void in military force as an area of military challenges within a
security issue that can only be resolved by special operations forces
(SOF) units. He offers a “negative definition” of special operations—
that is, he defines them by what conventional forces cannot do—
specifying that they comprise of the missions and tasks that lie outside
the capabilities, legal authority, or doctrinal scope of conventional
military units. The history of special operations shows that this void
in military force is dynamic, which is evident when considering
the deployment of SOF units in recent decades. There are three
distinct periods where it’s observable that SOF deployment differed
significantly from each other. Conceptually, the dynamics in the gap
are caused by ever-changing relationships between the capabilities
of conventional units and the military solutions requested in a
security issue.
  However, what are the factors or determinants shaping this void?
This monograph examines the void in military force by comparing
the deployment of SOF units at three significantly different stages
of its development. By comparing SOF deployment during World
War II (WWII), the Cold War, and the Global War on Terror (GWOT),
the following universal determinants of the void of military force can
be identified.
  (1) Changes in the balance of political power
  (2) The nature and level of national security risks at stake
  (3) The political and military risks involved in military response options
  (4) The level and intensity of the proliferation of threats
  (5) The presence of disruptive emerging technologies

  Understanding these universal determinants allows a better
understanding of the current period’s void in military force. With this
understanding, SOF deployment can be better prepared in the modern
era. Using the universal determinants to analyze today’s void can be
the subject of follow-up research.

     Introduction



THE EVOLUTION AND CONCEPTUALIZATION OF
SPECIAL OPERATIONS
  Special operations have played a crucial role in military history,
evolving alongside changes in warfare and strategy. These operations,
characterized by unconventional tactics, have historically been
employed to achieve strategic objectives beyond the capabilities of
conventional forces.

Historical Context of Special Operations
  The origins of special operations can be traced to ancient
civilizations, where elite units conducted reconnaissance, sabotage,
and targeted strikes. In ancient Greece, for instance, the Spartans
deployed the Krypteia1 for intelligence gathering and irregular warfare
(IW). During the medieval period, organizations such as the Knights
Templar engaged in covert operations to safeguard strategic interests,
including the protection of pilgrims during the Crusades.2
  The role of special operations expanded significantly during
WWII, with the establishment of elite units such as the British
Special Operations Executive (SOE),3 the Special Air Service (SAS),4
the U.S. Office of Strategic Services (OSS),5 and Nazi Germany’s
Brandenburgers.6 These forces executed behind-the-lines operations,
sabotage missions, and intelligence-gathering efforts that influenced
the course of the war.
  The Cold War era witnessed further institutionalization of special
operations, as intelligence agencies such as the CIA and the Komitet
Gosudarstvennoy Bezopasnosti (KGB) engaged in covert actions and
unconventional warfare (UW). Specialized military units, including
the U.S. Army Special Forces (SF), or Green Berets,7 and the British
Special Boat Service (SBS),8 were developed to counter emerging
global threats.

Conceptualizing Special Operations
  Scholars have sought to define the unique nature of special
operations. Searle, for example, describes them as “military or
paramilitary actions conducted by SOF that fall outside of conventional
warfare.”9 His concept of the void of military force refers to military
objectives that conventional forces cannot fulfill due to institutional
constraints. SOF are uniquely equipped to operate within this void,
leveraging their creativity, adaptability, and specialized training.10
  The evolution of SOF has been marked by distinct phases in their
selection, training, equipping, and deployment. If Searle’s theory holds,
the characteristics of the void of military force must have shifted
across these phases. Analyzing these transitions provides insight into
the enduring determinants of the void and informs the design and
deployment of future SOF.

RESEARCH OBJECTIVES AND QUESTIONS
  This study aims to enhance scholarly understanding of the void
of military force as a framework for assessing the utility of SOF.
The central research question is:

  Supporting questions include:
  • What constitutes the void of military force, and how does it relate
    to special operations?
  • How has the void of military force evolved over time?
  • Can universal determinants of the void be identified?

METHODOLOGICAL APPROACH
  This research adopts a conceptual and theoretical approach,
drawing upon existing literature on special operations. The study is
structured as outlined below:
  Chapter 1 explores the concept of special operations, defining key
terms and examining their strategic utility. The chapter also examines
the units that typically conduct special operations. Two theoretical
frameworks guide this analysis: special operation appearances and
the strategic utility of SOF as outlined by Colin Gray. The study on the
appearance of special operations utilizes a two-dimensional model
with the variables of directness and autonomy. The utility of special
operations as a strategic military instrument of power is discussed in
detail using the seminal work of Gray.
  Chapter 2 traces the historical development of special operations,
analyzing key events and their impact on SOF capabilities. The two-
dimensional model introduced in Chapter 1 is applied to categorize
historical operations and assess their strategic significance.
  Chapter 3 identifies universal determinants of the void of military
force by comparing its characteristics across different eras. This
analysis forms the basis for broader conclusions regarding the
evolution of special operations.
  Chapter 4 synthesizes findings and discusses implications for future
research on SOF doctrine and strategy.

INTRODUCTION
  This chapter begins by examining the concept of special operations.
It explores various definitions, characteristics, and the concept of the
void. Then, the chapter delves into the units that typically carry out
special operations and how appearances of special operations can be
identified and organized. Finally, the chapter focuses on how SOF can
be utilized to address security issues.

DEFINING SPECIAL OPERATIONS AND THE VOID OF
MILITARY FORCE
What are Special Operations?
  Finding a proper definition for special operations is essential
but complex. However, it is crucial to have good definitions and
delineations to do meaningful research on the phenomenon of special
operations. It is tricky because the exclusivity that should characterize
any good definition contradicts the spirit of special operations.
After all, special operations are “special” because they deviate from
the norm. To be valid, a definition should be short and capture the
defined subject’s essence but should not sacrifice user-friendliness for
arbitrary clarity.
  Attempts to define special operations are conducted by thinkers
in both the military and the sciences. NATO’s Allied Joint Doctrine
for Special Operations (AJP-3.5) describes special operations as

  Maurice Tugwell and David Charters have offered another useful
definition of special operations. They advise that special operations are
“small-scale, clandestine, covert or overt operations of an unorthodox
and frequently high-risk nature, undertaken to achieve significant
political or military objectives in support of foreign policy.”13 A notable
divergence between these two endeavors is that Tugwell and Charters
do not explicitly mention the forces that execute special operations.
However, they do establish a direct and relevant link between special
operations and the support of foreign policy. Gray also adopts this
definition, which he points out is both succinct and comprehensive.
He claims that six key features contribute to its superiority. This essay
adopts the definition of Tugwell and Charters; its key features are
addressed below.14

  Small-scale: First, the notion of small-scale must be addressed.
Special operations are typically conducted by small groups for the
following reasons:

                                      8
      CHAPTER 1: SPECIAL OPERATIONS, SOF, AND THEIR UTILITY



  (1) Small groups can move covertly and maintain secrecy more
effectively than large forces, which is crucial for operations requiring
surprise or infiltration behind enemy lines.
  (2) Small groups are more agile and flexible and can adapt quickly
to changing situations on the ground. They can maneuver through
complex environments and adjust their tactics as needed.
  (3) Small groups are less likely to be detected by enemy forces or
local populations, reducing the risk of engagement and increasing the
likelihood of mission success.
  (4) Special operations often focus on specific, high-value objectives
rather than large-scale engagements. Small groups are well-suited to
execute targeted missions with a clear goal.
  (5) Small groups leave a smaller footprint, making it easier to
operate discreetly and avoid escalating conflicts or provoking
opposition forces or local populations.
  The small size of special operations teams enables them to operate
with stealth, speed, and precision, maximizing their effectiveness in
achieving strategic objectives while minimizing risk.
  Clandestine, covert, or overt: By definition, a clandestine15 operation
attempts to conceal its very existence, a covert16 operation attempts to
conceal its true sponsorship, and an overt17 operation does not attempt
to conceal either the action or its perpetrators. This reference to
secrecy helps direct attention to the historical fact that, by their small-
scale character, SOF were most valuable serving in reconnaissance or
other intelligence-gathering missions rather than in combat roles.
  Unorthodox: In special operations, “unorthodox” refers to
approaches, tactics, or methods that deviate from conventional
military strategies. Unorthodox techniques are often innovative,
unconventional, and tailored to specific mission requirements. These
approaches may involve exploiting enemy vulnerabilities, utilizing
unconventional weapons or equipment, leveraging local resources or
knowledge, and employing non-traditional tactics to achieve strategic
objectives. SOF units are known for thinking creatively and adapting

                                     9
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



quickly to dynamic and challenging situations, using unorthodox
methods when standard approaches may not be effective or feasible.
  High-risk – Special operations, and their purposes, generally involve
high-risk circumstances. The operation of Clausewitz’s “friction”
factor in war can be uniquely deadly to the participants in special
operations.18 A definition of special operations provided by a study of
commando raids highlights why such operations are so high-risk in
nature: Special operations are “self-contained acts of war mounted by
self-sufficient forces operating within hostile territory.”19 Operations in
enemy territory are inherently high risk. With so much uncertainty and
so little room for error, it is not surprising that SOF plan their efforts
in excruciating detail, mastery of which can provide a sound basis for
emergency improvisation.
  Significant political or military objectives: Many thoughtful
commentators insist upon a strategic role rather than an operational
or tactical role for special operations. Even David Stirling, founder of
the famous special forces unit SAS, considered his initial memorandum
outlining a daring plan to destroy the Luftwaffe in North Africa on the
ground as essentially strategic. However, the strategic value of special
operations depends on the context of the war as a whole—whether
those operations are independent of distinctive campaign designs
or contribute to a campaign. The Tugwell and Charters definition of
special operations that provides the basis of this analysis avoids this
misconception by requiring that such operations be “undertaken to
achieve significant political or military objectives.”20
  Foreign policy: Lastly, defining special operations as being
conducted in support of foreign policy is helpful. It is important and
beneficial to remember that military activity must have a distinct
political purpose to be successful. Additionally, the reminder
that special operations should serve foreign policy helps elevate
analysis to include grand strategy–level thinking. SOF are a national
grand strategy asset; they are a tool of statecraft that can be
employed surgically in support of diplomacy and foreign assistance,

The “Outside the Box” Theory and the Void of Military Force
   Searle introduced an exciting study on the theory of special
operations in 2017 wherein he abandons the search for an idealized
view of special operations.21 Instead, he flips the discussion by giving
a specific definition that “negatively defines” special operations as a
broad set of military activities that conventional forces cannot execute.
The relationship between special and conventional operations is
meaningful in his theory, as it is dynamic and changes over time.
   In his influential essay, Searle presented a valuable model for
illustrating the relationship between conventional and special
operations. Searle’s model begins with a large circle or ellipse
representing the complete spectrum of military operations that are
within the purview and legal authority of the defense department
of any nation. It is unrealistic for the defense department to excel in
every conceivable type of operation. Instead of evenly distributing its
resources across all potential tasks, policymakers and military leaders
allocate resources based on national strategic objectives and future
expectations. They define conventional operations as the focal point
for the defense department, concentrating most of its resources on
conventional forces to carry out these operations. This is depicted in

                                            11
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



Figure 1 by the light gray box representing conventional operations
within the larger circle encompassing all potential military operations.
  Using Searle’s negative definition of special operations, everything
inside the circle of military responsibilities and authorities but outside
the conventional operations box is considered a special operation. This
space is also identified as the void of military force. For the remainder
of this monograph, it will be referred to as the void.
  This visualization of special operations helps to understand
several important aspects of the relationship between special and
conventional operations.
  The first thing to emphasize is that a narrower definition of
conventional operations allows conventional forces to become better
at those operations by dividing their training time among fewer
types of activities. This definition also makes the box smaller, causing
more activities to fall outside of it and become special operations.
As a result, efforts to focus conventional forces on a smaller range
of tasks should be coupled with an expanding range of special
operations capabilities.
  Another perspective on this model is to view special operations
capabilities as a safeguard against the inaccurate predictions of
policymakers and military leaders. As former U.S. Secretary of Defense
Robert Gates candidly acknowledged, “When it comes to predicting
the nature and location of our next military engagements, since
Vietnam, our record has been perfect. We have never once gotten it
right.” 22 Competent SOF serve as the insurance that shields us from
“lousy” predictions.
  Given that special operations often extend to the outer edges
of the defense department’s authorized activities, they are more
prone than conventional forces to intersect with the authorities and
activities of other government agencies such as the Department of
Foreign Affairs, the Department of Justice, and intelligence services.
Therefore, it is imperative for SOF to establish connections with these
government agencies.

The Relationship Between Special Operations and SOF
  After elaborating on the phenomenon of special operations, it is
relevant to explore the relationship between special operations and
SOF. Gray argued that special operations and SOF are not quite
synonymous. Special operations may be “what SOF do,” but they are
not only done by SOF. Special operations can also be executed by a
small group of soldiers from non-SOF units.24
  A different perspective on the relationship between special
operations and SOF, as described by Dr. Robert Spulak, argues that
special operations are missions to accomplish strategic objectives
when the use of conventional forces would create unacceptable risks
due to Clausewitzian friction. Overcoming these risks demands SOF
to eliminate the underlying sources of friction by leveraging their
distinctive qualities—skills, training, mindset, and physical and cognitive
capabilities—that result from the unique composition and attributes
of their personnel. Spulak lists three ultimate sources of friction: (1)

                                      13
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



constraints imposed by physical and cognitive limits, (2) informational
uncertainties and unforeseeable differences between perceived and
actual reality, and (3) the structural nonlinearity of combat processes.25
Additionally, Spulak lists three characteristics of SOF that enable
them to overcome these three sources of friction. First, SOF are “elite
warriors” who can exceed the physical and cognitive limits typical of
conventional forces. SOF are also flexible, enabling them to deal with
uncertainties and differences between perception and reality better
than conventional forces.26 Finally, SOF are creative which allows them
to exploit the unpredictability and “nonlinearity” of combat and create
more friction for the enemy. For Spulak, SOF are both different—due to
superior flexibility and creativity—and elite.
  Different scholars also make relevant distinctions between different
types of SOF units.27 During WWII, generally accepted as the time
that SOF units became institutionalized, a variety of so-called special
units were created and situated broadly in the spectrum of UW but
conducted different tasks. UW can be best imagined as a scale of
activities that spans the conditions of peace, the so-called gray zone,
and war. These activities include several different actions involving
different personnel from spies and paramilitary forces to SOF. In WWII,
the two British exemplars—the British SOE and the SAS—captured the
separation between paramilitary and SOF. While the British SOE was
designed to coordinate all action by way of subversion and sabotage
against the enemy overseas, the SAS was a purely military unit that
worked in uniform in enemy territory, usually after conventional forces
had a toehold in the theater. The difference between SOF and special
forces described by Alastair Finlan28 is also relevant. While SOF
equates to high-quality, usually battalion-size, infantry units of the
shock variety (such as the U.S. Army Rangers or the British parachute
regiment), he argues that special forces are truly unconventional,
non-confluent warfare units that are fundamentally different in size,
organization, culture, character, outlook, and mission orientation.

FILLING THE VOID OF MILITARY FORCE
  Two theoretical concepts are used to analyze the void of military
force: appearance of special operations and the strategic utility of SOF.

Appearance of Special Operations
  Special operations come in many different forms and shapes.
Dutch officer scholar Dr. Ben Gans considers four types of special
operations using two variables: duration (short vs. protracted) and
autonomy (autonomous vs. integrated).29 This exciting typology
misses the explicit and crucial distinction between direct and indirect
special operations. This distinction is not just a theoretical concept,
but a practical tool that enhances SOF’s strategic and operational
effectiveness. Ensuring the right approaches are used in the proper
contexts can significantly impact the outcome of operations.
  A prominent counterinsurgency (COIN) expert, David Kilcullen,
emphasizes the importance of understanding and employing direct
and indirect approaches in complex environments, particularly in COIN
operations.30 Other scholars, like John Arquilla and David Ronfeldt,
discuss the need for a balanced approach that integrates direct and
indirect methods to address asymmetric threats effectively.31 This
monograph uses Gans’ model, replacing the duration variable with
directness, and elaborates on the two variables that distinguish special
operations: directness and autonomy.
  Directness (direct vs. indirect): As we have seen, the global special
operations community often subdivides special operations into
two different types: direct and indirect.32 Direct special operations,
or direct action (DA), are executed with surgical precision—often
autonomously—and are typically short in duration. These operations
are often characterized as offensive, as they usually include a
precision strike capability. By conducting these operations, states
aim to generate strategic effects, which in turn contribute directly to
achieving their political objectives. Indirect special operations refer to
a category of unconventional military activities that involve influencing
or supporting proxy forces, partner nations, or local populations to
achieve strategic objectives indirectly. Instead of directly engaging

“Throughout the ages, decisive results in war have only been reached

upon, target, and upset an adversary’s equilibrium or balance to set
up and enable follow-on decisive blows to be landed. SOF’s power is
the ability to apply the precise effects of these collective capabilities
at carefully calibrated points of the adversary’s balance or equilibrium
described by Hart, whether that opponent is a state, non-state, or
irregular actor in the form of an insurgent challenging an incumbent
allied government.38
  Autonomy (autonomous vs. integrated): The terms “autonomous”
and “integrated” refer to different approaches in the coordination
and execution of special operations within a broader military context.
Autonomous special operations involve units or forces that operate
independently or semi-independently from conventional military
command structures. These units often have their own dedicated
command and control (C2) systems, logistics support, and decision-
making processes. They can operate with a high degree of autonomy,
allowing them to conduct missions with minimal reliance on external
support or coordination. Alternatively, integrated special operations
involve the seamless integration of SOF with conventional military
forces, intelligence agencies, and other government entities to achieve
strategic objectives. In integrated operations, SOF work collaboratively
with conventional forces and other partners, leveraging their unique
capabilities to enhance overall military effectiveness.39 Figure 2

that is offensive, kinetic, with a short duration, and often conducted
with surgical precision. On the scale of autonomy, a low score of one
means that a special operation is fully integrated into conventional

The Strategic Utility of SOF Within the Military Instrument
of Power
  It is necessary to understand what role special operations can play
in the military instrument of power. As seen in section 1.2, national
strategic objectives and future expectations have an impact on the
allocation of resources to conventional forces. Consequently, they
shape the void of military force. Gray, mentioned earlier, has studied
SOF’s strategic utility extensively and argued that special operations
can have strategic utility. In other words, special operations can
contribute to the course and the outcome of the entire conflict either
directly or indirectly through the operational level of the war.40
  Gray outlines the strategic utility of special operations in nine
categories or claims. Two of them are more important than the others
and are termed “master claims,” while the other seven are termed
“other claims.” The following section elaborates on the master claims—
economy of force and expansion of choice—while briefly mentioning
the other claims.
  Economy of force defines achieving significant results with
limited forces. Gray explains this further by stating that special
operations can (1) “act as a force multiplier and augment the strength
of regular forces,” (2) “accelerate the pace of success,” (3) attain
operational or strategic objectives by themselves, (4) prepare the
battlefield in regular operations, (5) wage war economically, having a
disproportionate effect on the battle and solving political or military
problems cheaply and quickly, (6) kill or capture individuals or
equipment that are difficult to reach with conventional forces, and (7)
seize the initiative, substituting for absent or incompetent conventional

   Expansion of choice expands the options available for decision

enemy realizes that a nation has a special operations capability, and in
times of war—both independently and in joint operations—enhances

   As identified by Gray, the other claims of the strategic utility of
SOF are: (1) innovation—SOF have “the ability to demonstrate new
tactical doctrines, equipment, and military methods;” (2) morale—

of SOF is relative. While they excel in peacetime and low-intensity
conflicts compared to other military capabilities, their absolute
strategic value may be outweighed in mid-intensity or high-intensity
conflicts. Additionally, the strategic value of SOF is influenced by
their performance and the importance of their assigned missions

CONCLUSIONS
   This chapter examines how SOF contribute to the military
instrument of power, offering a thorough treatment of the concept of
special operations, their various “appearances,” the units that execute
them, and their strategic utility. By establishing this foundation, we
created the analytical footing needed for the subsequent sections.
The central focus of the chapter was the articulation of the void—the
operational space in which special operations maneuver. Tracing the
evolution of the political–military environment reveals the factors that
shape the geometry of the ellipse and the square, and consequently
the size and character of the void. This examination yields deeper
insight into the nature of the void and informs conclusions about
the design and deployment of SOF units. In the next chapters, the
analysis extends, applying the three historically distinct phases of SOF
employment and the models introduced here, to identify overarching
trends that will guide future SOF development.

INTRODUCTION
  The history of modern SOF can be viewed in terms of clearly
defined evolutionary stages. Each stage, or age, of SOF begins with
an event or condition that catalyzes change. While there are several
examples of special operations elements and leaders throughout early
military history, the three ages described in this chapter refer to the
evolution of modern-day SOF from their WWII predecessors. This
chapter summarizes each of the three ages45 and analyzes them using
the theoretical concepts introduced in Chapter 1. By examining the
various appearances of special operations, the strategic utility of SOF,
and the specific (geo)political circumstances of that period, the void
can be described.

Conception and Emergence
  When Nazi Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, major
countries such as Britain, France, the U.S., and the Soviet Union were
hardly prepared for a major war on the European continent. Within a
short time frame, Hitler conquered large parts of Europe, leading to
significant strategic dilemmas for the Allies.
  After the withdrawal of the British expeditionary force from Dunkirk,
followed by the collapse of France, both strategic planners and
politicians began to consider how Britain might be able to continue to

                                    23
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



prosecute the war against the Axis powers. It was painfully clear that
Britain would be in no position to wage war on the European mainland
in the foreseeable future because its armies had abandoned their
heavy equipment in France and needed to be rebuilt virtually from
scratch. This effectively left Britain with economic warfare as the only
means through which the Axis powers could be attacked.
  Britain turned its attention to the possibility of waging economic
warfare through sabotage and subversion. Early evidence from
Poland and the protectorate suggested that resistance to occupation
was developing but needed help from abroad—particularly in the
provision of armaments—to expand. The minister of economic warfare,
Labor Member of Parliament Hugh Dalton, suggested the setup of
a new organization to coordinate resistance in Europe. Based on
the endorsements of some key political leaders, including British
Prime Minister Winston Churchill, and in the last acts of his political
life, Neville Chamberlain signed the British SOE-founding charter on
July 22, 1940: “A new organization shall be established forthwith to
coordinate all action, by the way of sabotage and subversion, against
the enemy overseas… This organization will be known as the [Special
Operations Executive] SOE.” 48
  On the other side of the Atlantic Ocean, in 1939, the U.S. was also
not prepared for large-scale conflicts. When Gen. George C. Marshall
took office as chief of staff of the U.S. Army, he assumed leadership
of a 174,000-man army that ranked 19th in world size. When William
Donovan, a civilian at the time, embarked on fact-finding trips to
England for U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt in 1940, Churchill
directed the leaders of the British SOE to brief the Americans on their
concepts for intelligence and special warfare operations in support
of European resistance groups. Upon his return to Washington D.C.,
Donovan proposed that the U.S. build a similar organization. As a
result, the Office of Coordinator of Information, later renamed to the
OSS, was established by Roosevelt with Donovan as its first director
and directly subordinate to the Joint Chiefs of Staff.49

  In addition to the setup of the British SOE, Churchill called for
a force to be assembled and equipped to inflict casualties on the
Germans, and bolster British morale. This led to the formation of
the British (and Interallied) commando units tasked to carry out
raids against German-occupied Europe. Initially drawn from within
the British Army from soldiers who volunteered for the Special
Service Brigade, the commandos’ ranks would eventually be filled
by members of all branches of the British Armed Forces and several
foreign volunteers from German-occupied countries. By the end of
the war, 25,000 men had passed through the commando course at
Achnacarry in the Scottish Highlands. This total includes not only
the British volunteers, but volunteers from Greece, France, Belgium,
the Netherlands, Canada, Norway, and Poland. Reaching a wartime
strength of over 30 units and four assault brigades, the commandos
served in all theaters of war from the Arctic Circle to Europe and
from the Mediterranean and Middle East to Southeast Asia. The U.S.
Army Rangers and U.S. Marine Corps Raiders50 were modelled after
the commandos. Their operations ranged from small groups of men
landing from the sea or by parachute to a brigade of assault troops
spearheading the Allied invasions of Europe and Asia.
  Similar to the military campaign of Nazi Germany on the continent
of Europe, its ally Japan conducted significant military campaigns and
geopolitical maneuvers across the Asia–Pacific region from 1941 to
1942. The invasion of the Republic of China, the annexation of French
Indochina, and subsequent incursions into Burma and British India
were conducted relatively unhindered.
  In their monograph The Fourth Age of SOF: The Use and Utility of
Special Operations Forces in a New Age, Will Irwin and Isaiah Wilson
write that the U.S. commander in the China–Burma–India theater, Gen.
Joseph Stilwell, needed ground forces to drive the Japanese out of the
area, but he knew that it would likely be at least a year before he could
expect any U.S. infantry to be shipped to this theater. When Donovan
offered the light, unconventional, and untried OSS Detachment 101 for
use in this theater, Stilwell—having nowhere else to turn—begrudgingly
agreed. Operational requirements soon resulted in the formation of

         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



other deep penetration units to precede or augment conventional
forces engaging the Japanese army such as “Merril’s Marauders,”51
and the Chindits.
  In 1941, the British High Command authorized the deployment of
several commando units for operations in the Middle East and North
Africa, including No. 7, No. 8, and No. 11. These units were later grouped
together as Layforce. As discussed by John Ferris,52 this formation
proved short-lived and was disbanded later that year due to strategic
and operational constraints, with many of its personnel redistributed
to other units. At the same time, new concepts of IW began to
emerge, emphasizing the use of small, highly mobile units capable of
operating deep behind enemy lines. Within this context, the SAS was
established in July 1941 under the leadership of David Stirling. Stirling
envisioned a force dedicated to conducting long-range raids against
Axis logistical and air installations in the Western Desert, particularly
those associated with German forces under Erwin Rommel. As noted
by historians such as Ben Macintyre,53 early SAS operations were often
carried out in cooperation with the Long Range Desert Group,
which provided essential navigation and transport capabilities in
the desert environment.
  As part of Operation OVERLORD, British and French SAS units were
deployed during the night from June 5 to June 6 in France.54 These
units conducted haphazard attacks to prevent German reinforcements
from reaching the initial Allied landing zones, and in tactical support of
advancing forces. In addition to the efforts of the SAS, the Allied High
Command deployed the Jedburgh teams to leverage the indigenous
pool of resistance fighters in France. The Jedburghs consisted of three-
person teams composed of a British or American officer; a second
officer who was French, Belgian, or Dutch; and a noncommissioned
officer radio operator. They were tasked to help organize, train, and
arm the rapidly growing resistance and to serve as a communications
link between the Allied High Command and the resistance.55

Allied SOF specialist forces. DA raids were marginalized; strategic

  The SOF involved in the first age can be distinguished into two

The Strategic Utility of SOF During the First Age of SOF
  As mentioned earlier, SOF during WWII were created out of
necessity. The military campaigns of the Nazis in Europe and the
Japanese in the Far East were so successful at the beginning of
the war that the Allies could only watch at first. According to Gray,
the tasking of SOF derives directly from judgment about national
interests.56 The national interests of the Allies at stake were existential,
more than enough to proceed with the deployment of all necessary
means to fight the enemy. The deployment of SOF was a logical
first step.
  The rapid emergence of SOF at the outset of WWII can be
understood through Gray’s first master claim—expansion of choice.
By fielding small, highly-trained units capable of striking aggressively
across the European mainland, the Allied political–military leadership
suddenly acquired a set of options that conventional forces, still
mobilizing for a full-scale counteroffensive, could not provide. These
SOF units gave the Allies the flexibility to act decisively, signal resolve,
and keep the initiative while the larger armies were still assembling.
In addition, the very presence of such forces amplified Allied morale;
as David Thomas observed in 1983: “For an army on the defensive,
the real value of commando operations was connected with their
psychological effect upon morale and the collection of intelligence.”57

The Void During the First Age of SOF
  After the invasion of Poland by the Nazis in 1939, the military
instrument of power became dominant. Searle’s model represents
this by a large ellipse. Initially, conventional military forces could

not confront Axis military campaigns in Europe, Africa, and the Far

the most significant was the requirement for direct special operations

becoming more integrated into conventional military campaigns.

Promotion and Expansion
  The second age of SOF took place during the Cold War. In this
period, the world was dominated by two superpowers: the U.S. and the
Soviet Union. Each possessed vast military arsenals, ideological fervor,
and global influence. This bipolar structure led to a tense standoff
characterized by proxy wars, nuclear brinkmanship, and ideological
competition across the globe. The balance of power was maintained
through a delicate equilibrium of deterrence, where neither side could
risk direct confrontation due to the threat of mutually assured
destruction. In the 1960s, Liddell Hart wrote his famous book Strategy:
The Indirect Approach where he stated that “the nuclear deterrent,
however, does not apply and cannot be applied to the deterrence of
subtler forms of aggression.” 59 He could not have been more right.

                                      30
           CHAPTER 2: THE THREE AGES OF SOF ANALYZED



  During the Cold War and under the shadow of the nuclear threat,
the major powers significantly increased their use of guerilla warfare.
It is in this time that Nikita Khrushchev 60 declared his support for
“wars of liberation” across the globe and newly elected U.S. President
John F. Kennedy declared a growing need for a vigorous special
warfare capacity. In 1961, Kennedy announced to Congress that he
had instructed Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara to act rapidly
in expanding the speed and the scope of the training of existing
forces for nonnuclear war, paramilitary operations, and limited or
unconventional wars. McNamara talked of a 150 percent increase in
U.S. anti-guerilla forces.61 Non-conventional forces at that time were
more of the ‘special’ type (SF) than the ‘elite’ type (SOF).62
  During the conflicts in Malaysia and Vietnam, conventional British
and U.S. armies encountered communist guerillas and had difficulty
dealing with them. One of the solutions found was the widespread use
of Green Berets and SAS teams.63 In Vietnam, SF units were deployed
to conduct long-range reconnaissance patrols (LRRPs) led by the CIA.
Project Delta, for example, was established to gather intelligence along
the Ho-Chi-Minh supply trail. Special forces teams consisted of six to
ten fighters, half American and half South Vietnamese. Using advanced
means and methods for that offensive, the commando fighters worked
together with U.S. Air Force aircraft and guided them in targeting
enemy supply squads moving along the route. During the Vietnam
conflict, U.S. Army SF grew from 1,500 men to 9,000. The U.S. Navy
established two new special warfare units to be known as SEAL units,
with first deployments on the Mekong River delta.
  In Europe, long-range reconnaissance units (LRRUs) of NATO
countries played a crucial role in implementing the “stay-behind”
concept during the Cold War. These specialized military units were
tasked with gathering intelligence deep behind enemy lines, often
operating covertly and independently for extended periods. In the
context of stay-behind operations, LRRUs served as the eyes and ears
of clandestine networks and provided critical information about enemy
movements, infrastructure, and vulnerabilities. LRRUs were ideally
suited for this role due to their advanced training in reconnaissance,

                                    31
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



stealth, and survival techniques. They penetrated hostile territory
undetected, established communication with stay-behind operatives,
and relayed vital intelligence back to NATO command centers.
Additionally, LRRUs facilitated the insertion of supplies, equipment,
and personnel into occupied territories, strengthening the resilience of
resistance movements and enhancing NATO’s ability to conduct UW in
the event of a Soviet invasion.64
  During the 1980s, U.S. SF played a crucial role in UW campaigns in
South and Central America. These campaigns often involved covert
operations aimed at supporting anti-communist forces, destabilizing
governments perceived as sympathetic to communism, and protecting
American strategic interests in the region. One notable example is
the deployment of U.S. SF to train and advise anti-communist militias
and paramilitary groups. In Nicaragua, U.S. SF personnel trained and
supported the Contras, a guerrilla insurgency fighting against the
leftist Sandinista government. Similarly, U.S. SF provided training,
equipment, and assistance to government forces combating Marxist
rebels in El Salvador during the Salvadoran Civil War. Over the course
of a decade, U.S. SF personnel professionalized the Salvadoran armed
forces and helped them to conclude a long insurgency.65
  By the late 1970s, terrorism increasingly became an area of interest
to SOF as a growing number of hijackings resulted in hostage
situations. SOF sought to learn from some successful foreign hostage
release operations, such as one conducted by Israeli commandos at
Entebbe airport in Uganda in 1974, a second carried out by the German
Grenzschutzgruppe 9 (GSG 9) commandos at Mogadishu Airport in
Somalia in 1977, a third carried out by the British SAS at the Iranian
embassy in London in 1980, and a fourth carried out by the Dutch
Bijzondere Bijstandseenheid (BBE) during a hostage situation at
De Punt in The Netherlands in 1977. These events led to a significant
change in the attitude of the public and the military to SOF.
  In 1985, following the Hezbollah suicide bombing in Beirut in 1983,
RAND Corporation investigated the contemporary tools available for
the U.S. to deal with international terrorism. It determined that SOF

The Forces that Executed Them
  The second age of SOF saw a dramatic increase in the size of the

were assigned new tasks. For example, the GSG 9 was established in

Italy in 1978.

The Strategic Utility of SOF During the Second Age of SOF
  With the eternal threat of destructive nuclear war, interests
and antagonisms were mainly fought indirectly. The Soviet Union
encouraged the spread of communism by supporting uprisings against
former colonizers or supporting Marxist-oriented regimes to increase
their influence in the world. With the U.S. in the lead, the West tried to
counter this movement by supporting pro-Western governments or
insurgents. This indirect approach prevented a direct clash between
the two superpowers. SOF, such as the Green Berets and the British
SAS, were deployed worldwide to fuel this indirect struggle for world
hegemony. During this period of SOF development, Gray’s master
claim—expansion of choice—played an essential role in the decision
to deploy SOF units. In supporting either friendly governments using
foreign internal defense or friendly insurgents using UW, discretion
and a long-term approach were often necessary. Unlike conventional

units, SOF units were well positioned for this. Consequently, the 1960s
saw strong growth in the deployment of SOF units, particularly in the
United States. The military relevance of SOF for indirect warfare is also
evident in what Gray calls “escalation control.” By deploying limited
military resources when there was an imminent escalation between
the U.S. and the Soviet Union, it was possible to scale back relatively
quickly and easily. During the 1970s, a new task for SOF surfaced.
A wave of terrorism engulfed the Western-oriented world, and it soon
became apparent that only SOF had an answer for this. SOFs high level
of training, availability on short notice, and use of innovative tactics
and techniques proved suitable for dealing with these terrorist threats;
it offered policymakers “economy of force.”

Regeneration and Validation
  The failed Iranian hostage rescue mission in April 1980 proved to
be the catalyst for change that initiated the third age of SOF. In May
1980, the U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff commissioned a Special Operations
Review Group to examine all aspects of the failed mission. The
commission’s report concluded that a command whose mission was
to integrate the special units from all branches of the military into SOF
and equip them with weapons and combat support systems suited to
the nature of their high-priority operations needed to be established.
This command became known as Joint Special Operations Command
(JSOC) and marked the commencement of a period of rebirth and
reorganization—a renewal of interest in and the professionalization of
America’s SOF.69
  During the First Gulf War with Operation DESERT SHIELD and
Operation DESERT STORM in late 1990 and early 1991, U.S. and British

                                    36
           CHAPTER 2: THE THREE AGES OF SOF ANALYZED



SOF were employed in a wide range of traditional wartime special
operations missions. SOF provided a critical component in successful
coalition forces C2. All the activities of SOF during the months of
preparation and during the operation itself demonstrated, above
all, its operational flexibility. The range of operations, from rescue
and recovery to direct offensive operations—like the famous Scud
hunts70—demonstrated how far SOF had come and how they could
be integrated into regular warfare. The number of missions assigned
to the forces also indicated increased confidence in SOF’s operational
capabilities among officers, and highlighted several ways to use SOF
during high-intensity conflict to assist conventional forces. SOF have, in
effect, become force multipliers.71
  After the collapse of the Soviet Bloc in 1989, the world entered a
period of uncertainty which led to the emergence of what scholars
termed “fourth-generation warfare” (4GW)72 or various other labels
like “limited war,” “war amongst the people,”73 and “low intensity
confrontation.”74 This new form of conflict was characterized by
asymmetry, urban warfare, and challenges in decision-making where
traditional state power could become a weakness. Yedidya Ya’ari and
Haim Asa argued that modern democratic values were exploited by
enemies to manipulate against state adversaries.75 Thomas Liebes and
Zohar Kampf highlighted the role of media in shaping perceptions
of violence.76 Western militaries, still geared towards conventional
warfare, faced difficulties in adapting to decentralized enemies. SOF,
with their flexibility and clandestine nature, became crucial assets in
addressing these challenges. In the decade that followed the First Gulf
War, SOF emerged as the primary tool of the West in non-war military
operations. Their role expanded over time, encompassing a diverse
range of missions: from covert raids for intelligence gathering, to
providing guidance for remote-controlled missiles, as seen in Operation
ENDURING FREEDOM (OEF). This operation, conducted in close
collaboration with the Afghan Northern Alliance and U.S. airpower,
led to the toppling of the Taliban regime in Afghanistan in 2001. Other
missions included sabotage, hostage rescue, CT, anti-kidnapping,

                                      37
          ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



and assassination operations, as well as psychological warfare and
peacekeeping missions.77
  Following the 9/11 attacks, the Bush Doctrine emphasized
preemptive actions against terrorism, elevating the role of SOF in
global conflicts. Thus, the active and aggressive Global War on Terror
(GWOT)—as opposed to the defensive and relatively passive approach
that had preceded the attack on the Twin Towers—became central to
the strategy of some Western countries. The increasing reliance on
SOF, marked by expanding budgets and missions, continued through
subsequent administrations and reflected a shift towards “light
footprint” strategies. Despite their effectiveness, the heavy toll on
SOF personnel and the debate over their extensive deployments have
sparked public and military scrutiny.

between warfare’s evolving nature and traditional military forces’
capabilities, particularly in addressing asymmetric threats and
unconventional challenges. This void emerged as conflicts shifted
towards IW, terrorism, and non-state actors, creating a mismatch
between the conventional capabilities of militaries and the demands of
modern conflict environments. SOF emerged as crucial actors in filling
this void. Conducting mostly direct and integrated special operations,
SOF offered flexibility, agility, and specialized skill sets tailored to
address the complexities of contemporary security threats. Their

  Comparing the three ages of SOF, significant strategic shifts in
response to global events can be observed. The types of special
operations have changed in appearance several times. In the first age
of SOF, special operations were characterized as direct–autonomous
at the beginning of WWII, then transitioned into direct–integrated
special operations as the war progressed. The second age of SOF saw
a dominance of indirect–integrated special operations throughout the
1950s and 1960s. The 1970s and 1980s were then marked by the rise of
international terrorism and witnessed a shift from indirect–integrated
to direct–autonomous operations. The third age of SOF, shaped by
the GWOT, saw a return to direct–integrated operations—this time

and more assertive geopolitical players like China and Iran, special

imbalance led to various SOF units. With targeted, direct–autonomous

defeated. Later during the war, special operations became more

  As detailed in Chapter 1, Gray outlines the strategic utility of special
operations in nine comprehensive categories or claims. Two of them—
economy of force and expansion of choice—stand out as “master
claims.” The other seven also play crucial roles and are termed “other
claims.” These claims, when combined, provide a comprehensive
understanding of the strategic utility of special operations—which is
the main focus of this analysis. In the paragraphs below, both claims

  Gray has done some work on the relationship between the utility

fight for; (3) major interests, which are difficult, but not impossible to

SOF and its appearance. The higher the national interest, the higher
the chance of deploying SOF for autonomous special operations.

themselves within enemy territory. One of the most noticeable,

and targeted operations to disrupt Taliban leadership and control

the morale of Taliban fighters. This coordinated approach enabled a
minimalist military presence, reducing both risks and expenses when
compared to a traditional invasion. The overwhelming display of power
and efficiency resulted in the rapid disintegration of Taliban resistance,
underscoring the efficacy of modern warfare tactics that combine
cutting-edge technology with highly skilled ground forces. Through
this, the fifth universal determinant of the void is identified as the
presence of disruptive emerging technologies.

      Chapter 4: Conclusions



  This monograph seeks to contribute to the ongoing scholarly
discourse surrounding SOF deployment. It draws upon the pioneering
work of Tom Searle, who has linked SOF deployment to what he calls
the void of military force—referring to tasks that conventional units
cannot execute. Searle argues that this void is dynamic and subject to
change, but he does not delve into how this void evolves or the factors
that drive these changes. Similarly, scholars such as Irwin and Wilson
have examined the shifting role of SOF since WWII, identifying three
distinct periods of varied SOF deployment worldwide. While their work
offers valuable insight into the historical development of SOF, it falls
short of explaining the catalysts behind these changes.
  This study aims to connect two different research areas in a new
and innovative way. By using Searle’s concept of the void and Gray’s
important work on the strategic significance of SOF, along with
Irwin and Wilson’s identified periods of SOF deployment, the goal
is to develop a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of
the universal factors influencing the void. This provides a solid basis
for further investigation. By carefully analyzing the visible forms
and strategic usefulness of SOF, five key factors that echoed across
the eras, each playing a significant role in shaping the void, have
been identified.
  (1) Changes in the balance of political power
  (2) The nature and level of national security risks at stake
  (3) The level of political and military risks involved in military
      response options
  (4) The level and intensity of the proliferation of threats
  (5) The presence of disruptive emerging technologies
  Having identified the universal determinants of the void, the
research question of this publication—To what extent do the

                                    55
         ECHOES IN THE VOID: FINDING UNIVERSAL PATTERNS



determinants of the void of military force in the last three eras
reveal a universal pattern, and if so, what is this pattern?—has been
answered, marking the next step in the scholarly understanding of
the void in the field of military studies. It turns out that if the different
periods of SOF development are analyzed consistently, universal
factors that always apply can be identified. With that understanding,
follow-up research can be initiated.
   The findings of this study have significant practical implications
for SOF units. Utilizing the universal determinants can drive further
research to understand the current void better. Analyzing changes in
geopolitical balance, for example, can derive conclusions about the
current primary mode of SOF deployment, whether direct or indirect.
Examining cutting-edge, disruptive technologies like AI and unmanned
systems can yield valuable insights into developing necessary skills
and supporting technologies for SOF personnel. A more thorough
understanding of the present void will empower SOF units worldwide
in their development and readiness. This understanding is crucial as
SOF units contemplate their role in the post-GWOT era. This period
will significantly shape their future operations and require immediate
strategic adjustments.

A. WELL-KNOWN COMMANDO RAIDS
  • Operation GUNNERSIDE A team of Norwegian commandos
    trained by the British SOE infiltrated and sabotaged the heavy
    water plant at Vemork in Norway on February 27, 1943. Heavy
    water was a key component of Germany’s nuclear research
    program and the operation prevented Germany from developing
    an atomic bomb. It was later called “the most successful act of
    sabotage in all of [WWII]” by Churchill.
  • Operation CHARIOT This daring British commando raid, also
    known as the St. Nazaire Raid, took place on March 28, 1942 and
    targeted the heavily defended dry dock at St. Nazaire in German-
    occupied France. The mission involved ramming the obsolete
    destroyer HMS Campbeltown, packed with explosives, into the
    dock gates, followed by a commando assault to destroy key
    installations. The successful destruction of the dry dock rendered
    it unusable for the German battleship Tirpitz, significantly
    hindering German naval capabilities in the Atlantic.
  • Operation GRANIT A German military special operation during
    WWII that aimed to capture the Belgian Fort Eben-Emael on
    May 10, 1940. Utilizing glider-borne paratroopers and specialized
    explosive charges, German forces swiftly neutralized the fort’s
    defenses in a surprise attack. The successful capture of Fort Eben-
    Emael facilitated the rapid German advance into Belgium, marking
    a significant early victory in the Battle of France.

EXECUTIVE OPERATIONS
 • Operation ANTHROPOID The assassination of SS-
   Obergruppenführer Reinhard Heydrich, one of the main architects
   of the Holocaust and the Nazi ruler of Czechoslovakia, who was
   also known as the Butcher of Prague. Two Czech operatives
   trained by the British SOE parachuted into Prague and ambushed
   Heydrich’s car on May 27, 1942. Heydrich died a week later from
   sepsis. The operation boosted the morale of the Czech resistance
   and provoked Hitler’s wrath, who ordered brutal reprisals against
   the Czech population.
 • Operation POSTMASTER The capture of three Axis ships from a
   neutral Spanish port in Fernando Po (now Bioko) in West Africa.
   A team of British and Polish operatives disguised as dock workers
   boarded the ships on January 14, 1942 and overpowered their
   crews. Then, they sailed the ships to Lagos in Nigeria under British
   control. The operation demonstrated Britain’s naval supremacy
   and disrupted Axis supply lines.
 • Operation JOSEPHINE B The destruction of a transformer station
   at Pessac in France. The station supplied electricity to a submarine
   base at Bordeaux where German U-boats were stationed. A team
   of French operatives trained by the British SOE paddled across a
   river on June7, 1941 and planted explosives on the station’s pylons.
   The explosion cut off power to the base and damaged several
   U-boats. The operation was one of the first successful acts of
   sabotage by the British SOE in France.
 • Operation JEDBURGH A secret mission during WWII in which
   three-man teams (which often included women) from different
   Allied countries parachuted into Nazi-occupied territories. Their
   goal was to help the local resistance fighters with weapons,
   training, and coordination. They also carried out sabotage and
   guerrilla attacks against the German forces. The operation was
   named after a town in Scotland where some of the teams trained.

• Operation MINCEMEAT In April 1943, the waterlogged corpse of
 a British Royal Marine was found floating off the coast of Spain.
 The dead Brit had a suspicious-looking attaché case chained
 to his wrist, and this soon caught the attention of the Germans,
 who colluded with pro-Nazi elements in the Spanish military to
 surreptitiously gain access to its contents. Inside, they found a
 shocking letter to a British officer in Tunisia outlining a secret
 Allied scheme to stage an invasion of Sardinia and Greece in
 the coming weeks. The dead man’s documents would have
 been a major intelligence coup for the Nazis, if not for one small
 issue: they were all fake. As part of a plan dubbed Operation
 MINCEMEAT, British spymasters had dressed the body of a
 deceased tramp in the guise of a fictitious Allied courier named
 William Martin. After the corpse’s briefcase was stuffed with
 phony military plans, a Royal Navy submarine secretly deposited
 the body off Spain in the hope that it might hoodwink the
 Nazis. The result was the perfect con: Not only did the Germans
 intercept what they believed to be crucial information about
 where the Allies would attack the Mediterranean, they were
 convinced they had done so without tipping off the British. Duped
 by Operation MINCEMEAT’s bogus intelligence, Hitler diverted
 tank divisions and other personnel to Greece, only to be caught
 off guard in July 1943, when the Allies instead invaded Sicily and
 Italy with some 160,000 troops.
• Operation EICHE In 1943, Operation EICHE, also known as the
 “Gran Sasso Raid,” saw a team of German commandos pull off the
 daring rescue of none other than Benito Mussolini. The mission
 came in the wake of the Allied invasion of Italy, which had resulted
 in the fascist dictator being expelled from office and arrested by
 the king of Italy. Concerned that Mussolini was crucial to keeping
 Italy in the war, Hitler ordered Otto Skorzeny, a Waffen-SS officer,
 to free “Il Duce” before he could be handed over to the Allies as
 part of a peace deal. After several false starts and dead ends,
 Skorzeny and his team tracked Mussolini to the Hotel Campo
 Imperatore, a ski resort situated on a remote mountaintop in the

   Apennine range. With no easy way to reach the resort from the
   ground, Skorzeny opted for a risky aerial attack. On September 12,
   1943, he and a team of commandos made a silent descent on the
   mountain in 12 gliders and stormed the hotel, easily overwhelming
   the bewildered Italian guards. After destroying the post’s radio
   equipment, the commandos located a much-relieved Mussolini,
   who supposedly exclaimed, “I knew my friend Adolf Hitler would
   not leave me in the lurch!” Desperate to make his getaway before
   reinforcements could arrive, Skorzeny convinced one of his
   support aircrafts to make a precarious landing on the rugged
   mountaintop and then personally escorted Mussolini to safety in
   Austria. The dictator went on to spend several months as a puppet
   leader in northern Italy before partisan forces finally captured and
   killed him in April 1945.

   Echoes in the Void: Finding Universal Patterns in the Utility
of SOF Across Epochs offers an examination of why special
operations forces (SOF) consistently emerge as a preferred
instrument of national power when conventional military
capabilities prove inadequate for the strategic environment.
Building upon the concept of the “void of military force,”
Lt. Col. Paul de Jager of the Royal Dutch Army analyzes
SOF development and employment across three different
eras to identify the enduring conditions that create demand
for special operations. The study demonstrates that SOF
effectiveness is not merely a function of unique capabilities,
but of their ability to operate within gaps created by political
constraints, strategic uncertainty, and the limitations of
conventional force.
   This monograph argues that five recurring determinants
